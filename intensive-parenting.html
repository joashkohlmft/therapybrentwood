Parenting & the Couple Bond March 2026 ⏱ 12 min read

Running on Empty: why your kids are draining your relationship

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·If you are endlessly patient with your kids all day and then short, flat, or snappish the moment your partner walks in, you are not a bad partner. You are a depleted one, and there is a real mechanism behind it.
  • ·Empathy and co-regulation are a finite, renewable resource. Spend the whole day calming a dysregulated child and there is often nothing left in the tank to turn toward each other at night.
  • ·Parenting can become a competing attachment, the one relationship you pour everything into. It is the only one that is socially praised, so the cost to the couple goes unspoken and un-grieved.
  • ·The short fuse over bedtime and chores is usually a shadow cycle: the fight is not really about the logistics. It is the emptiness of the couple’s own bond showing up as reactivity.
  • ·Protecting the relationship is not stolen from your kids. A secure, connected couple is the blueprint of safe love your child absorbs, so tending the bond is one of the most parental things you can do.

The 9pm version of you

Here is a scene I see all the time. All day you are the parent you always wanted to be. Patient with the tantrum. Curious about the feelings. You narrate, you validate, you get down on the floor. And then your partner walks in at the end of the day, asks a perfectly ordinary question, and something in you goes flat, or clipped, or quietly furious. The most generous, attuned version of you was spent hours ago, on someone who is four. What is left for the person you actually chose is the scraped bottom of the pot.

If that is you, I want to say two things right away. First, this is not a character flaw, and it does not mean you love your partner less. Second, it is not mysterious. It is a predictable systems problem, and it has a mechanism I can name.

I also want to be careful here, because the backdrop to all of this is a genuinely good-hearted movement. Gentle parenting, intensive parenting, being more present and attuned than our own parents often were, these are good instincts, born of love and of wanting to do better. That is exactly why the cost to the couple is so easy to miss. When the thing draining you is virtuous, nobody thinks to grieve it.

Adult love runs on three systems, and one of them is finite

Let me give you the map I work from. In the research I lean on, adult love runs on three connected systems: attachment, caregiving, and sex. Attachment, the bond itself, the sense that this person is my safe place, is the one that holds up the other two. When it is secure, warmth and closeness have room to breathe. When it is starved, they quietly go with it. (This is the model in Johnson & Zuccarini, 2010.)

Now add one physiological fact that changes everything for parents. The capacity to soothe another nervous system, to co-regulate, is real, and it is finite. Every time you calm a dysregulated child, you are loaning out your own nervous system, and that loan has a cost. A parent spends the entire day being the calm in someone else’s storm. By nine at night the reservoir is simply empty. There is nothing left to co-regulate with the one adult in the house who also needs it.

This is the honest truth underneath “I’m just so reactive lately.” A depleted nervous system does not organize around warmth and joy. It organizes around threat. Un-refilled and running on empty, it defaults to the short fuse, because that is what an exhausted body does. You are not failing at patience. You have run out of the raw material patience is made of.

Parenting as a competing attachment

There is a concept in the research that names this without blaming anyone: a competing attachment. It describes what happens when one partner pours their reaching and their care into someone or something else, and the other partner starts to feel like a rival for it. Classically the examples are work, or the phone, or an affair. But children count too, and as that competing pull grows, the felt security between the partners quietly falls.

Here is the cruel twist with parenting. It is the one competing attachment that is not just tolerated but morally praised. You are allowed to say out loud that you resent your partner’s job, or their golf, or their phone. You are not allowed to say you resent the baby, and honestly, you do not even want to, because you love the baby fiercely. So the resentment has nowhere to go. It goes underground. And underground resentment does not disappear. It comes back out as crankiness, as distance, as a flat “we’re fine, just tired.” That is why this whole subject feels a little taboo, and why so few couples ever say it plainly.

The shadow cycle: why the fight is never about bedtime

This is the piece I most want you to have, because it is the clinical heart of it.

When a couple comes in fighting about logistics, whose turn it is for bedtime, who forgot the form, why the dishwasher is like that again, the fight almost never makes sense on its own terms. The heat is wildly out of proportion to the dishwasher. That is your clue that something else is running underneath.

What is running underneath is what I think of as a shadow cycle. The couple has two bonds going at once: the visible, day-to-day machinery of running a household and raising kids, and the mostly invisible one, their own bond and closeness, the one nobody has tended in months. When that hidden bond is depleted, its emptiness does not stay quiet. It leaks upward into the visible fights as reactivity, less patience, less vulnerability, a faster and sharper protest. The empty tank is the fuel on the fire.

So the reframe is this: you are running on empty in the fight about bedtime because you are depleted in the shadow cycle, the one about the two of you. The bedtime argument is the smoke. The starved couple bond is the fire. This is why couples who “communicate better” about the chore chart so often find it does not help. They are optimizing the smoke.

The fight about whose turn it is for bedtime is almost never about bedtime.

And the most common line I hear, the one that sounds like the whole explanation, is “we’re just too busy and too tired.” I believe you that you are. But notice what that sentence is really saying. The problem is usually not that the love died. It is that the two of you got deprioritized, all the way to the bottom of a very long list. The fix is not manufacturing more feeling. It is protection and time. I sometimes put it bluntly: if you took the money you would spend on therapy and instead protected one real hour a week for each other, some of you would not need the therapy.

The guilt, and why it is built on a false choice

Now the part that actually frees people, because the thing keeping most parents stuck here is guilt. The quiet fear that any energy spent on the couple is energy stolen from the kids.

Let me hand you a structural truth that dissolves a lot of that guilt. The bond between a parent and a child is reciprocal, but it is not mutual, and it was never meant to be. Your child is not responsible for meeting your needs, for regulating you, for being your emotional home. That is not a failure of the child. It is the correct design. Which means that when you pour all of your own attachment needs into your children, you are aiming them at the one relationship that is structurally unable to answer them. The longing comes back unmet, and you are left more depleted, and more confused about why. Your child cannot be your attachment figure, and was never meant to be. The couple bond is not competing with your kids for a luxury. It is the only place the adults’ needs can legitimately land.

And here is the reframe that turns the whole thing around: prioritizing the bond is not the opposite of parenting. It is parenting. In the family-therapy work I do, you cannot pour care into a child through a caregiver who is running on empty; you have to secure and soften the caregiver first. A couple who feels connected and co-regulated is not taking something from their children. They are showing them, in real time, what safe love looks like. Kids do not learn secure attachment from a lecture. They absorb it from the emotional weather between the two adults who are raising them.

A secure couple bond is not taken from your kids. It is the blueprint you hand them.

That is also, for many of us, the deeper thing underneath: the ache to do it differently than it was done to us. A lot of parents are pouring everything into their children precisely because they know what it felt like to grow up without it. The way to give your child the security you did not have is not to erase yourself into the parenting role. It is to let them grow up inside a bond that actually works, and to know that closeness and independence are not enemies. Secure connection is what lets a child eventually walk away and stand on their own.

So the guilt itself is worth a gentler look. In the way I think about emotions, guilt here is usually a secondary feeling, the alarm on top. Underneath it is something good and primary: the longing to be a wonderful parent, and the longing to still be met as a partner. Those two are not in a fight. The “it has to be the kids or us” story is one the exhausted, threat-organized nervous system tells at 9pm. It is not true.

When where you live turns up the pressure

Some settings pour gasoline on all of this. In high-achievement, high-comparison places, and I see it constantly with couples in Franklin and across Williamson County, intensive parenting stops feeling optional. The travel team that eats every weekend. The private-school arms race. The sense that the “good parents” here are the ones whose calendars are completely consumed by their kids’ enrichment. Add the money pressure that comes with all of it, and two people who love each other can end up running a small logistics company together, exhausted, and quietly certain that easing up would mean failing their children. It would not. But the culture makes the couple bond the easiest thing to sacrifice, because sacrificing it looks, from the outside, like devotion. I have written more about that particular squeeze on my couples therapy in Franklin page.

The way back

The good news is that this is one of the most workable kinds of distance there is, because nothing is broken between you. You are not adversaries. You are two depleted people who love each other and their kids and have not been left with a drop for one another.

The work I do, which is Emotionally Focused Therapy, does not start with a chore chart. It starts by naming the cycle and putting it, not each other, in the crosshairs. The enemy was never your tired partner. It is the depleted pursue-and-withdraw pattern the exhaustion drives. From there we reach past the reactivity for the softer, truer thing underneath it, which is almost always some version of I miss us, I feel alone in this, I am scared we have become roommates. When couples can say that instead of relitigating the dishwasher, the temperature in the whole house changes. (If “roommates” landed, I have written about that specific drift in roommate syndrome, and about how closeness quietly fades along with it in sexless marriage.)

Then the sequence matters. You rebuild the sense of safe connection first, and let warmth and closeness return with it, not the other way around. You cannot force a depleted bond back to life. But you can refill it. That means protecting time and small daily bids of reaching the way you protect anything that matters, deliberately, on purpose, before there is any left over, because there is never any left over. Think of it as re-filling the tank your children are drinking from. A connected couple has more to give their kids, not less.

None of this asks you to love your children one degree less. It asks you to stop treating the bond that holds your whole family up as the one thing that can always wait. This is the heart of what I do in couples therapy, and it is some of the most hopeful work there is, because the love is not gone. It is just buried under a very long, very devoted, very tired day.

You do not have to love your kids any less to protect the bond that holds them. In fact, protecting it is one of the most parental things you can do.

Common questions

Why do I have endless patience for my kids but none for my partner?

Because the capacity to soothe another nervous system, to co-regulate, is real but finite. When you spend the whole day being the calm in a child's storm, you are loaning out your own nervous system, and that loan has a cost. By night the reservoir is empty, so there is nothing left to turn toward the one adult in the house who also needs it. It is not a character flaw or a sign you love your partner less. It is a depleted nervous system defaulting to the short fuse.

Is it bad to put your kids before your relationship?

The either-or is a false choice. The couple bond is not competing with the kids for a luxury; it is the foundation the whole family stands on. A child's deepest security does not come from another activity, it comes from feeling that the two people holding up their world are still connected. Protecting the relationship is not stolen from your children. A secure, co-regulated couple is the working model of safe love a child absorbs, so tending the bond is one of the most parental things you can do.

Why do we only fight about logistics and chores now?

Because the logistics fight is usually a shadow cycle. The heat is out of all proportion to the dishwasher, which is the clue that something else is running underneath: the couple's own bond, the one nobody has tended in months. When that hidden bond is depleted, its emptiness leaks upward into the visible fights as reactivity. The fight about whose turn it is for bedtime is almost never about bedtime.

We're not fighting, we've just become roommates since having kids. What happened?

You most likely became excellent co-managers and near-strangers as partners: all handoffs and logistics, no reaching. It is not that you stopped caring. You are two depleted people pouring everything into the children and getting deprioritized, all the way to the bottom of the list, in the process. The bond is not gone; it is starved. It refills when the two of you become a protected priority again rather than the last item on the list.

Is it selfish to want time away from my kids and alone with my partner?

No. Child-free couple time is not selfish behavior, it is protective behavior that stabilizes the whole family. Think of it as refilling the tank your children drink from. A connected, co-regulated couple has more to give their kids, not less. The guilt that says otherwise is usually a secondary feeling sitting on top of two good longings: to be a wonderful parent, and to still be met as a partner. Those two are not enemies.

Can having a baby ruin your relationship?

It does not ruin it, but it reliably strains it. Research on couples across the transition to parenthood finds that about two-thirds see relationship satisfaction decline in the first three years after a baby. That is common, not doom, and it is highly workable. What predicts which couples come through it is not luck or never struggling, it is whether they keep tending the bond between them rather than letting it become the one thing that always waits.

How do we reconnect when we're both exhausted and have no time?

Not by trying to force it. The usual line, we are just too busy and too tired, is really saying the two of you got deprioritized. The fix is priority and protected time, not more feeling. Protect one real, regular hour for each other the way you protect anything that matters, before there is any left over, because there is never any left over. Rebuild the sense of safe connection first with small daily bids of reaching, and let warmth and closeness return rather than demanding they come first.

Does a strong parents' relationship actually help the kids?

Yes, profoundly. The parent-child bond is reciprocal but not mutual: a child is not, and should not be, responsible for meeting or regulating an adult's attachment needs. Children learn secure attachment not from a lecture but by absorbing the emotional weather between the two adults raising them. A secure couple bond is not taken from your kids. It is the blueprint of safe love you hand them, and the thing that eventually lets them feel safe enough to stand on their own.
