These are the working definitions I use in my own practice. Emotionally Focused Therapy has a precise vocabulary, and terms like negative cycle, bonding event, and softening have specific clinical meanings that shape how I understand and work with the couples and individuals I see. I wrote this glossary to define those terms plainly, both for people in or considering EFT and as a reference for how I use these concepts across the rest of this site.

Core EFT Concepts

Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)

An evidence-based approach to couples, individual, and family therapy developed by Dr. Sue Johnson and Dr. Les Greenberg in the 1980s, grounded in attachment theory. EFT is designated by the APA as the gold standard in couples therapy research, the most extensively studied couples therapy model in the world, with approximately 75% of couples achieving full recovery and 86–90% reporting significant improvement. Its outcomes have been replicated across 40 years and multiple countries. Read our full EFT guide →

RelatedThe Three Stages of EFT, Attachment Theory

Negative Cycle

The recurring pattern of interaction between partners in which each person’s protective response, pursuit, withdrawal, attack, shutdown, triggers the other’s fear, creating an escalating loop that leaves both feeling worse and more disconnected. In EFT, the negative cycle, not the partner, is identified as the shared problem. Both partners are seen as caught in the cycle together, each responding to perceived threat to the bond.

RelatedPursuer-Withdrawer Pattern, Demon Dialogues, De-Escalation

Pursuer-Withdrawer Pattern

A specific and common negative cycle in which one partner moves toward the other seeking connection, reassurance, or resolution (the pursuer), while the other moves away from perceived conflict or overwhelm (the withdrawer). The pursuer’s approach triggers more withdrawal; the withdrawal triggers more pursuit. Over time the cycle becomes the dominant relational dynamic. Full article →

RelatedNegative Cycle, Withdrawer Re-engagement, Blamer Softening

De-Escalation

The first stage of EFT couples therapy, in which the therapist helps partners identify and step outside their negative cycle, reducing reactivity and creating enough safety to go deeper. De-escalation is not resolution. It is the foundation that makes genuine emotional access possible. Without it, the deeper work cannot happen.

RelatedNegative Cycle, The Three Stages of EFT

Softening

A pivotal change event in EFT couples therapy in which a typically blaming, critical, or defended partner drops their protective stance and reaches toward the other from genuine vulnerability. Softening is often the turning point in the therapy process, the moment at which the emotional terrain between partners visibly shifts. It creates the conditions for a bonding event.

RelatedBlamer Softening, Bonding Event, Primary and Secondary Emotions

Primary and Secondary Emotions

EFT distinguishes between primary emotions, the first and deeper, often vulnerable response to a moment (fear, hurt, loneliness, shame, longing), and secondary emotions, the reactive feelings that cover them (anger, frustration, defensiveness, numbness). Couples usually fight from their secondary emotions while the primary ones go unspoken. Much of EFT is the slow work of reaching the primary emotion underneath the reactive one, because that is what actually moves a partner.

RelatedSoftening, Raw Spots

A.R.E.: Accessible, Responsive, Engaged

The three qualities that define a secure bond in EFT, described by Dr. Sue Johnson as the question partners are always quietly asking each other: “Are you there for me?” Accessible means I can reach you. Responsive means you answer my emotional signals. Engaged means you are actively, attentively present. When the answer to A.R.E. is yes, the bond feels safe; when it is uncertain, the attachment system sounds the alarm.

RelatedEmotional Accessibility, Emotional Responsiveness

The Three Stages of EFT

EFT couples therapy unfolds across three stages and nine steps. Stage One, de-escalation, calms the negative cycle and names it as the shared enemy. Stage Two, restructuring the bond, is where the deeper change happens, through withdrawer re-engagement and blamer softening. Stage Three, consolidation, integrates the new patterns and helps the couple carry them into ordinary life. Progress is rarely perfectly linear, but the map is consistent.

RelatedDe-Escalation, Bonding Event

Enactment

A core EFT intervention in which the therapist slows a moment down and directs partners to turn and speak to each other, rather than to the therapist, from their deeper emotion. Enactments turn insight into experience: instead of talking about the relationship, partners have a new, corrective interaction inside the session itself. Many of EFT’s turning points, including softening, happen inside an enactment.

RelatedBonding Event, Softening

Demon Dialogues

Dr. Sue Johnson’s term, from Hold Me Tight, for the three self-reinforcing negative patterns couples fall into: Find the Bad Guy (mutual blame), the Protest Polka (one partner pursues while the other withdraws, the most common), and Freeze and Flee (both partners give up and go numb). Naming which demon dialogue you are caught in is often the first step to stepping out of it.

RelatedNegative Cycle, Pursuer-Withdrawer Pattern

Attachment Concepts

Attachment Theory

The psychological framework developed by John Bowlby describing how humans form deep emotional bonds with significant others, and how the security or insecurity of those bonds shapes behavior, emotion, and wellbeing across the lifespan. Attachment is not a childhood phenomenon, adults depend on their intimate partners as primary attachment figures, which is why relationship loss or chronic disconnection feels so existentially threatening. Attachment theory is the scientific foundation of EFT.

RelatedSecure Base, Internal Working Model

Secure Base

A concept from attachment theory describing a relationship that provides enough safety and stability for a person to explore the world, take risks, and return to comfort and care when threatened or distressed. A secure base is not comfort alone, it is confidence that the attachment figure will be there. In EFT, building a secure base between partners is the central therapeutic goal.

RelatedAttachment Theory, Co-Regulation and Self-Regulation

Attachment Injury

A specific moment of abandonment or betrayal at a critical time of vulnerability, such as a partner’s emotional absence during illness, miscarriage, loss, or crisis, that creates a persistent wound in the emotional bond. Attachment injuries are different from ordinary conflict. They do not resolve through apology or time alone; they require specific therapeutic attention to the wound itself. The Attachment Injury Resolution Model in EFT addresses these directly.

RelatedRaw Spots, Attachment Panic

Attachment Panic

An intense state of alarm in the attachment system triggered when a person perceives that their emotional bond with a significant other is in danger. Attachment panic manifests as urgent pursuit, emotional flooding, frantic texting, or escalating behavior to re-establish contact, responses that are often misread as controlling, irrational, or “too much” rather than recognized as fear. Understanding attachment panic reframes the pursuer’s behavior from pathology to a predictable biological response to perceived abandonment. See also: Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome.

RelatedAttachment Protest, Raw Spots

Attachment Protest

The behavioral and emotional response to perceived disconnection from an attachment figure, typically expressed as anger, criticism, demanding, or escalating pursuit. Attachment protest is a cry for connection wearing the costume of conflict. In EFT, therapists work to access the fear and longing beneath the protest rather than responding to the protest at face value.

RelatedAttachment Panic, Pursuer-Withdrawer Pattern

Raw Spots

Attachment-based sensitivities, shaped by past hurts, losses, or chronically unmet relational needs, that cause certain interactions to feel disproportionately painful. When a raw spot is activated, the emotional response can seem out of proportion to the immediate situation because it is also activating something much older. Partners in EFT learn to recognize and name each other’s raw spots, which changes how they respond to each other in those moments.

RelatedAttachment Injury, Primary and Secondary Emotions

Internal Working Model

The unconscious relational map, formed in early attachment relationships, that shapes how a person expects to be treated, how worthy of love they believe themselves to be, and how they respond in close relationships throughout life. A person with an insecure internal working model may expect rejection, abandonment, or engulfment even when these are not present in the current relationship. EFT works to update these models through new emotional experience, not through insight or cognitive reframing alone.

RelatedAttachment Theory, Attachment Styles

Attachment Styles

Patterns of relating shaped by early attachment experience and carried into adult relationships: secure (comfortable with closeness and able to depend on others), anxious or preoccupied (fearing abandonment and seeking reassurance), avoidant or dismissive (prizing independence and pulling back from closeness), and disorganized or fearful-avoidant (longing for closeness and fearing it at once, often after relational trauma). In EFT these are not fixed labels but strategies the attachment system learned, and new experiences of safety can move a person toward earned security. RelatedInternal Working Model, Attachment Theory

Co-Regulation and Self-Regulation

Two ways the nervous system finds calm. Co-regulation is settling through connection with another person, a soothing tone, steady presence, touch, eye contact; it is how humans are built to regulate from infancy onward. Self-regulation is the capacity to steady yourself, through breath, grounding, or a brief pause, enough to stay present rather than reactive. Healthy relationships rely on both, and EFT helps partners become sources of co-regulation for each other rather than triggers.

RelatedWindow of Tolerance, Secure Base

Change Events in EFT

Corrective Emotional Experience

A moment in therapy, or in a relationship, when a person receives a response genuinely different from the one that wounded them: being seen, comforted, or held when they expected rejection or abandonment. Corrective emotional experiences gradually update the internal working model and create new relational templates. EFT is specifically structured to create these moments between partners and between client and therapist.

RelatedBonding Event, Internal Working Model

Bonding Event

A moment of genuine emotional contact between partners, typically involving one partner reaching from vulnerability and the other responding with presence and care, that strengthens the attachment bond and creates a new shared relational experience. Bonding events are not manufactured; they emerge when enough safety and vulnerability are present. They are the relational units through which secure bonds are built in EFT.

RelatedSoftening, Hold Me Tight Conversation, Corrective Emotional Experience

Emotional Accessibility

One of the two core components of a secure bond in EFT, describing a partner’s ability to be emotionally present, reachable, and engaged rather than defended, distracted, or unavailable when the other reaches for connection. Emotional accessibility is the foundation for any genuine response. A partner who is accessible can be influenced; a partner who is defended cannot be reached.

RelatedA.R.E., Emotional Responsiveness

Emotional Responsiveness

The second core component of a secure bond in EFT, describing the ability to respond to a partner’s emotional signals with genuine care, attunement, and presence, rather than with dismissal, logic, minimizing, or counter-demands. Together, emotional accessibility and emotional responsiveness define what it means for a partner to be “there” in the attachment sense of the word.

RelatedA.R.E., Emotional Accessibility

Hold Me Tight Conversation

In EFT and the Hold Me Tight retreat program developed by Dr. Sue Johnson, a structured conversation in which partners practice reaching for each other from genuine vulnerability, learning to ask for what they need and to respond to each other’s deeper longings with presence and care. The Hold Me Tight conversation is the fourth of seven EFT-based conversations in the retreat program, and is often described as its turning point. Hold Me Tight retreats →

RelatedBonding Event, A.R.E.

Withdrawer Re-engagement

The first of the two key change events in Stage Two of EFT, in which the more withdrawn or shut-down partner begins to move back toward the relationship, becoming emotionally present and able to name their own needs and fears rather than retreating. A withdrawer’s return re-opens the conversation and makes the second change event, blamer softening, possible.

RelatedBlamer Softening, Pursuer-Withdrawer Pattern

Blamer Softening

The pivotal change event in EFT, in which a more critical or pursuing partner, now feeling safer, reaches for the other from vulnerability rather than protest, asking directly for comfort and connection. Softening reorganizes the bond and is often the moment couples describe as the turning point of their therapy. It depends on the withdrawer having become accessible enough to respond.

RelatedSoftening, Withdrawer Re-engagement

EFT Applications

EFIT: Emotionally Focused Individual Therapy

The individual therapy application of EFT, developed by Dr. Sue Johnson and Dr. Leanne Campbell. EFIT applies attachment theory to personal healing, addressing trauma, depression, anxiety, grief, shame, and the relational wounds that shape a person’s inner world and close relationships. Where couples EFT targets the bond between partners, EFIT targets the bond between self and self, and the inner working models that drive how a person relates. Individual therapy →

RelatedEmotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), EFFT

Window of Tolerance

The zone of emotional arousal in which a person can remain present, connected, and engaged with difficult material without becoming overwhelmed (hyperarousal, flooding, panic, rage) or shutting down (hypoarousal, dissociation, numbness, collapse). Trauma-informed therapy, including brainspotting and somatic approaches used alongside EFIT, works within and gently expands this window, ensuring safety throughout the healing process. Trauma & Brainspotting →

RelatedCo-Regulation and Self-Regulation, Flooding

EFFT: Emotionally Focused Family Therapy

The family application of EFT, which brings attachment science to the bonds between parents and children and among family members. EFFT works to restore parents as a secure base and safe haven, repairing the disconnection and reactive cycles that build up in families under stress. Joash is ICEEFT-certified in EFFT, alongside EFCT for couples and EFIT for individuals. Family and family-of-origin work →

RelatedEmotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), EFIT

Related Concepts (Gottman and the Wider Field)

The Four Horsemen

Dr. John Gottman’s term for four communication patterns that reliably predict relationship breakdown: criticism (attacking character rather than raising a specific complaint), contempt (mockery, eye-rolling, or superiority, the single strongest predictor of divorce), defensiveness (warding off responsibility, often with a counter-complaint), and stonewalling (shutting down and withdrawing from the conversation). EFT and Gottman describe the same painful moments from different angles; EFT reads each “horseman” as a move in the attachment cycle with a softer emotion underneath. EFT vs. Gottman →

RelatedFlooding, Stonewalling

Flooding

A state of physiological overwhelm during conflict, racing heart, tight chest, a mind that goes blank or hot, in which the body’s threat response takes over and productive conversation becomes almost impossible. Gottman calls this diffuse physiological arousal. When either partner is flooded, the most useful move is usually a short, agreed-upon break to let the nervous system settle before returning. See also window of tolerance.

RelatedWindow of Tolerance, The Four Horsemen, Stonewalling

Stonewalling

Withdrawing from an interaction by going silent, shutting down, or physically leaving, often an attempt to escape flooding rather than an act of indifference. To the other partner it can feel like abandonment, which only escalates the cycle. In EFT, stonewalling is understood as a withdrawer’s protective strategy, and the work is to help that partner stay present and reachable instead.

RelatedThe Four Horsemen, Flooding, Withdrawer Re-engagement

Original Frameworks

Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome (ARS) Original

An original clinical framework coined in 2024 by Joash Koh LMFT describing the injury that forms when one partner consistently carries 100% of a relationship’s emotional repair work, initiating, apologizing, and sustaining the relationship, while the other partner chronically disengages from that effort. ARS is distinct from the pursuer-withdrawer pattern in that the asymmetry has calcified into a stable, structural mismatch: one person doing all the work of keeping the relationship alive, the other having learned that waiting is safe. The result is a specific and compounding attachment injury in the carrying partner. Full framework →

RelatedPursuer-Withdrawer Pattern, Withdrawer Re-engagement

Dual Control Model

A neuroscience framework for sexual response developed by Janssen and Bancroft describing two competing neurological systems: the Sexual Excitation System (SES, the accelerator) and the Sexual Inhibition System (SIS, the brakes). The model explains why desire difficulties are rarely about wanting too little and more often about too many active inhibitors, stress, safety concerns, body image, relational disconnection, unprocessed trauma. In sex and intimacy therapy, identifying a person’s specific inhibitors is often more clinically useful than trying to increase excitation. The brakes nobody told you about →
