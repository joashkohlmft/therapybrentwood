The Ick: Is It Your Gut or Your Guard?

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·The ick is that sudden, almost physical “nope” toward someone you were into a minute ago. It is a real feeling, and a common one. In a 2025 study of single adults, nearly two in three said they had felt it.
  • ·But a feeling being real does not make it accurate. Sometimes the ick is your gut: it has noticed something about how a person treats people, and you should listen. Sometimes it is your guard: a nervous system that learned closeness is risky, stepping on the brakes just as things get real.
  • ·The biggest tell is timing. An ick that shows up right after someone leans in (texts back fast, says “I like you,” makes plans) is worth a second look before you act on it.
  • ·In long relationships, the ick looks different. It is usually a slow distaste, not a sudden flash, and it often sits on top of hurt that never got said. That version is less about disgust and more about grief.
  • ·You never have to push through disgust, especially physical or sexual disgust. The move is not to override the ick. It is to get curious about it before you let it make the decision.

The ick is real

You are on the third date. It is going well. Then they run to catch the waiter, or say “yummers,” or hold their phone a certain way, and something in you curdles. The attraction just drops. A minute ago you were picturing a fourth date. Now you are picturing your exit.

That is the ick. It started as a TikTok joke, but it describes something real and common. A 2025 study of single adults found that nearly two in three had felt it, and about a quarter of them ended things on the spot. Most told a friend about it. Few ever told the person.

That last detail is the one that caught my attention as a couples therapist. The ick gets talked about. It almost never gets talked to. And in my experience, what goes unsaid is usually where the real story is.

It is not a clinical term. Here is how I think about it.

You will not find the ick in a diagnostic manual. But I hear about it all the time, from people who are dating and from people twenty years into a marriage.

What I have noticed, over and over, is that the reaction people notice first is rarely the whole story. They notice the shutdown, the coldness, the irritation, the distaste. What they tend to miss is the softer feeling underneath, which is very often fear. Fear of being left. Fear of being swallowed up. Fear of needing someone who might not be there.

That idea sits at the center of how I work as an Emotionally Focused couples therapist. So when someone tells me “I just got the ick,” I do not assume they are wrong, and I do not assume they are right. I get curious about which of three things is happening.

Gut: when the ick is information

Some icks are your judgment working well.

He is charming with you and dismissive with the server. She makes fun of the thing you love, and it does not feel like teasing. He keeps pushing after you said no to a second drink. She talks about every ex as “crazy.” These can arrive as a visceral turn-off before you have words for them, and that is fine. Your body is often faster than your reasoning.

Disgust is a real emotion with a real job. It tells you to step back from something that is not good for you. I treat it with respect, not suspicion. You do not need an airtight argument for why you do not want to kiss someone. And you should never talk yourself through physical or sexual disgust to be “fair.” Overriding that signal does not build attraction. It teaches your body that its no does not count.

The tells of a gut ick

  • It is about character, values, or how they treat people, not a quirk.
  • It holds steady. It does not move from one small thing to the next.
  • You would feel it even if this person were not interested in you.

If that is your ick, you do not need this article to give you permission. You can leave, kindly and clearly.

Guard: when the ick is protection

Other icks are something else. They show up at a suspicious moment, they latch onto something small, and they seem to need a reason to exist.

The timing tell. Notice when it hit. Many people find their ick lands right after the other person leans in: they texted back quickly, said they had a great time, introduced you to a friend, made plans for next month. Before that, the same habits were invisible, or even cute. Interest went up, and the ick showed up on cue.

The moving target. A guard ick tends to migrate. If he stopped wearing the shoes, it would be the laugh. If she stopped saying the phrase, it would be the way she chews. When the ick keeps relocating, the ick is probably not about the shoes.

The relief. Pay attention to what you feel when you picture ending it. If the strongest feeling is relief, like a pressure valve opening, that is worth noticing. Relief at getting distance is a different feeling from disappointment that something did not work.

The rerun. Have you been here before? Same stage, different person, same sudden turn? A pattern across partners is the clearest sign the ick is coming from inside.

What may be underneath

Many of the people I sit with who get the guard ick learned early that needing others was risky. Reaching out got ignored, mocked, or punished, so they adapted: turn the feelings down, rely on yourself, keep a little distance so no one can get close enough to hurt you. Attachment researchers call these “deactivating” strategies. I think of them as a smart adaptation that has outlived its job.

Here is what I most want people to know: turning feelings down is not the same as not having them. In my office, the person who says “I just don’t feel much” is often the person working hardest to keep a lot of feeling under the floorboards. The need does not disappear. It goes underground, and the nervous system keeps watch for the moment closeness becomes real.

There is also a pattern I see that people recognize with an uncomfortable jolt: wanting closeness, then recoiling when it is actually offered. It usually traces back to a time when closeness was longed for and also not safe. The heart says come here. The body says not that close.

Sometimes an ick is a way of locking onto one flaw so there is a door to walk out of.

I want to be careful. I cannot tell you that your ick is this. Nobody can from the outside. What I can tell you is that a sudden turn-off arriving exactly as someone starts to like you back is worth asking about before you obey it.

Two other things that can look like a guard ick

The echo. Sometimes an ick is an old injury. A smell, a tone of voice, a beard, a way of laughing that belongs to someone who once hurt you. That is not shallow, and you are not being dramatic. It is your body remembering. It deserves gentle attention, and sometimes individual therapy, not a lecture about being picky. (More on how this works in Why Trauma Makes You Reactive.)

The checklist. Some people hold partners to a very exacting list, and small deviations register as disgust. I see this most in people who were held to impossible standards themselves. If this sounds familiar, you may also recognize yourself in the secret tests people give partners. Both are a way of protecting yourself from disappointment by catching it early.

Gut or guard? Five questions to ask before you decide

None of these is a verdict. Together they give you a better read than the feeling alone.

  1. When did it start? Before they showed interest, or right after?
  2. Is it about character or a quirk? How they treat people, or how they hold a fork?
  3. Does it move? If this one thing changed, would the ick go away, or find a new home?
  4. What do I feel when I picture leaving? Sadness, or mostly relief?
  5. Have I been here before? Same stage, same sudden drop, different person?

And one bonus question: would I care about this if they were a little less into me? If the answer is no, the ick may be about distance, not about them.

Grief: the ick in a long marriage

Most of the conversation about the ick is about dating. In my office, I see it in long marriages too. It just looks different.

In a long relationship, the ick is rarely a sudden flash about chewing. It is a slow, creeping distaste. The sound of his breathing at night. Her voice when she is on the phone. The way he says “babe.” You notice you flinch a little when they touch your shoulder. You feel a bit ashamed of it, and you do not say it out loud.

Here is what I usually find underneath, and it is not disgust.

It is a pile of small misses that nobody named. The hand that reached and was not taken. The phone that got the attention. The resentment that got swallowed at dinner, again. Each one is minor. Together they slowly change how your partner feels in your body. In my experience, couples rarely drift apart because they fight too often. They drift apart when the affection and responsiveness quietly drain out.

What sitting with couples has taught me is that anger, criticism, coldness, and contempt are usually protective. They form over softer feelings that feel too risky to show: hurt, loneliness, I gave up on you seeing me. Contempt, the eye-roll and the curl of the lip, is one of the signs I take most seriously in a couple. When I see it, I start asking what hurt it is standing guard over.

Sometimes the distaste belongs to the partner who has done most of the reaching for years and has quietly stopped. Sometimes it belongs to the partner who has gone numb to avoid conflict and now feels very little at all. If either sounds like your home, the pursuer-withdrawer cycle and roommate syndrome describe how couples get here.

What this is not: a reason to force yourself toward a partner your body is recoiling from. Desire does not respond to pressure. Disgust in the bedroom works like a brake, and the way through is making the relationship safe enough for the brake to ease, not pressing harder on the gas. (The brakes nobody told you about goes deeper on this.)

A safety note: if the contempt in your home is constant, if there is cruelty, or if you feel afraid of your partner, this is past what a reframe can fix. Please reach out to a professional, and if you are not safe, to someone who can help you get safe.

If you are the one who got “ick’d”

It stings to find out someone went cold over the way you run or the phrase you use. It can feel like proof that something is wrong with you.

Usually it is not. In my experience, a lot of icks say as much about the person feeling them (their standards, their nervous system, their history) as about the person on the receiving end. That does not make it hurt less, but it can make it less personal.

And if the ick came up inside a relationship, a lot of icks are about changeable habits. A partner who tells you gently (“this one thing gets under my skin, can we talk about it?”) is giving you something most people never get: the truth, said to your face instead of to a group chat. If you tend to feel every flicker of distance as rejection, Black Cat and Golden Retriever and why knowing your attachment style isn’t enough may help make sense of it.

What to do with an ick

If you are dating

  • Pause before you exit. Unless it is a gut ick about how they treat people, give it a beat. A feeling that is still there in a week is telling you something different from one that faded by morning.
  • Name it to yourself. Try two sentences and see which one fits: “I am disgusted by this,” or “I am scared of where this is going.” They lead to very different decisions.
  • Try one more date, on purpose. Not to force attraction, but as an experiment. Go in curious. Does the ick hold, or does it shrink once you stop scanning for it?
  • Tell the person, if it is small and fixable. It is tempting to only tell your friends. Telling the person, kindly, is rarer and often more useful.
  • Watch for the rerun. If this keeps happening at the same stage, the most loving thing you can do for your future relationships may be some individual work on what closeness stirs up. That is not a sign you are broken. It is a sign your protection is working overtime. (Individual therapy is one place to start.)

If you are married or partnered

  • Treat it as a signal, not a verdict. “I’ve been pulling away from you, and I don’t like it” is a far better opening than a list of what bothers you.
  • Look for the hurt underneath. Ask yourself when the distaste started and what was happening between you then.
  • Share changeable things gently and specifically, without sarcasm or the eye-roll. Contempt makes everything harder to hear.
  • Get help with the cycle, not just the habit. In my work with couples, we slow down the loop that keeps you both protected and far apart, so the softer feelings can finally be said and heard. When that happens, I have watched a lot of icks quietly lose their grip.

When the ick is right

Not every ick is a defense to be worked through. Some icks are your gut, and your gut is right. You are allowed to not want someone, and you do not owe anyone a reason that survives cross-examination.

Getting curious about an ick is not the same as overriding it. A curious ick is still allowed to end in a kind goodbye. The goal is only that you make the decision, not an old alarm that went off before you had time to look.

A hopeful close

The ick has become a punchline, and some of the punchlines are funny. But underneath the jokes is something tender. Sometimes it is a body saying this person is not safe for me. Sometimes it is a body saying I am not sure it is safe to be wanted. Sometimes, in a long marriage, it is a body saying I have been hurt and nobody noticed.

All three deserve to be taken seriously. Only one of them is a reason to leave. Helping people tell them apart, in individual work or in couples therapy, is some of the most meaningful work I do.

The ick is real either way. The question is whether it is telling you about them, or about how safe it feels to be wanted.

Common questions

What is the ick?

The ick is a sudden feeling of turn-off or repulsion toward someone you were attracted to, often set off by something small, like how they eat, run, or talk. It is a popular term rather than a clinical one, but it describes a real and common experience. In a 2025 study, about two in three single adults said they had felt it.

Is the ick a red flag or a sign of avoidant attachment?

It can be either, which is why it is worth sorting out. An ick about how someone treats people or your boundaries is often good information. An ick that arrives right as someone becomes interested, latches onto something trivial, and keeps moving from one flaw to the next may be a protective pullback from closeness. It is worth asking which one you are feeling before you act on it.

Why do I get the ick when someone likes me back?

For some people, closeness that becomes real sets off an old alarm, especially if needing others once felt unsafe. In my work, I often see people turn their feelings down and find a reason for distance right at that moment, or want closeness and then recoil when it arrives. A sudden ick at exactly that point does not prove this is happening, but it is a strong reason to get curious before you leave.

Can you get over the ick?

Sometimes. If the ick is about a small, changeable habit or about your own fear of closeness, it often fades with time, curiosity, and safety. If it is about someone’s character or values, or it is physical or sexual disgust that will not shift, it is fine to listen to it. You never have to push through disgust to be fair.

Can you get the ick from your spouse after years together?

Yes, though it usually looks different from the dating version. In long relationships it tends to be a slow distaste rather than a sudden flash, and in my experience it often sits on top of hurt that was never said: missed reaches, swallowed resentment, years of feeling unseen. Treating it as a signal of that hurt, rather than a verdict on your partner, is usually the way back.

Is the ick normal?

Very. Most people have felt it at some point, and research suggests women are more likely to recognize and report it. What matters is less whether you get the ick and more what you do with it: whether it helps you avoid a real mismatch or keeps ending relationships just as they start to work.

Should I break up over the ick?

If the ick is about how someone treats you or others, or it is a steady, physical no, you can leave kindly. If it is about a quirk, arrived right after they showed interest, or has happened at the same stage with several people, give it time and curiosity first. A pause rarely costs you much. Acting on every ick can cost you a relationship that was good.

How can EFT couples therapy help with the ick?

In a committed relationship, Emotionally Focused couples therapy helps partners slow down the cycle of distance and protection that often sits under a long-term ick, so the hurt underneath can be said and heard. As emotional safety returns, much of the distaste tends to soften. For people whose icks repeat across relationships, individual work can help with what closeness stirs up.
