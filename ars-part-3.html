You’re Not Crazy: Why You Feel Like the Only One Trying

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome — 4-part series

You're Not Crazy: Why You Feel Like You're the Only One Trying

This is Part 3 of a four-part series on Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome. This piece is not written for clinicians. It is written for you. The one who is tired. The one who keeps trying. The one who is starting to wonder if something is fundamentally wrong with them. If that is you, keep reading.

In brief

  • ·If you feel like you are the only one trying in your relationship, you are not imagining it — and you are not too much.
  • ·The exhaustion you feel is not a character flaw. It is a rational response to sustained one-sided effort.
  • ·ARS creates a specific kind of loneliness: being in a relationship while feeling completely alone inside it.
  • ·Your pursuit, your emotional reactivity, your sense that something is wrong — these are signals, not problems.

You Already Know Something Is Wrong

You do not need a diagnosis to know something is wrong.

You have known for a while now. Maybe for months. Maybe for years. There is a feeling you carry, low and persistent, like a sound you cannot quite locate in a quiet house. Something is off. Something is missing. Something that used to be there is not there anymore, or maybe was never quite there in the way you needed it to be.

And you have tried. You have tried in ways that have cost you real things. Sleep. Confidence. The easy, uncomplicated version of yourself that existed before this relationship took up so much room inside your head.

You have tried explaining. You have tried softening. You have tried being more patient, more understanding, less reactive, less needy, less you. You have tried scheduling conversations and writing letters and reading books and sitting in a therapist's office describing a dynamic that sounds, out loud, almost too exhausting to believe.

And still. Your partner waits. Or sleeps. Or moves through the day as if everything is fine.

And I know what happens next. Inside your head, I mean.

You replay it. You go back through the conversation looking for the moment you could have said it differently. You wonder if you are too sensitive. Too intense. Too much work to love. You think about the way your therapist once gently suggested you might want to explore your "reactivity." You think about the way your partner looked at you during the last argument, tired and slightly defeated, as if you were a problem they had stopped believing they could solve.

And part of you, the part that has been worn down by months or years of this, starts to wonder if they are right.

Maybe it is me. Maybe I am the problem. Maybe a different person, a calmer person, a less needy person, could love this partner without all of this pain.

I want to stay with that thought for a moment before I tell you what I actually think. Because I know that thought is not just a passing doubt. For many people living inside this dynamic, it has become a kind of background noise, low and constant, running underneath everything. The suspicion that the evidence is overwhelming and the verdict is already in.

You have been told it is you enough times, and in enough different ways, that some part of you has started to file it as fact.

It is not fact. But I understand why it feels like one.

It Is Not You

Let me say this as plainly as I know how.

The fact that you feel alone in your relationship is not proof that you are too sensitive. It is not proof that you are too needy, too intense, or too much. It is not proof that you are incapable of a healthy relationship or that you love in a way that is fundamentally broken.

It is proof that you are in a relationship where the emotional labor has fallen almost entirely on your shoulders. And that is not the same thing at all.

There is a difference, a real and significant difference, between a person who is anxious by nature and a person who has become anxious in response to a specific relational environment. Between a person who is clingy and a person who has been reaching, repeatedly, into silence, and has had to reach harder each time just to get any response at all.

You may have been told these are the same thing. They are not.

What you are experiencing has a name: Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome. And while the name is clinical, what it describes is deeply human. It is what happens to a person, to any person, when they are left to carry 100% of a relationship's emotional life alone, consistently, over time, with no relief and no acknowledgment.

Your nervous system is not broken. It is responding exactly as it was designed to respond to prolonged relational threat.

What It Actually Feels Like From the Inside

There is the version of you that other people see.

The one who brings things up too often. Who cannot seem to let things go. Who sends the texts and starts the conversations and sometimes, in the worst moments, says things in a tone of voice that frightens even yourself. The one who cries at the wrong times, or goes very quiet in a way that fills the room with a particular kind of tension.

That version of you is real. But it is not the whole story. And it is not the beginning of the story.

The beginning of the story is this.

Something happens, or often, nothing happens, and that nothingness is the whole problem. Your partner goes quiet after a difficult moment. Or responds to your reaching with a flat, one-word answer. Or falls asleep without resolving anything, as if the rupture between you is simply not registering on any of their instruments.

And something in your body fires. Not a thought first. A sensation. A dropping in the chest. A tightening. Something that feels, if you have never had a name for it before, like the floor suddenly becoming unreliable beneath you.

That sensation is your attachment system. It is one of the oldest and most essential parts of your human neurology. It exists because we are a species that survives through connection, not in spite of needing other people, but because of it. And it is exquisitely sensitive to signals that the bond we depend on is in danger.

When your partner goes quiet, your nervous system does not read it as: my partner needs some space. It reads it as: the bond is in danger. Do something. Now.

So you do something. You reach. You ask. You try to open a door that keeps closing. You push harder because the alternative, sitting alone with the silence and no resolution, feels unbearable in a way that is almost physical. And the harder you push, the more your partner seems to retreat. And the more they retreat, the more desperate you become.

And then it is over, for now, and you are left holding two things simultaneously.

The original pain of their absence. And a fresh, hot layer of shame about how you responded to it.

You're too much. You're crazy. Why can't you just let it go?

This is the voice. You probably know it well by now. What I want you to know is that this voice is not telling you the truth. It is telling you what the dynamic has taught you. And those are not the same thing.

Why You Cannot Simply "Calm Down"

People have probably told you to calm down. To be less reactive. To give your partner space. To stop pursuing. To just let things go and see what happens.

And you have probably tried. God, you have tried. You have white-knuckled your way through silences that felt like slow suffocation. You have put the phone down and not sent the text and lain awake at 3am with your body humming with an alarm that no amount of deep breathing seems to reach. You have done the breathing exercises and the journaling and the long walks designed to clear your head, and you have come back to the same house, the same silence, the same feeling of the floor being subtly unreliable beneath you.

It is not that you lack willpower. It is not that you haven't tried hard enough.

Because here is what those well-meaning suggestions do not account for.

You cannot think your way out of a body that is in survival mode.

When your nervous system has registered prolonged relational threat, it does not respond to logic. It does not respond to reminders that you are an adult, that this is not an emergency, that your partner is probably just tired. It responds to evidence. And the evidence it has been accumulating, from months or years of reaching into silence, from every failed repair attempt, from every moment your distress went unacknowledged, tells it one thing with great consistency:

This bond is not safe. Stay alert.

Telling a nervous system in this state to simply calm down is a little like telling someone with a broken leg to just walk it off. The instruction is not wrong exactly. It just has no access to the actual problem.

What helps is not suppression. What helps is processing. What helps is finding the feeling beneath the alarm, the grief, the longing, the quiet terror of being invisible to the person you love most, and finally having a space where that feeling can be spoken out loud and met with something other than silence.

The Part Nobody Talks About

There is something that happens in relationships like this that almost nobody names, and I want to name it here because I think it may be one of the loneliest parts of the whole experience.

At some point, you stop being sure whether you still love your partner or whether you have simply become addicted to the cycle.

You are not sure anymore where the genuine connection ends and the desperate need for relief begins. You notice that the moments you feel closest to your partner are not the ordinary, quiet moments, the Tuesday evenings, the Saturday mornings, but the moments right after a rupture finally resolves. The brief warmth that follows a crisis. The way they reach for you when the tension lifts.

And you find yourself wondering, with a shame you can barely look at directly: am I staying for love, or am I staying because I have confused the relief of the cycle ending with the feeling of being loved?

This is not a sign that you are broken. It is a sign that you have been in an asymmetric system for long enough that your nervous system has learned to find its moments of safety in the only places they have been reliably available.

It is a sign that something needs to change. Not necessarily the relationship. But something.

I want to say something to the part of you that is ashamed of this confusion. The part that thinks a person who truly loved their partner would not be asking these questions. That real love would be cleaner than this, clearer, less entangled with exhaustion and relief and the complicated calculus of staying.

That part of you is wrong. Not because love is not real, but because love, when it has been lived inside an asymmetric system for long enough, starts to look different from the outside than it feels from the inside. The confusion is not evidence that you do not love your partner. It is evidence that you have been trying to love them in conditions that were never designed to sustain love. You have been pouring something real into a vessel that kept leaking. And you are tired. And the tiredness has started to blur the edges of everything, including the love itself.

That does not mean the love is gone. It means it needs room to breathe. Room it cannot find inside the cycle.

The Loneliness Nobody Sees, And What You Deserve to Know About It

People from the outside often do not understand why you stay. Or why you are so upset. Or why you cannot just talk to your partner about it. They say things like: "Have you tried just telling them how you feel?" And you want to laugh, or cry, because yes. You have tried. In seventeen different ways, in seventeen different tones of voice, at seventeen different times of day.

The loneliness of ARS is not just the loneliness of being in a relationship where you feel unseen. It is the loneliness of not being able to explain that loneliness to anyone outside it. Because from the outside, you are in a relationship. You share a home, perhaps. You show up to things together. The structure of coupledom is intact.

But inside the structure, you are alone. You are carrying something no one can see. And the weight of it, after months or years, begins to change you in ways that are hard to articulate. You become more vigilant. More sensitive to small shifts in mood. More easily undone by a tone of voice, a slight withdrawal, an evening of noticeable distance. Not because you are fragile, but because your nervous system has been trained, by accumulated experience, to treat those signals as serious.

You are not overreacting. You are accurately reading a pattern that has been consistent and real.

And here is what you deserve to know about all of it.

You deserve to know that the intensity of what you feel is not disproportionate. It is proportionate to what you have been living. Proportionate to months or years of reaching for someone who keeps not quite meeting you there. Proportionate to the experience of being physically present in a relationship while feeling emotionally alone inside it.

You deserve to know that wanting to be reached for is not needy. It is human. The need for a partner who initiates, who repairs, who reaches back, who says "are we okay?" sometimes without being asked, that is not an unreasonable demand on a relationship. It is one of the most basic things a relationship is for.

You deserve to know that your pain is real even if your partner cannot see it. Even if a previous therapist inadvertently focused more on your reactions than on the structural imbalance producing them. Even if the people around you have gently suggested that you might be the difficult one.

Your pain is real. It is legible. It makes complete sense given what you have been carrying.

And you deserve to know that this does not have to be the rest of your story.

What You Deserve to Know

You Are Not the Only One

Right now, somewhere, there is someone else lying awake with their phone in their hand, drafting a text they will probably delete. Someone else replaying a conversation from three days ago, looking for the moment they could have said it differently. Someone else staring at the ceiling after sex that felt less like intimacy and more like a transaction, wondering how they got here, wondering if this is just what love looks like after a while, wondering if the longing they feel for something more is a want or a need or a delusion.

You are not the only one. And you are not crazy.

You are someone who has been carrying something that was never meant to be carried alone.

That is not the end of the story. It is, if you are willing, the beginning of a different one.

What Comes Next

The version of this story where things get better does not begin with you working harder. It does not begin with you finding the right script, becoming less reactive, or giving your partner more space and hoping they notice.

It begins with you being met.

By a therapist, first. Someone who does not flinch at the size of what you are carrying. Someone who will not hand you a worksheet when you start to cry, or gently redirect you toward a softer tone when what you actually need is for someone to finally say: yes. This is real. What is happening to you makes complete sense.

In that space, something begins to shift. Slowly. Not because you have been fixed, but because you have been heard. Maybe for the first time, in the specific way that this particular pain needed to be heard.

And from that place of being genuinely met, something becomes accessible that the cycle has kept buried. The feeling beneath the alarm. The grief beneath the anger. The quiet, almost unbearable longing that has been driving all of it from the beginning.

Not "why won't you just talk to me."

Something quieter and truer than that. Something like: "I miss you. I am afraid. I need to know I still matter to you."

That softer reach, when you find it, changes everything. Not because it magically changes your partner, though it sometimes does. But because it changes your relationship to yourself. You stop being a person who is too much and start being a person who has needs. Real ones. Human ones. Ones that were always legitimate, even when they were being expressed in the only language available to you at the time.

That is not a small shift. It is, in many ways, the whole shift.

And it is waiting for you. Not somewhere distant or difficult to reach. But in a room, with the right person, where you finally do not have to carry any of this alone.

If you are ready to find that room, I am here.

For the one who has been told they are too much

The frantic texts. The tears. The exhausting loop that goes nowhere. The desperate need to just get your partner to look at you — really look at you.

These are not signs of a broken person. They are the completely predictable response of a nervous system fighting for a bond it cannot afford to lose. That is not pathology. That is loyalty to love, in the only language your body knew how to speak.

You are not the only one. And you are not crazy.

You are someone who has been carrying something that was never meant to be carried alone. That is not the end of the story. It is, if you are willing, the beginning of a different one.
