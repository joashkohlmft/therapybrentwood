Why Money Fights Are Never Really About Money

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·Couples who fight about money are almost never actually fighting about money. The dollar amount is the doorway. What is really on the table is safety, trust, freedom, and whether your needs count.
  • ·Underneath most money fights are two competing attachment needs: "I need to know we are on the same page and I can count on you," and "I need some autonomy, I cannot be controlled." Both are legitimate. They just collide.
  • ·The spender and the saver are usually running the same pursue-withdraw cycle you know from other fights, only now it is wearing a dollar sign.
  • ·The scripts driving it were mostly written in childhood, long before this relationship. The fight about a purchase is often two old family stories arguing with each other.
  • ·Arguing about money is one of the strongest predictors of divorce, even after you control for how much money a couple has. The hope in that: the problem is the pattern, not the paycheck, and patterns can change.

The fight that is never about the receipt

Almost every couple I work with has some version of the money fight. It flares up over something small, a purchase, a bill, a number on a statement, and within minutes it is bigger and hotter than the thing that started it, and it ends the same way it always does, with both people feeling unseen and a little alone. Then it goes quiet until the next one.

Here is the thing I most want you to take from this piece: you are almost certainly not fighting about money. The money is real, and the numbers matter, but the heat, the part that leaves you both raw, is coming from somewhere underneath. Money is one of the most loaded symbols we have. It stands in for safety, freedom, power, love, and control, and when a couple fights about it, those are the things actually on the table. The budget is just the doorway.

Let me show you what is usually on the other side of that door, and why the way through is almost never a better spreadsheet.

What money is really standing in for

When you fight about a purchase, your nervous system is not doing math. It is asking older, deeper questions. Are we going to be okay. Can I count on you. Do I get any say here. Do my needs matter to you. Am I safe. Money is unusually good at carrying those questions because for most of us it got tangled up with them very early.

For one person, money means security, the wall between the family and disaster. For another, it means freedom, the ability to breathe and not be controlled. For another it is love, the way care got shown or withheld. For another it is worth, proof that they are enough. Two partners almost never assign money the same meaning, and they usually do not know they have assigned it one at all. So they end up fighting in two different languages, each certain the other is being unreasonable, when really they are defending two different survival strategies.

Picture this. One of you comes home with something that cost two hundred dollars. To the one who bought it, it was nothing, a small bit of breathing room in a life that feels tightly managed. To the one who sees the statement, it is not two hundred dollars at all. It is proof that they are alone in worrying, that the ground could give way and nobody else is holding it. Within a minute you are fighting, and you are each certain the other is being ridiculous, because you are not actually arguing about the same thing. One of you is defending a little freedom. The other is defending the family against disaster. Neither of you is wrong, and neither of you feels heard.

The two needs underneath the fight

Slow almost any money fight down and it resolves into two competing needs, both of them legitimate, both of them tender.

On one side is usually a need for security and partnership, some version of I need to know we are on the same page, that I can count on you, that you are not going to blindside me. Often this belongs to the partner who learned early that money is not safe, who watched it run out, who has been careful ever since because careful is what kept them okay.

On the other side is usually a need for autonomy and trust, some version of I need to not be controlled, to have some room that is mine, to be treated like an adult. Often this belongs to the partner who grew up feeling managed, monitored, or squeezed, for whom being watched and questioned is the exact old feeling they swore they would never live in again.

Neither of those needs is wrong. That is the hard, hopeful truth of it. The saver reaching for security and the spender reaching for freedom are both reaching for something good. But each one's move sets off the other's alarm. The tighter one partner grips for safety, the more controlled the other feels, and the more they push for room, the less safe the first one feels, and around it goes.

Diagram: The Money Standoff

The spender and the saver are doing a familiar dance

If that push and pull sounds familiar, it should. It is the same pursue-withdraw cycle that runs underneath so many couples' fights, just wearing a dollar sign this time. One partner pursues, wanting to talk about the budget, wanting reassurance, wanting to feel the two of you are a team. The other withdraws, going quiet, getting defensive, avoiding the conversation because it only ever seems to end in feeling like a failure or a suspect. The pursuer reads the withdrawal as you do not care, the withdrawer reads the pursuit as you do not trust me, and both of them are wrong about the other and right about their own pain. I have written about this pattern in its general form in the pursuer-withdrawer cycle, and money is one of the most common places it lives.

It is not always a saver and a spender

I have described this as a saver and a spender, because that is the most common shape, but it is not the only one, and it helps to know your own. Sometimes both partners are spenders, and the real problem is that neither of you wants to be the one who looks hard at the numbers, so the debt quietly grows while you avoid it together. Sometimes both of you are anxious savers, starving a life you could actually afford because neither of you feels safe enough to enjoy it. And very often the fight is not about spending at all but about earning and power, who brings in more, who therefore feels they get the final say, and who quietly feels their voice does not count. Whatever the shape, the same thing holds. The surface is money. The real subject is safety, fairness, and whether the two of you are a team.

The scripts were written before you met

Here is why these fights feel so much older than your relationship: they are. Most of us absorbed a set of unspoken rules about money in childhood, long before we could question them. Clinicians sometimes call these money scripts, and they tend to fall into a few patterns, being cautious and secretive with money, avoiding it as if it were dirty, chasing it as proof of worth, or treating it as the thing that will finally make everything okay.

You do not choose these. You inherit them. One partner remembers their parents screaming about bills and swore they would always keep a cushion. The other remembers a parent who used money and gifts to apologize, or to control, and swore they would never be managed that way again. Those two childhood scenes are often the ones actually arguing in your kitchen, thirty years later, using your voices. When couples can see that, something softens. It is much easier to be tender with a frightened kid than with a “controlling” or “irresponsible” partner.

The budget was never the problem. The problem was that money had become the language you fought your safety battles in.
A brief note on financial secrecy. Sometimes the issue is not a difference in meaning but a breach of trust, a hidden account, an undisclosed debt, a secret purchase. When it happens it usually is not simple deceit. More often it is shame, or a bid for a scrap of autonomy in a relationship where money had come to feel controlled. It still has to be faced, and like other breaches of trust it can be repaired, by getting underneath the act to the fear beneath it. My pieces on affairs and what real repair asks for walk through that kind of healing.

When the threat is real

I want to be careful not to over-spiritualize this, because sometimes money stress is not a symbol at all. Sometimes it is a genuine, external threat. A job loss, a medical bill, a business that failed, the slow grind of a cost of living that keeps outpacing the paychecks. When a person loses the ability to provide the way they believed they were supposed to, it can hit at something far deeper than the bank balance. It can hit their sense of who they are.

This lands especially hard along the lines of how many of us were raised, because money and traditional gender roles are still tightly braided together, even in couples who think of themselves as modern and equal. For a lot of men in particular, worth and manhood got quietly welded to providing, to being the one who keeps the family safe and afloat. It is one of the oldest scripts there is, and it runs deep. So when the income falls away, or a partner begins to out-earn him, or the role he was raised to fill is suddenly out of reach, it can feel like a failure of identity, not just of finances. And the most common response to that particular shame is not to reach out. It is to pull away, to go quiet, to carry it alone and disappear a little, because being seen failing at the one thing you believed you were supposed to be for your family can feel unbearable. A partner often reads that withdrawal as coldness or checking out, when underneath it is usually someone drowning in a story about not being enough.

It runs in other directions too, and in its own shapes, the partner who swore she would never have to depend on anyone and now has to, the one carrying a whole household on an income that was never meant to hold it alone, the same-sex couple with no inherited script for who is supposed to provide at all, the couple whose roles flipped and who have no map for it. However it shows up, the work here is different from decoding symbolism. The work is refusing to face the real threat alone. The couples who survive genuine financial hardship are not the ones who never struggle. They are the ones who manage to turn and face the wolf together, side by side, instead of letting it convince them the problem is each other.

When where you live turns up the volume

Sometimes the pressure gets amplified by the world around you. In a high-cost, high-comparison area, and I see this a lot with couples in Franklin and across Williamson County, the ordinary money strain gets a second engine: the quiet, constant comparison. The neighbors' renovation, the private school everyone assumes, the cars, the trips, the sense that keeping up is simply what is expected here. Lifestyle inflation is not vanity. It is a nervous system trying to feel like it belongs and is doing okay. But it turns the volume way up on every one of the fears underneath, and it can make two people who love each other feel like they are failing at a race neither of them agreed to run. I have written more about that particular squeeze on my couples therapy in Franklin page.

The way through

The way through a money fight is almost never a better budget. Couples come in expecting me to referee the finances, to declare who is right. I try to do something more useful, which is to help them stop fighting about the content and start looking at the pattern.

One of the most useful questions I can ask a couple is not “what should you do about the money.” It is: “When the two of you try to talk about money, what happens between you?” That question moves the whole conversation off the dollars and onto the thing that actually keeps breaking, the moment it goes sideways, the point where one of you shuts down and the other pushes harder. That failed conversation, not the number in dispute, is where the relationship actually needs help. This is the heart of the work I do in couples therapy, built on Emotionally Focused Therapy.

From there it gets quieter and more human. We get underneath the position, the saver's we have to be careful, the spender's stop controlling me, to the softer thing beneath it, the fear of not being safe, the fear of not being trusted, the longing to be a team. When two people can say the real thing, I get scared we will end up like my parents or when you check the account I feel like a child again, the fight changes shape entirely, because now they are not enemies across a spreadsheet. They are two people trying to feel safe, finally able to help each other do it.

If you want one concrete place to start before you ever set foot in a therapist's office, try this. Pick a calm time, not the middle of a fight, and agree on two small rules going in: you are not going to decide anything in this first conversation, and each of you will name the feeling under your position instead of the position itself. Not you spend too much but I get scared we are not going to be okay. Not you never let me breathe but I feel like a child who has to ask permission. You will not solve the money that night, and you are not trying to. You are trying to let each other finally see the fear the number was carrying. That is the conversation that actually changes things.

The money still has to get sorted, and it does, more easily than couples expect once the threat underneath has cooled. But the budget was never the problem. The problem was that money had become the language you fought your safety battles in. Change that, and the numbers get a lot less scary.

If the same money fight keeps happening no matter how many budgets you make, it is not a math problem. It is a pattern, and patterns can change.

Common questions

Why do my partner and I keep fighting about money?

Because you are almost certainly not fighting about money. The dollar amount is the doorway. Underneath most money fights are two competing attachment needs, usually something like "I need to know we are on the same page and I can count on you" on one side, and "I need some autonomy, I cannot be controlled" on the other. Both are legitimate, and each partner's move sets off the other's alarm, which is why the same fight keeps happening no matter how many budgets you make.

Is fighting about money normal in a relationship?

Very. Money is one of the most common things couples argue about, and research finds that arguing about money is one of the strongest predictors of divorce, even after controlling for how much money a couple actually has. That is not because money is uniquely important, but because it carries so much meaning: safety, freedom, power, love, and worth all get bound up in it.

What do money fights really mean?

When you fight about a purchase, your nervous system is not doing math. It is asking older questions: are we going to be okay, can I count on you, do I get any say, do my needs matter, am I safe. Money is unusually good at carrying those questions because for most of us it got tangled up with them very early. For one partner money means security, for another freedom, for another love or worth. The fight is about those meanings, not the number.

Why is my partner a spender and I'm a saver (or the other way around)?

Most of us absorbed unspoken rules about money in childhood, sometimes called money scripts, long before we could question them. One partner watched money run out and learned to be careful; another felt managed or controlled through money and swore they would never live that way again. The saver reaching for security and the spender reaching for freedom are both reaching for something legitimate. They are usually two old family stories arguing with each other through you.

Is hiding money from your partner cheating?

Financial secrecy, sometimes called financial infidelity, is more common than people admit; a recent national survey found close to half of couples do not know everything about each other's finances, and many consider hiding money at least as serious as physical cheating. When it happens it usually is not simple deceit. It is often shame, or a bid for autonomy inside a relationship where money had come to feel controlled. It still has to be faced, and like other breaches of trust, it can be repaired.

How do we stop fighting about money?

Not with a better budget. The most useful move is to stop fighting about the content and look at the pattern. Instead of asking "what should we do about the money," ask "when the two of us try to talk about money, what happens between us?" That shifts the conversation off the dollars and onto the moment it keeps going sideways, the point where one of you shuts down and the other pushes harder. That failed conversation, not the number, is where the relationship actually needs help.

Can financial stress cause a breakup or divorce?

It can, but it is the conflict pattern, not the income, that predicts the damage. Sometimes money stress is a genuine external threat, a job loss, a lost provider role, a high cost of living, and it can hit a person's very sense of who they are, especially where identity is tied to providing. The common response is to withdraw in shame rather than reach out. Couples who survive genuine hardship are not the ones who never struggle, but the ones who face the threat side by side instead of turning on each other.

Should we see a therapist or a financial advisor for money problems?

They help different layers, and many couples need both. A financial advisor helps with the plan; a couples therapist helps with the pattern. A simple test: if the same money fight keeps happening no matter how sensible the plan is, the problem is not really the math. It is a relationship pattern, and that is what couples therapy is for.
