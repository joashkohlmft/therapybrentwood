# My Responsibilities When Working on Our Relationship

*A guide for couples-therapy readiness, drawing on Emotionally Focused Therapy, attachment-based work, and relationship repair practice*

This guide is written in the first person, so you can read it as something you are telling yourself. No one does this perfectly. The goal is not perfection but ownership: taking responsibility for how you show up, how you affect your partner, and how you participate when the relationship needs repair.

Use it as a personal commitment, a conversation starter with your partner, and something to return to when things feel hard.

---

## A few words you will see often

The short definitions below are meant to make the rest of the guide easier to read. More definitions are available here: [therapybrentwood.com/eft-glossary](https://www.therapybrentwood.com/eft-glossary)

**Secure connection** — The deep emotional bond that lets a partner feel safe, trusted, and held. Adult love relationships rest on it, much the way a child rests on a reliable caregiver.

**The cycle (or negative cycle)** — The repeating, predictable pattern a couple falls into when distressed. A common version: one partner pushes for connection, the other pulls back. Each move makes the other worse.

**Attachment injury** — A specific moment when a partner felt abandoned, betrayed, or left alone in a real time of need. If not repaired, that moment keeps surfacing in later conflicts.

**Flooded / triggered / dysregulated** — Physically overwhelmed in a conflict: racing heart, tight chest, blank mind. When you are flooded, clear thinking and openness are almost impossible until the body settles.

**Repair attempt** — Any small move offered to de-escalate or reconnect: an apology, a softer tone, a kind joke, a touch, a pause, an attempt to begin again.

**Co-regulation** — Helping each other calm down through tone of voice, presence, touch, eye contact, or words. Partners do this for each other constantly, often without noticing.

**Vulnerability / softer emotions** — The feelings underneath anger, distance, or criticism: usually fear, hurt, shame, loneliness, or longing.

## Bonding Event: Twenty-one commitments I can make

### 1. I commit to the therapy process for a real stretch of time.

Repair rarely works when one foot is already out the door. I will give therapy enough time to work, typically several months of consistent effort, rather than treating it as a quick experiment to abandon the first time I feel discouraged. During that period, I will not use threats of separation as leverage.

Commitment does not guarantee a happy ending, and it does not override real safety concerns. It means my behavior matches what I say I want.

### 2. I show up willingly and stay engaged.

I will attend sessions consistently, participate honestly, and not act as though my partner is making me come. I do not have to feel ready every time, but I do need some willingness. Change requires repetition, discomfort, and patience. When something hard comes up in me, overwhelm, shame, skepticism, or defensiveness, I will try to name it rather than disappear into silence, sarcasm, or refusal.

### 3. I work on myself, not just on what is wrong with my partner.

Couples' work is not only about what my partner needs to change. I will honestly look at my own moves: defensiveness, avoidance, criticism, difficulty trusting, trouble naming what I need, and the way I shut down or strike out under stress. Attachment-based work asks each partner to notice how they protect themselves and how those protections affect the bond.

### 4. I bring the relationship work home.

Insight that stays in the therapy office does not change a relationship. I will practice between sessions: slowing down in conflict, using softer language, offering reassurance, gently revisiting a hard moment, and trying a repair instead of escalating. Homework is where trust is rebuilt. I will be as flexible as I can with scheduling, reading, reflection, and any assignments we agree on.

### 5. I treat my partner with respect, and I refuse contempt, cruelty, and abuse.

Strong feelings do not cancel basic respect. Even in conflict, I will not use mockery, humiliation, intimidation, or personal attacks. Contempt (eye-rolling, name-calling, talking down) is one of the most reliable predictors that a relationship is in trouble; it tells my partner their worth is up for negotiation. Beyond contempt is chronic lying, coercion, threats, aggressive outbursts, or any form of abuse. Those patterns are not part of the work, they make the work impossible, and they must be named and addressed seriously. If I am the one causing harm, my job is to stop, accept accountability, and seek whatever additional help is needed. Respect is the floor that makes the rest of this work possible.

### 6. I notice when we are fighting about details and steer back to what is really happening.

Couples can get stuck arguing about content: who said what, whose memory is right, which version is correct. Content matters, but when we are both flooded, fixating on the facts usually results in losing the connection. I will try to notice when we are sliding into a content battle and gently redirect us toward the deeper layer: what each of us is feeling, how we are affecting each other, and what is happening in the cycle. Steering back to the process is not dodging the problem. It is making the problem solvable.

### 7. I try to understand my partner's experience, even when I see things differently.

My partner's experience is real, even when I do not share every conclusion or memory. My job is not to prove my innocence or argue them out of their pain. It is to slow down and understand how the moment landed on them. Understanding is not the same as agreeing; I can hold my own perspective while making real room for theirs. When I cross-examine instead of listening, I usually deepen the injury.

### 8. I risk being open instead of hiding behind defenses.

In distressed relationships, most of us reach for protection: defending, criticizing, intellectualizing, counter-attacking, going numb. Those moves usually make sense given past hurt, but they rarely build closeness. My job is to move beyond the protection and name the softer thing underneath: fear, hurt, loneliness, shame, longing, the wish to be reassured. Vulnerability is risky and can feel exposing. Still, a secure connection grows when one partner can show what is actually going on inside.

### 9. I respond carefully when my partner opens up.

When my partner takes a risk and shows me something tender, I want to handle that moment well. I do not need a perfect script. I do need to communicate that I am listening and that what is happening to them matters to me. Useful responses sound like: "Thank you for telling me." "I can see why that hurt." "I get why this matters to you." "You matter to me. I do not want you to be alone with this." These are not lines to perform. They are ways of being emotionally available and responsive.

### 10. I name my deeper fears and what I actually need.

Unspoken fears drive many fights. I might fear rejection, not being enough, being controlled, being abandoned, being unseen, being blamed, or simply not mattering. I will work on naming those directly. I will also name what I am actually after: closeness, reassurance, respect, comfort, gentleness, consistency, space, and connection. When I only show anger or distance, my partner misses the real message. Clearer naming turns conflict into contact.

### 11. I make space for repair to actually work.

Repair attempts are the small moves that keep a fight from going over the cliff: an apology, a softer tone, a kind joke, a hand extended, a pause, an attempt to begin again. My job is to make them and to recognize my partner's. If I reject every imperfect attempt, I teach the relationship to give up. The same applies to giving change a chance to land. I will not set tests that my partner cannot pass, move the goalposts without warning, or treat one mistake as proof that nothing real is happening. I may still need more than one apology or more than one conversation, and that is fair. But I have to leave room for repeated, accountable effort to count. Letting my partner succeed does not mean pretending trust is already rebuilt; it means letting repair actually do its work.

### 12. I repeat reassurance, more often than feels natural.

In a distressed relationship, one good moment is usually not enough. Old hurts make partners unsure whether the connection is real or will last. I will say "You matter," "I love you," "I care," "I want to get this right," "I am here," "We are not enemies," not once, but often enough that they begin to be believed. Reassurance is not weakness. It is how trust is rebuilt. Trust grows out of pattern, not isolated moments.

### 13. I calm myself instead of making my partner do all the regulating.

My partner can support me, but my partner cannot be my entire nervous system. When I notice I am flooded, pounding heart, clenched jaw, blank mind, I will use what works: slow breathing, grounding, lowering my voice, taking a short and agreed-upon pause, naming what is happening, and returning when I can actually be present. Self-regulation is not shutting down or pretending I am fine. It is enough steadiness inside me to stay connected rather than reactive.

### 14. I offer consistent care, not just convenient care.

Relationships feel safer when support is dependable. I will not only be caring when I am in a good mood or when the issue feels small. My partner needs to know whether I will show up when it matters most. Intermittent support is its own kind of injury; it offers hope and then disappears. Consistency does not mean constant intensity. It means becoming someone my partner can count on.

### 15. I own my impact, not just my intent.

I may not have meant to wound, dismiss, scare, or shut my partner out. But if that is how my behavior landed, the impact still needs to be acknowledged. Accountability strengthens trust; defensiveness erodes it. Owning impact sounds like: "I can see how that affected you." "I get why that hurt." "I do not want to keep doing that." "I will work on responding differently." Real accountability is more than saying sorry. It is understanding the injury, validating it, and changing the pattern over time.

### 16. I use therapy language with care.

The concepts we learn in therapy, such as triggers, wounds, attachment needs, and cycles, can be genuinely useful for understanding ourselves and each other. They can also drift, sometimes without my noticing, into a different role: explaining away my own behavior, labeling my partner's reactions, or strengthening my side of an argument. I will try to catch when that starts in me. The point of therapy is more honesty and more safety, not more sophisticated blame. And when my partner takes a risk by being vulnerable, I want to hold what they share gently. It is for understanding between us, not for the next disagreement.

### 17. I do not threaten to leave to control the moment.

When I am angry, scared, or overwhelmed, I may feel pulled to threaten the relationship itself. I will not use divorce, separation, or moving out as a weapon to win, scare, or force compliance. Those threats destabilize the bond and make real repair much harder. If I am genuinely unsure whether I can stay in this relationship, my job is to bring that into therapy as something to work on, not to throw it like shrapnel into a fight.

### 18. I speak up early instead of going silent and planning an exit.

One of the most damaging patterns in couples' work is quiet disengagement: pretending to participate while privately building a case against the relationship. If I am discouraged, disconnected, resentful, or doubting that the work is helping, I will say so early. That gives the relationship and the therapy a chance to respond. Silent withdrawal followed by sudden departure is more painful and less honest.

### 19. I choose outside support carefully.

Outside support matters, but not every voice helps the relationship. I will be thoughtful about who I confide in. I want people who help me think clearly, stay accountable, and support real repair. I do not want people who inflame contempt, applaud impulsive decisions, or encourage me to skip the hard work. This is not secrecy; it is discernment. Healthy confidants can strengthen the work. Unhelpful ones can lock me into the negative cycle.

### 20. I am patient because trust rebuilds slowly.

Change in a relationship is rarely a straight line. There will be progress, setbacks, tender moments, slips back into the old cycle, and another try. I will work to tolerate the truth that a secure connection is built gradually. Impatience can curdle into hopelessness or pressure. Patience does not mean passivity. It means staying actively engaged in the work even when the payoff is not immediate.

### 21. I remember that my partner is not the whole problem, and neither am I.

Distressed couples often organize themselves around blame. I will look honestly at my own moves in the relationship. Even when one partner has caused a major injury, both of us eventually need to understand how the cycle keeps getting reinforced now. That does not erase accountability for serious harms. It actually supports more effective repair. When I can see my own pattern without collapsing into shame or attacking back, I become someone the relationship can use.

---

## A commitment I can come back to

I can return to this guide when therapy feels hard, when I am tempted to blame, withdraw, or give up, and when I need to remember that relationship repair is built through repeated acts of courage, accountability, care, and responsiveness.

---

> **A note on safety.** This guide assumes that the relationship can be worked on safely. Where there is abuse, coercive control, ongoing intimidation, or danger, safety must come first. Vulnerability work is not appropriate in an unsafe relationship. Please speak with a qualified clinician or contact a domestic violence resource for help.
