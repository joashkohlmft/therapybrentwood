Open Marriage & Non-Monogamy April 2026 ⏱ 12 min read

Open Marriage: it was never about the structure, it was always about the bond

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·The useful question is not whether opening a relationship is right or wrong. It is whether it strengthens or threatens the felt security of your bond. The way I work does not moralize the arrangement; it tends the bond inside it.
  • ·Consensual non-monogamy is common, about one in five adults have tried some form, and a large recent analysis found couples in it report satisfaction and commitment on par with monogamous couples. Monogamy is not the automatically healthier structure.
  • ·But that data comes mostly from people already thriving in it, so it describes couples who succeeded at non-monogamy, not couples deciding whether to try. "Just as satisfied" is a real finding, not a prediction that opening up will go well for you.
  • ·Opening from strength, with a secure bond and a real mutual yes, can work. Opening to fix disconnection, or with one partner quietly agreeing out of fear, usually backfires. Openness cannot supply a safety that is not already there.
  • ·Jealousy is a signal, not a verdict; and what separates transparent non-monogamy from an affair is consent and honesty, not the presence of another person.

The question worth asking

Most conversations about open marriage get stuck on the wrong question. Is it right or wrong. Is it natural. Is it just a slow road to divorce. Part of what makes it so charged is that non-monogamy is not the normative, assumed way to have a relationship, so it still carries real social, religious, and cultural stigma, and simply disclosing it to family, friends, or a faith community can cost a couple something. People arrive already braced for a verdict, from a culture, a family, sometimes a faith, and they expect a therapist to hand down one more.

I do not work that way, and the model I practice does not either. The question I actually find useful is not whether opening a relationship is right or wrong. It is more honest: does this strengthen the felt security of the bond between you, or does it threaten it. That is the compass. Whatever a couple decides flows from that, plus a willingness to keep telling each other how they really feel about what is happening. I do not have an agenda about the shape of your relationship. My whole interest is in the security of the bond living inside whatever shape you choose.

Refusing to hand down a verdict is not a dodge. It is where the real work starts, because the moment the question becomes the bond rather than the ruling, most of the fear and shame around the topic loses its grip.

More common than you think, and what the research really says

Part of what makes this so loaded is the sense that wanting it, or trying it, makes you an outlier or a cautionary tale. The numbers say otherwise. Something like one in five adults report having been in some form of consensual non-monogamy at some point in their lives. It is not a fringe. It runs quietly through ordinary neighborhoods and school pickup lines and long, stable relationships.

And the honest read of the data is more reassuring than the culture assumes. In a recent analysis of more than thirty studies and over twenty-four thousand people, researchers found no meaningful difference in relationship satisfaction or sexual satisfaction between monogamous and consensually non-monogamous couples, and that held for gay and straight couples alike and across different kinds of arrangements. So the belief that monogamy is simply the healthier, more loving structure does not hold up. One likely reason is telling: infidelity is one of the great satisfaction-killers in monogamous relationships, and the explicit, agreed-upon nature of an open arrangement takes that particular landmine off the board.

Where that data comes from changes what it means. Almost all of that reassuring research is gathered from people who are already successfully non-monogamous, many of whom were oriented that way from early on, or who opened from a place of real strength. The studies, by their nature, recruit people who are into it and doing well. It is a little like surveying marathon finishers about whether running a marathon feels good. The answers come back warm, and they tell you almost nothing about how the race would go for someone who has not trained.

So the two claims really are different, and the difference is the whole thing. “Couples in consensual non-monogamy are just as satisfied” is a true statement about outcomes for people already living it well; it is worth saying out loud, because it should set down the shame and the fear that you are reaching for something broken. “Opening up will go well for you” is a prediction about your relationship in particular, and the research does not actually make it, because it never studied couples standing where you are standing, at the deciding line rather than years into a version that already worked. Hold the first claim and let it quiet the shame. Just do not let it harden into the second, because that quiet slide, from “other couples do fine” to “so we will too,” is exactly where people get hurt.

“Couples in open relationships are just as satisfied” is true. It is not the same sentence as “opening up will go well for you.”

What the bond actually runs on

To talk about this well, you have to know what any close relationship runs on underneath, because it is the same engine whether a couple is monogamous or open. We are wired for connection. Underneath the logistics and the sex and the day-to-day, every securely bonded couple is quietly answering three questions for each other: are you accessible to me, are you responsive to me, are you engaged with me. Can I reach you, will you turn toward me, are you really here.

When the answer is yes, the bond feels safe, and safety is not the enemy of passion. It is the ground passion grows in. In the research I lean on, adult love runs on attachment, caregiving, and desire together, and the attachment bond is the one that holds up the other two (Johnson & Zuccarini, 2010). Emotional safety is the foundation openness is built on, not its opposite. Which matters here because opening a relationship does not remove those three questions. It multiplies the moments when they get asked. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is the reason the bond deserves real attention when you do.

Jealousy is not the enemy

If there is one thing I most want to hand a couple exploring this, it is a completely different relationship with jealousy. We treat jealousy as a character flaw, a sign of insecurity, a failure of enlightenment, something to be ashamed of and willpower away. In the way I understand emotions, it is none of those things. Jealousy belongs to a small family of feelings, along with hurt, that evolved specifically to guard our closest bonds. It is not noise. It is data. When jealousy flares, it is your attachment system doing exactly its job, sending up a flare that says something about the bond needs attention right now.

So we do not shame it and we do not obey it blindly. We get curious about it. Underneath the spike of jealousy, when you slow it all the way down, is almost always a tender and legitimate attachment question: am I still special to you. Am I still chosen. Am I still safe here. Those are not immature questions. They are the deepest ones we have. And they can be answered, directly, by a partner who understands that the flare was never an accusation but a reach. Worked this way, jealousy stops being the thing that ends open relationships and becomes one of the places they grow the most.

Transparent is the whole difference

People often assume the line between an open marriage and an affair is the third person. It is not. The third person is present in both. The line is somewhere else entirely, and it is worth naming precisely, because it is the thing that makes one of these a betrayal and the other a shared choice.

What wounds in an affair is not fundamentally that a partner had sex with someone. It is that the felt security of the bond was broken, in secret, without consent. The injury is the secrecy and the shattered trust, and it lands with the force of a real trauma. Transparent non-monogamy is built on the exact opposite of that. It runs on consent, on honesty, and on agreements the two of you author together and can revisit as you go. When both partners know, both agreed, and both keep talking openly about how it is actually landing, you are not in the territory of betrayal at all. You are in a different structure that a couple built on purpose, together, in the light.

Which is also why a broken agreement inside an open relationship is a real injury, not a technicality. The safety was never about the absence of other people. It was about the promises you made to each other and kept. That is what a partner is trusting. (I have written about the anatomy of that kind of wound, and its repair, in emotional and sexual affairs and what real repair asks for.)

Opening from strength, not to fix it

Here is the distinction that matters more than any other, and the one I would want a couple to hear straight before they change anything.

There is a real difference between opening a relationship from strength and opening it to fix something, and the research is unusually clear about it. When couples who opened up were studied, they turned out to have been more satisfied than average to begin with. They opened from strength, not from distress. And opening raised their sexual satisfaction while leaving their relationship satisfaction essentially unchanged. Sit with that, because it is the whole thing: opening a relationship does not rescue a struggling one. It cannot. In the model I work from, you cannot outsource a safe haven that is not there, and adding another person to a bond that already feels shaky usually just multiplies the shakiness.

So the high-risk version of this has a recognizable shape, and I will name it plainly, because a couple living inside it deserves the truth. It tends to be one partner's idea more than a shared discovery. It tends to be reached for to solve something already hurting, boredom, a stalled sexual connection, a creeping distance. And it tends to be agreed to under some quiet attachment threat, a yes that is really a frightened no, given to keep from losing the other person. When those three are in the room together, opening up is genuinely hard and often makes things worse, not better.

That last one deserves its own word, because it is the crux. Consent only means something when it is freely given. Openness one partner accepts because they are afraid of losing the other is not a shared choice; it is accommodation, and it plants a hidden injury that surfaces later as resentment or withdrawal. From the sexual side, the same danger looks like going along with something against your own quiet no, which does not build desire, it slowly trains it away. None of this is a reason for the more hesitant partner to be pushed, and none of it is a reason for the more eager one to feel ashamed of wanting something. It is a reason to slow all the way down and get honest together, so that whatever you choose is genuinely two people choosing it.

And the hopeful other side is just as real. When a couple has a secure bond, a true mutual yes, and they are opening from strength rather than to repair a rupture, that same research says it can work, and the satisfaction holds. The whole of what I do is help build exactly that precondition, a bond secure enough and honest enough to carry it. That, far more than the structure itself, is what decides which of these two stories a couple ends up living.

How these relationships grow

This is not only about avoiding harm. Non-monogamy done well asks a couple to get remarkably good at the very things that make any relationship thrive, which is why so many couples find the conversation itself changes them. A few of the moves that help, and that I work on with couples in any structure:

Answer the longing, not the logistics. So much of this can collapse into negotiating rules, how often, with whom, what exactly counts. Those matter, but the real work is underneath the rules, in each partner hearing what the other is actually longing for and afraid of, and responding to that.

Comfort first, then closeness. When something stings, the reach for reassurance has to be met before anything warmer can come back online. Safety first. Trying to leap ahead while there is still fire in the room rarely works.

Give from we, not from obligation. There is a lovely finding that when partners give to each other from a genuine “I want to, because this is good for us” place, their own warmth toward each other tends to grow, while giving from duty quietly drains it. That is as true of an open relationship as any other.

Invite, do not accuse. The same need lands completely differently depending on its shape. “You never make me feel chosen” closes a partner down. “I want to feel chosen by you, especially now” opens them up. It is the same longing, in fear-form or in reaching-form, and the reaching form is the one that gets answered.

None of this is unique to non-monogamy. It is just that non-monogamy asks for it more often, and more honestly, than a relationship coasting on default assumptions ever has to. Couples who take it on frequently tell me they have never communicated so deeply in their lives.

An honest, hopeful close

I will not pretend it asks nothing of you. Inviting another person into the intimate life of a bond does ping the attachment system, by design; that is simply how we are built. But that is not a verdict against it. It is the reason it rewards couples who keep tending the bond rather than assuming it will tend itself. Every real change in a relationship, a new baby, a move, a shift like this one, is a kind of turning point where the bond has to be reshaped and renewed. It does not have to break. Very often it deepens.

If you are exploring this, from curiosity or from a decision already half-made, you do not need a therapist to approve or disapprove of your arrangement. You need one who can help you keep the bond between you accessible, responsive, and secure while you figure out how you want to love. That is the whole of the work I do in couples therapy, built on Emotionally Focused Therapy, and I am glad to do it without judgment, for whatever shape your relationship is taking.

You do not need a therapist to approve or disapprove of how you structure your relationship. You need one who helps keep the bond inside it secure, whatever shape you choose.

Common questions

Is an open marriage healthy?

It can be, and so can a monogamous one. A large 2025 meta-analysis of dozens of studies and more than twenty-four thousand people found no significant difference in relationship or sexual satisfaction between monogamous and consensually non-monogamous couples, so monogamy is not automatically the healthier structure. But that research is drawn largely from people already thriving in non-monogamy, so it does not mean opening up will go well for any given couple. What predicts a healthy relationship is the security of the bond, not the structure.

Does wanting an open relationship mean something is wrong with our marriage?

Not necessarily, and it is worth getting curious about what the wish is for. Opening from a secure, satisfied place is very different from opening to fix boredom, distance, or a stalled sex life. The research suggests couples who open successfully were already doing well and opened from strength; opening to repair a strained bond rarely works, because adding another person cannot supply a safety that is not already there.

Is jealousy a sign an open relationship won't work?

No. Jealousy is not a character flaw or a verdict on the arrangement; it is an attachment signal, information that something about the bond needs attention. Underneath a spike of jealousy is almost always a tender question, am I still special, still chosen, still safe with you, and those questions can be answered directly by a partner. Couples who treat jealousy as data to get curious about, rather than something to be ashamed of, tend to do far better than those who try to suppress it.

What is the difference between an open marriage and an affair?

Consent, honesty, and shared agreements, not the presence of another person. What wounds in an affair is not fundamentally the sex; it is that the felt security of the bond was broken in secret, without agreement. Transparent non-monogamy is the opposite: both partners know, both agreed, and both keep talking openly about how it is landing. A broken agreement inside an open relationship, by contrast, becomes a real attachment injury on the same axis as an affair.

How do we open our relationship without hurting it?

Slowly, and from a secure place. Make sure the yes is real on both sides and not one partner accommodating out of fear. Build agreements together, in specifics, and treat them as revisitable as reality teaches you things. Keep full honesty rather than drifting into not-telling to protect each other. And keep tending the bond between you, staying accessible, responsive, and engaged, so it stays the secure base while other connections exist.

Can an open marriage make a relationship stronger?

For couples who come to it from strength, often yes. Doing non-monogamy well asks a couple to get remarkably good at honesty, at naming needs and fears, and at answering each other's longings rather than negotiating rules, and those are the very skills that deepen any bond. Many couples say the conversation itself brought them closer than they had been in years. It is not a magic upgrade, but it is not a downgrade either.

What if one of us wants it and the other doesn't?

That gap is the single most important thing to slow down for. Consent given because you are afraid of losing your partner is not a shared choice; it is accommodation, and it plants a hidden hurt that surfaces later as resentment or withdrawal. The answer is not for the hesitant partner to be pushed or the eager one to feel ashamed of wanting something. It is to get underneath both positions, the wish and the fear, until whatever you choose is genuinely two people choosing it.

Do open relationships actually last?

There is no good evidence that one structure lasts better than the other, and the research does not settle it either way. Longevity tracks the security of the bond and the honesty of the agreements, the same things that hold any relationship together.
