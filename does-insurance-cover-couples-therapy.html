Why I Don't Take Insurance for Couples Therapy (And Why That's Better for Your Relationship)

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

If you have landed here, there is a good chance you have already made a few phone calls, opened your insurance app, and come away confused.

You are trying to do the responsible thing. You are trying to get help for your relationship without being reckless about money. That instinct is a good one, and I want to honor it by giving you a straight answer instead of the runaround most people get.

So here it is, plainly. Most health insurance does not cover couples therapy, and I have made the deliberate choice not to bill insurance for it. That is not a loophole or an oversight on my part. It is a decision I made on purpose, and once you understand why, I think you will see that it protects the quality of the work rather than getting in the way of it.

Let me explain what is actually happening underneath all that confusion.

In brief

  • ·Insurance pays to treat a diagnosed illness in one person. Couples therapy treats the relationship, which is not an illness, so most plans will not cover it.
  • ·The common workaround is to diagnose one partner as the patient and bill it as individual therapy. I am not willing to do that to your record or your care.
  • ·Even the honest route, relationship (Z) codes, is rarely reimbursed, often capped at a few sessions, or rejected outright.
  • ·Working out of network keeps your care honest and undiluted. Superbills, HSA/FSA funds, and possible out-of-network reimbursement help with the cost.

The short version, for anyone in a hurry

Insurance pays for the treatment of a diagnosed medical illness in one identified person. Real couples therapy does not treat one person's illness. It treats the relationship between two people. There is no medical diagnosis for "we keep having the same fight" or "we have grown distant," because those are not illnesses. They are human, painful, and worth addressing, but they are not what the insurance system was built to pay for.

That single mismatch explains almost everything else on this page.

Why insurance and couples therapy do not fit together

Health insurance runs on what is called the medical model. To pay a claim, an insurer needs three things: one identified patient, a diagnosable mental health condition from the official manual, and evidence that the treatment is "medically necessary" to treat that condition.

Couples therapy breaks all three at once. There is no single patient, because the client is the relationship, not either individual. There is no qualifying diagnosis, because relationship distress is not a disorder anyone has. And the work is not aimed at treating a sickness, it is aimed at repairing a bond. From the insurer's point of view, none of that is reimbursable, no matter how much the two of you are hurting.

Diagram: What Insurance Requires vs. What Couples Therapy Is

This is why you will hear, over and over, that couples therapy "is not covered." It is not that insurers think your relationship does not matter. It is that their entire system is designed around treating one sick person, and a relationship is not a person.

The workaround, and why I will not use it

Here is the part almost no one tells you. Some therapists who take insurance for couples work get around the problem by doing something I am not willing to do. To make the claim payable, one partner has to be labeled the patient and given a mental health diagnosis, something like depression or an anxiety disorder. The sessions are then billed as that person's individual treatment, even though both of you are in the room.

I want you to sit with what that actually means. For insurance to pay, one of you has to be designated the sick one. That diagnosis goes into your permanent medical record. It can follow you into future health coverage, life insurance applications, and security clearances. And clinically, it quietly reframes the entire endeavor: the therapy is now officially about treating that person's disorder, not about the two of you and the pattern you are caught in together.

The therapists who work this way are usually generalists, skilled individual clinicians who see couples occasionally, rather than specialists trained specifically in a couples model. So what often happens is exactly what the billing code implies: you get individual therapy with your partner sitting nearby, rather than genuine couples therapy that works on the relationship itself.

I am not willing to put a false or inflated diagnosis on either of you to make a claim go through. And I am not willing to call something couples therapy when the paperwork says it is one person's individual treatment. You deserve better than a workaround.

The honest alternative, and why the system still breaks it

To be fair, there are excellent, ethical couple therapists who refuse the diagnose-one-partner route and do the honest thing instead. Rather than labeling one of you as sick, they bill using what are called Z codes, the relationship codes that name the actual situation, something like "relationship distress with a partner." No false diagnosis. No pathologizing. Just an accurate description of what the work really is.

I have real respect for the therapists who bill this way. The problem is that the system rarely lets it work.

Very few insurers reimburse Z codes at all. Among the ones that do, coverage is usually capped at a small handful of sessions, which means couples get cut off right around the point where the real work is only beginning, left hanging just as things start to move. Other insurers reject the Z code outright, and then both therapist and clients are buried in appeals and paperwork for care that was never going to be covered in the first place, time and energy pulled away from the actual therapy.

That leaves the honest therapist with only one way to keep serving those clients: absorb the loss and take a steep pay cut, session after session. Some do it, for a while, and I admire them for it. But it is not sustainable. And I will be honest with you: it is not something I can do and still keep this practice open for the long term.

So I made a cleaner choice. I stay out of network, keep the work honest and undiluted, and tell you the cost plainly up front, rather than promising coverage the system was never really going to provide.

Why I chose to work outside insurance

When I set up my practice, I had a choice. I could contract with insurance panels, accept their rates and their rules, and do the kind of work those rules allow. Or I could stay out of network and protect the quality and honesty of what I offer. I chose the second, and I want to be transparent about the reasons rather than hide behind "we just don't take insurance."

Why I stay out of network
No forced diagnosis.

Neither of you gets pathologized so a claim can be paid. Your records stay clean. Our work stays honest about what it actually is.

The relationship stays the client.

Insurance would require me to treat one of you. My training is in treating the bond between you. I am not willing to distort the work to fit a billing code.

Depth over volume.

Insurance rates for this work are low relative to the specialized training it requires, which pushes many in-network clinicians toward short, high-volume caseloads. Working outside that system lets me keep a small caseload and give each couple real attention, including longer sessions and intensive formats when that is what a couple needs.

Genuine specialization.

I am among the more highly trained couple therapists you will find, especially for complex situations, and I have invested years in that training. Staying out of network is part of what makes it possible to keep doing this work at that level.

None of this is a knock on colleagues who take insurance. For many people and many kinds of care, in-network therapy is a real gift and the right choice. I am simply telling you honestly why, for this particular work, I made a different one.

Being licensed to see couples is not the same as being trained for it

There is one more thing worth saying plainly, because it sits underneath everything above.

Any licensed therapist is permitted to see couples. Far fewer are actually trained to do it well. Couples work is its own discipline, with its own models, its own research, and its own particular demands, and it is genuinely hard. Sitting in a room with two activated nervous systems and a cycle running live between them is not the same skill as helping one person one on one.

We understand this everywhere else. We would never assume that a therapist licensed to work with children automatically knows how to do play therapy with a four year old. We know play therapy is a specialization that takes its own training and years to do well. Yet somehow, when it comes to couples, we expect any licensed clinician to simply know how. Why?

A relationship in distress is not an easier case than a child in distress. If anything it is harder. It deserves a therapist who chose this work on purpose and trained for it specifically.

What this means for you, practically

Working with an out-of-network therapist does not mean you are on your own financially. Here is how people actually manage it.

Superbills and out-of-network reimbursement. If your plan has out-of-network benefits, I can provide a superbill, an itemized receipt you submit to your insurer for partial reimbursement. Many PPO plans reimburse a meaningful percentage of each session once you have met your out-of-network deductible. It is worth calling your insurer and asking two questions: "Do I have out-of-network outpatient mental health benefits?" and "What is my out-of-network deductible and reimbursement rate?" Please know that reimbursement is never guaranteed, and for couples work the same diagnosis limitations can apply, so treat anything you get back as a bonus rather than a plan.

HSA and FSA funds. Therapy is a qualifying medical expense, so you can typically pay with a Health Savings Account or Flexible Spending Account card. That lets you use pre-tax dollars, which softens the real cost.

Clear, predictable pricing. Because I am not billing insurance, there are no surprise codes or denied claims months later. You know exactly what each session costs before we begin, the same flat rate for individuals and couples, and you are entitled to a Good Faith Estimate under the federal No Surprises Act. The full breakdown of fees and payment sits on the FAQ page.

What you are actually paying for

I want to name this directly, because "out of network" can sound like a euphemism for "expensive."

You are paying for a therapist who is triple-certified in Emotionally Focused Therapy, the couples model with the strongest research base for actually changing the pattern you are stuck in. You are paying for someone who will treat your relationship as the client, keep your records honest, and give you real time and attention rather than a rushed slot between fifteen other claims. And you are paying for work that is designed to be effective enough that you do not need it forever.

Good couples therapy is not meant to be a permanent expense. It is meant to change something, so that reaching for each other works again, and then to end. Measured that way, I think it is one of the more worthwhile things a couple can invest in. If you want to understand what makes this approach different, EFT compared with the Gottman Method lays out the evidence, and the couples therapy page walks through how the work actually goes.

If cost is genuinely a barrier

I never want money to be the only reason a couple stays stuck. If the fee is out of reach, tell me. I can point you toward lower-cost options, including training clinics and in-network generalists, and I would rather help you find good care somewhere than have you go without. There is no pressure and no judgment in that conversation.

Common questions

Does insurance cover couples therapy?

Usually not. Insurance pays for the treatment of a diagnosed medical condition in one identified person, and couples therapy treats the relationship rather than one person's illness. There is no medical diagnosis for relationship distress, so most plans will not cover true couples therapy.

Is marriage counseling covered by insurance?

Generally no, for the same reason. It may be partially covered only when one partner has a qualifying mental health diagnosis and the sessions are billed as that person's individual treatment, which changes what the therapy actually is.

Why don't you take insurance for couples therapy?

Because billing insurance would require labeling one partner as the patient, putting a mental health diagnosis on their permanent record, and reframing the work as individual treatment rather than couples therapy. I am not willing to do either. Staying out of network lets me keep your records honest and treat the relationship as the client.

Can couples therapy be billed with a relationship (Z) code instead of a diagnosis?

Some ethical therapists do exactly this, using Z codes that name relationship distress honestly rather than diagnosing one partner. The trouble is that very few insurers reimburse Z codes, and when they do it is often capped at a few sessions or rejected outright, which leaves couples cut off early or buried in paperwork. It is the honest route, but the system rarely makes it workable.

Can I use my HSA or FSA to pay for couples therapy?

Yes. Therapy is a qualifying medical expense, so in most cases you can pay with your HSA or FSA card and use pre-tax dollars.

What is a superbill, and can I get reimbursed?

A superbill is an itemized receipt you submit to your insurer to request partial reimbursement under any out-of-network benefits you have. If your plan includes out-of-network outpatient mental health coverage, you may be reimbursed a portion of each session after meeting your deductible. Reimbursement is never guaranteed, so treat it as a possible bonus.

How much does couples therapy cost without insurance?

You will know the exact fee before we begin, with no surprise billing. Fees are flat and transparent, the same rate for individuals and couples, and HSA and FSA funds and possible out-of-network reimbursement can offset the cost. See the full fee breakdown.
