Why Trauma Makes You More Reactive With Your Partner

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Somewhere along the way, someone probably handed you a verdict. You’re too sensitive. You overreact. It was just a comment. Maybe it came from your partner, mid-fight. Maybe it came from a parent, decades ago. Maybe by now you have taken over the job and say it to yourself.

If that inner voice is loud, here is something worth knowing about it: even the critic was once protection. For many people, that harsh inner voice earned its place by predicting attacks before they arrived, by keeping you small enough, quiet enough, careful enough to be safe. In therapy I do not try to delete it, and you do not need to hate it. It was a bodyguard. It is just still working a job that may have ended years ago.

I want to offer you a different explanation, one that matches what I actually see in my office. The size of your reaction is not a measure of how unreasonable you are. It is a rough map of the size of what got touched. And what got touched has a history.

In brief

  • ·Every nervous system has a threshold, a line past which a conversation becomes a threat. Trauma, in all its forms, lowers that line.
  • ·This includes quiet forms most people never call trauma: homes where anger meant danger, mistakes met contempt, or love was tied to performance.
  • ·The size of a reaction maps to the size of what got touched, not the size of the trigger. “It was just a comment” never de-escalates anything, because to the triggered body, it was not.
  • ·“Be less sensitive” is not a treatment plan. Thresholds move through new experiences of being met differently, not through willpower.
  • ·The line between individual trauma and relationship problems is blurrier than most couples hope. Good therapy honors both.

The Threshold

Every nervous system has a threshold, a line past which it stops conversing and starts surviving. What trauma does, in all its forms, is lower that line.

Some of the couples I work with carry what you might call capital-T trauma: abuse, violence, a parent’s addiction, combat, sexual assault, a previous relationship that was frightening to be in. If that is you, the connection is probably not news. You already know your body keeps an archive, and that certain tones of voice or facial expressions can pull a file from it without your permission.

But I want to speak just as directly to the couples who would never use the word trauma about themselves, because in my experience they are the majority, and they are often the most confused about why their fights are so big. Maybe nothing terrible ever happened to you. But perhaps you grew up in a home where anger meant someone was about to get hurt, or where mistakes were met with contempt, or where the reliable response to your distress was “stop crying or I’ll give you something to cry about.” Perhaps love was present but conditional, tied to performance, or grades, or keeping the peace. Researchers sometimes call these attachment injuries or small-t trauma. Your body does not care what they are called. It learned, early and thoroughly, what closeness can cost, and it has been guarding the door ever since.

Sue Johnson and Leanne Campbell, in their work on trauma-focused EFT, describe these survival responses as exactly that: not personality flaws but distortions of natural, positive coping, answers to impossible situations. And they name the cruelest feature of the design. The responses become habitual and automatic until they form what they call a trauma trap: a self-perpetuating pattern of triggers and coping that keeps the emotional problem alive. Read that again slowly, because it explains years of stuckness. The coping that protects you from the trigger is the very thing that prevents the new experience that would update it. You duck before the blow that is not coming anymore, so your body never gets to learn that it stopped coming. That is why willpower alone cannot move the threshold. The trap was built, precisely, to hold it in place.

What a Low Threshold Looks Like on a Tuesday Night

Here is the practical consequence. A partner with an untouched history might hear “you forgot to run the dishwasher” as information. A partner whose history taught them that failure means rejection hears the same sentence and their body answers a different question entirely: am I about to be found unworthy again? The words were small. What the words landed on was not.

There is a hallmark of trauma at work here that almost nobody explains to the people living it: when an old echo fires, coping arrives before feeling. The blame, the shutdown, the freeze, the frantic fixing shows up in the split second before the hurt itself is even consciously felt. This is why triggered people so often say, bewildered and ashamed, “I don’t know why I did that.” They are telling the truth. The reaction outran their awareness, because that is what it was built to do. In EFT we describe that coping as obsolete, not pathological. It solved a real problem once, and solved it well. It is simply out of date.

Once the threshold is crossed, the body reaches for its survival responses, fight, flight, freeze, or fawn, and if both partners cross it, their responses start triggering each other. I have written about that whole sequence in the other two articles in this series, starting here. What I want to hold onto in this article is the moment just before: the crossing itself, and why one partner crosses so much earlier than the other.

This is also why I am careful with couples who have been told, or have told each other, “you’re too sensitive.” Sensitivity is not the problem. The threshold is where the history put it.

Telling someone to raise their own threshold by willpower is like telling someone to be taller.

Is This Trauma Work or Couples Work?

Couples often ask me some version of “how much of this is my partner’s trauma and how much is us?” It is a fair question, and the honest answer is that the line is blurrier than either partner usually hopes.

A history of trauma lives in an individual body, but it gets triggered, soothed, or worsened inside the relationship. Sometimes couples therapy is exactly the place that healing happens, because a partner who can stay present while your oldest alarm fires is one of the most powerful correctives there is. Sometimes an individual’s trauma responses are intense enough that focused individual work, such as Brainspotting or individual EFT, needs to run alongside or ahead of the couples work. In my practice I do both, and part of my job in the first sessions is helping you figure out which sequence fits your situation rather than forcing one.

There is a paradox at the center of this question, and naming it helps couples stop blaming themselves for how hard this is. To heal an attachment wound, the survivor has to risk vulnerability with the very person who has hurt them, or who resembles the people who did. That is why the work must move slowly, in half-steps, and why “just open up to your partner” is cruel advice. It is also why the payoff is unmatched. When it works, the corrective experience does not happen in theory or in a workbook. It happens with your actual attachment figure, in your actual kitchen.

What I will say plainly: “go fix your trauma first and then come back” is rarely the right answer on its own, and neither is pretending the history is not in the room. The two are woven together. The work has to honor both.

How a Threshold Actually Moves

If willpower does not move the line, what does? New experience, of a specific kind.

The alarm was installed by moments where reaching for someone ended badly: with anger, contempt, absence, or conditions. It gets updated by moments that run the old sequence with a new ending. You show the tender thing, the fear under the anger, the shame under the silence, and your partner does not attack, leave, or go blank. They stay. They come closer. The body registers something it may never have registered before: that is not what happens anymore.

This is the heart of the work I do in Emotionally Focused Therapy, and I want to be honest about it: these are not conversations couples typically reach on their own, precisely because both alarms are wired to prevent them. That is not a failure of love or effort. It is what the alarms are for. When the trauma is stuck deeper in the body, where words do not quite reach it, I pair this work with Brainspotting, which works with the brain’s own processing rather than around it.

People sometimes carry a fear into this work that they rarely say out loud: will therapy make me relive it? That fear deserves a direct answer. The aim of trauma work is not recovery of the memory. It is recovering the self: finally feeling what was not okay to feel at the time, and, this was Bowlby’s insight, not encountering that vulnerability alone this time. Therapy does not change what happened to you. It changes what what-happened keeps doing to you. It is never too late to add the missing ingredient.

The threshold does not leap. It moves the way it was set: one experience at a time. But I have watched partners who flinched at a raised eyebrow learn to stay present through a real disagreement, and I have watched the partner who caused the flinching learn they were never the whole cause. Both of those are forms of healing, and they tend to arrive together.

Questions Couples Ask Me About Trauma and Reactivity

Why does my partner explode over things that seem tiny to me?

Because the thing that seems tiny to you is landing on something that is not tiny to them. The size of a reaction is a rough map of the size of what got touched, not the size of the trigger. This does not make every reaction acceptable, and it does not make you responsible for their history. It does mean that “it was just a comment” will never de-escalate anything, because to their body, it was not.

Do I have trauma even if nothing terrible ever happened to me?

Possibly, in the sense that matters here. The nervous system is shaped not only by dramatic events but by the emotional climate it grew up in: what happened when you cried, failed, got angry, or needed something. If the answer was reliably painful, your body learned from it, whatever word you use for that learning.

Is it possible to heal this, or are we permanently broken?

I have sat with couples who were certain they were beyond help and watched them find their way back to each other. I have also seen how much harder the road is when patterns have run unchallenged for decades, or when one partner has already left the relationship internally. So I will not hand you an easy yes. What I can tell you is that trauma-driven reactivity is among the things EFT was specifically built for, and the research behind it is as strong as anything we have in couples therapy. Being stuck in a brutal cycle is not the same thing as being broken. Two honest expectation-setters, though. With trauma in the room, progress is rarely a smooth line: expect recurring storms and deepening closeness to arrive together, where other couples settle more steadily. And a good session is not always one you leave smiling. Sometimes it is simply one where each of you reached for the other and shared what was available that day. Couples who are braced for that reality quit far less often, and quitting early is the main way this work fails.

Should the traumatized partner do individual therapy before couples therapy?

Sometimes, but less often than people assume, and rarely as an either-or. Trauma gets triggered in the relationship, which means the relationship is often the most powerful place to heal it, provided the therapy makes it safe enough. In a first session I help couples work out the right sequence for their situation, which may be couples work alone, individual work alongside, or a season of one before the other. It is worth knowing that “treat the trauma first” is well enough recognized as a dead end that EFT Trainers have named it among the classic traps of couples therapy: logic that sounds careful but pulls the work away from the place where the trauma actually gets triggered, which is so often the place it must be healed.

If Someone Called You Too Sensitive

You were not weak for flinching, and you are not weak now for reacting. Your body learned its lessons in places where the lessons were real. The goal was never to need nothing. It is to finally have your reach for another person land somewhere safe.

And somewhere in your story, I would bet on it, there are moments that prove the opposite of weakness. The child who kicked. The one who hid a sibling, who barricaded a door, who found somewhere else to be until the yelling stopped. The adult who chose to leave. In trauma work we go looking for those moments deliberately, because they tell a truth the shame has been hiding. There is a line from this work that I have never forgotten, spoken to a client who carried decades of it: you could not win the war, but you never surrendered. Your reactions today, the flinching, the guarding, the scanning, are not evidence that you are broken. They are evidence that you never stopped fighting to stay safe. That fighter is who walks into therapy. We just give them a different war to win.
