Cross-Cultural & Multicultural Counseling · Brentwood & Nashville, TN

Therapy that holds the whole of who you are

You carry more than most people see. A therapist who has lived across worlds, not just studied them, understands what it costs to belong to more than one.

What is cross-cultural counseling?

Cross-cultural counseling is therapy that holds the full complexity of who you are, including the cultural frameworks, family expectations, immigration experiences, and identity questions that shape your emotional world and your relationships. I offer cross-cultural counseling for Asian Americans, immigrant couples, multicultural couples, and third-culture individuals throughout Tennessee via telehealth, bringing lived cross-cultural experience to the work.

There is a particular kind of loneliness that comes from living between cultures. You speak multiple languages, not just linguistically, but emotionally. You know what it is to perform one version of yourself at work and another at home. To love someone who grew up in a completely different world and find that the gap between you is not just personal, but cultural, structural, generational.

And you may have tried to explain this to a therapist before, only to spend the session educating them about your background rather than doing the actual work.

That is not this.

Lived experience, not just cultural competence training

I was born in Malaysia, into a Chinese family navigating Confucian expectations, filial piety, and the particular silence around emotional need that many Asian families carry as both protection and inheritance. I have sat where you are sitting, in the space between what is expected and what is felt, between the self that is presented and the self that is hidden.

Before I became a therapist, I spent years working as a telecommunications engineer across Southeast Asia, Australia, New Zealand, and South Asia, living out of suitcases, crossing cultural borders constantly, learning that what looks like indifference in one culture is dignity in another, that what reads as aggression in one context is desperation in another. I have navigated professional systems, personal relationships, and intimate loss across multiple cultural frameworks.

This is not a credential. It is a different kind of presence in the room.

Cultural humility is not about knowing everything about every culture. It is about genuine curiosity, the readiness to hear, engage, and explore without assuming. To ask, and then ask again.

Who do I work with in cross-cultural counseling?

Asian Americans & the diaspora

First-generation, 1.5-generation, and second-generation Asian Americans navigating the space between inherited family systems and the American cultural context. The unspoken expectations. The difficulty of saying "I need help" in a family where help was always given, never asked for.

Immigrant couples & families

Couples who arrived together or found each other here, and now find that the cultural ground beneath each of them is shifting differently. The strain of assimilation on a marriage. The way immigration changes not just where you live, but who you are allowed to be.

Cross-cultural couples

Partners from different cultural, ethnic, or racial backgrounds who love each other and sometimes find that love is not sufficient protection against the friction of genuinely different worlds. Different expectations about family, intimacy, emotional expression, silence, and time.

Third-culture individuals

People who grew up between cultures and have never felt fully at home in any of them. Who code-switch as naturally as breathing, and who carry a particular kind of grief at the things that were lost in translation, including sometimes, parts of themselves.

Professionals navigating cross-cultural workplaces

High-achieving professionals, often Asian or immigrant background, navigating predominantly White professional spaces. The exhaustion of code-switching at work and at home. The way the hypervigilance required for professional survival follows you into intimacy.

LGBTQ+ individuals & couples

LGBTQ+ people of color, queer immigrants, and same-sex couples navigating the intersection of sexual identity and cultural background. The particular weight of being doubly outside, in a culture that may not accept your queerness, and in a queer community that may not fully see your ethnicity. This work is done with full affirmation and without assumption.

Interracial & interfaith couples

Couples whose racial, ethnic, or religious differences surface not just in conflict, but in the way they each make meaning of the world. The way race and faith shape not just belief, but attachment, what safety feels like, what love looks like, what repair requires.

Why is EFT particularly well-suited to cross-cultural therapy?

Most Western therapeutic models were developed from research on White, middle-class populations. Their assumptions about emotional expression, family structure, and what "healthy" looks like carry cultural fingerprints that are rarely acknowledged.

EFT is different. It is built on attachment theory, which John Bowlby grounded in the biology of human bonding across all cultures, all species, all of human history. The need for felt safety with those we love is not a Western idea. It is encoded in our nervous systems regardless of where we were born, what language we speak, or how our families expressed closeness.

Attachment needs are universal. Their expression is cultural. The Asian partner who goes quiet under pressure is not necessarily withdrawing in the way a Western attachment model assumes. The immigrant who overworks may be expressing love in the only language their family ever used. EFT's task is to access the underlying need, and that task is the same across every cultural container.

As Dr. Paul Guillory's groundbreaking work on EFT with African American couples demonstrates, culturally attuned EFT requires the therapist to hold both the universal emotional reality and the specific cultural context simultaneously. Race-based distress, intergenerational trauma, and cultural identity are not peripheral to the therapeutic work, they are woven into the very fabric of how couples reach for each other and what gets in the way.

This is equally true for Asian couples navigating Confucian frameworks of duty and self-effacement, for immigrant couples managing the grief of dislocation, and for cross-cultural couples whose attachment injuries carry the fingerprints of their specific histories.

What does cross-cultural counseling look like in practice?

Cross-cultural counseling works by holding the cultural frame and the emotional experience together, never reducing one to the other. Sessions begin with understanding how each person's cultural background has shaped what they expect from love, what feelings are safe to show, and what they have had to do to belong in their families of origin and their communities. From there, the work goes beneath those cultural patterns to find the attachment need that is trying, imperfectly, to be met. This is where lasting change becomes possible: not in managing cultural difference, but in genuinely reaching each other across it.

Consider a couple I will call Wei and Sarah. Wei grew up in a Chinese family where love was demonstrated through provision and sacrifice, never through words or touch. Emotional directness felt not just uncomfortable but dangerous, a form of imposition. Sarah, whose family was emotionally expressive and direct, experienced Wei's silence as rejection. She pursued. He withdrew. The cycle felt familiar to both of them, but its roots were not visible in the standard pursuer-withdrawer frame.

What was happening underneath was this: Wei's silence was not indifference. It was the most loving thing he knew how to do, to not burden the person he loved with his internal world. And Sarah's pursuit was not aggression. It was terror, the terror of reaching for someone who seemed not to be there.

When Wei finally heard that Sarah's voice getting louder was not criticism but fear, fear that she was losing him, something shifted. And when Sarah finally understood that Wei's silence was not distance but a form of care, she stopped fighting the silence and started moving toward it.

The cultural frame does not explain away the emotion. It reveals what the emotion is actually protecting.

This is the work. Not explaining culture to each other. Not managing difference. But going underneath the cultural pattern to find the attachment need that is trying, imperfectly, to be met.

A note on the Nashville context

Middle Tennessee, Brentwood, Nashville, is home to a rapidly growing Asian American population, significant immigrant communities from across East and Southeast Asia, South Asia, and Latin America, and an increasing number of interracial and cross-cultural couples who often struggle to find therapists who genuinely understand their world.

As a Chinese Malaysian who has lived and worked across multiple continents, been trained in one of the most rigorous clinical traditions in the world, and is a doctoral candidate in Counselor Education and Supervision at the University of Tennessee, I bring both the lived reality and the clinical depth this work requires.

I am also the co-founder of EFT Asians in America, a community built specifically for Asian therapists and clients engaging with EFT, and for the particular ways Asian cultural frameworks intersect with attachment theory.

What I am not

I am not a cultural translator or a diversity checkbox. I am not here to validate any particular cultural framework as superior or to pathologize any particular way of loving. I am not able to fully know your cultural experience from the outside, and I will never pretend to.

What I bring is genuine curiosity, the discipline to stay in the question rather than reaching for premature answers, and the clinical training to help you access what is actually happening in your relationship beneath the cultural surface.

The attachment need underneath your silence is real. The fear underneath your pursuit is real. The love that brought you both here is real.

Those realities cross every cultural border I have ever encountered.
