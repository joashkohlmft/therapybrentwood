When Therapy Words Become Weapons

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Somewhere in the last decade, we all got a vocabulary upgrade.

Words that used to live in a clinician’s notes, gaslighting, narcissist, avoidant, toxic, trauma response, now live in our text threads and our kitchen arguments. On the whole I think that is a good thing. A generation that can name attachment and dysregulation has better tools than the one before it.

But I want to talk about what happens when those tools get pointed at the person we love most. Because in my office I watch it almost every week. Two people who care about each other reach for the sharpest, most clinical-sounding word they know, and instead of bringing them closer to being understood, the word does the opposite. It ends the conversation. It freezes the moment. And it quietly makes both people lonelier than they were before anyone said anything.

This piece is a companion to my article on the knowing gap, because it is really the same problem wearing a more sophisticated outfit. Knowing the name for something is not the same as being close to it. A clinical label, delivered in a hard moment, is often the most convincing way we have of talking about our pain instead of from it.

In brief

  • ·Clinical labels like gaslighting, toxic, and narcissist usually arrive as a verdict, and a verdict is the opposite of a reach for connection.
  • ·The label is almost always a protective move by someone in pain, not a character flaw. It just does not do what the person is hoping it will do.
  • ·Real abuse and coercive control exist and are serious. This article is about two well-meaning partners in a cycle, not about excusing harm.
  • ·Underneath most labels is a softer, truer, and more powerful signal, and moving from the word back to that feeling is what actually rebuilds closeness.

First, a line I will not blur

Before I say a single word about therapy language being overused, I need to draw a hard line, because getting this wrong would do real harm.

⚠ An important distinction

Gaslighting is real. Coercive control is real. Emotional abuse and narcissistic abuse are real, they are serious, and if you are living inside one of them, the answer is not to soften your language so your partner feels more comfortable. This article is not about you, and I do not want it used against you.

What I am writing about here is something different: two well-meaning partners, roughly equal in power, stuck in a painful cycle that neither of them chose and neither of them likes. That is the vast majority of the couples I see. The way you tell the difference is not the intensity of a single fight. It is the pattern. A cycle is a painful dance that both people are caught in and both can step out of. Abuse is a steady, one-directional pattern of control and fear, a slow erosion of your reality, your freedom, and your safety.

If you read the rest of this and something in you says no, this is not a cycle, I am genuinely not safe, please trust that. Talk to a therapist on your own, or reach out to a domestic violence resource. The tools below are for the couple in a cycle. They are not a way to talk anyone out of naming real harm.

With that line drawn, let me tell you what I actually see.

The label is a move, not a diagnosis

When a partner says “you’re gaslighting me” in the middle of a fight, they are almost never conducting a clinical assessment. They are in pain. Something just landed as my reality does not count with you, and the panic of that is enormous. In that panic, reaching for a clinical word does something very human. It hands you a moment of control over a situation that feels completely out of control. It says, at least I know what this is. At least I have a name for it. At least I am not crazy.

I want to be careful here, because this is the exact place where an article like this can turn cruel, and I refuse to write the cruel version. The instinct to grab a label is not a character flaw. It is a survival move. When the ground opens up under you, of course you reach for something solid to hold. A diagnosis feels solid. It makes frightening, formless feelings suddenly nameable and orderly.

So when I hear a clinical word land like a bullet in my office, I do not think this person is manipulative. I think this person is scared, and this is how they are trying to steady themselves. That is the posture the rest of this depends on. The labels are reaching for something real. The problem is not that the person is bad. The problem is that the tool does not do what they are hoping it will do.

Why the label backfires every time

Here is the trouble. A label, no matter how accurate, is a verdict. And a verdict is the opposite of a reach.

When you tell your partner they are a narcissist, you have not opened a door. You have closed one. You have described them as fundamentally, permanently defective, and there is no version of “you are a narcissist” that a partner can hear as an invitation to come closer. What they hear is a sentence, in the legal sense. So they do the only thing a cornered nervous system knows how to do. They defend. They go cold, or they go to war. Either way, they are now busy protecting themselves from the accusation, and they never once hear the thing underneath it, which was almost always some version of are you there for me? do I matter to you?

Before the label, there was one hurt in the room. After it, there are two people alone.

Notice what the word did to the two of you. One of you is hiding behind the accusation. The other is hiding behind the defense against it. The clinical word manufactured the exact distance you were protesting.

And there is a crueler mechanism still. A behavior can change. A move in a dance can be different by the next song. But a narcissist cannot become un-narcissistic by dinner. When you freeze your partner into a diagnosis, you have quietly removed the possibility of repair, because you cannot reconnect with a personality disorder. So the label ends up foreclosing the very hope you were reaching for. You wanted closeness. You reached for the one word guaranteed to make closeness impossible.

Talking about, instead of talking from

This is where it connects back to the knowing gap. Clinical language is the purest form of talking about an experience instead of from it.

“You’re gaslighting me” is a report. It is a cognitive appraisal, an analysis of your partner’s behavior delivered from a safe distance above the pain. What it is not, is the pain itself. Compare it to what is actually true underneath: when you tell me that did not happen, I feel like I am losing my mind, and I feel completely alone in it. One of those sentences is a diagnosis your partner has to defend against. The other is a hand reaching out in the dark. Only one of them can be answered with tenderness.

The heart of it “The label is loud. The reach is clear. In relationships, clear beats loud every time.”

The clinical words are encrypted signals. Your partner cannot decode “you’re avoidant” into anything they can respond to with warmth, because it does not contain the actual message. It contains a category. The raw signal, I get so scared when you go quiet, because it feels like I have lost you, is one your partner can actually receive.

From the label back to the feeling

I built this for the couples I work with, and I want to be honest about how to use it. The right-hand column is not “what you really mean,” because I do not get to tell you what you feel. It is what the word might be protecting. It is an invitation to slow down and check whether there is a softer, truer, and frankly more powerful thing sitting just underneath the clinical one.

The clinical wordWhat it might be protecting
“You’re gaslighting me.”When you say it didn’t happen, I feel like I can’t trust my own mind, and I feel so alone in this.
“You’re so avoidant.”When you go quiet, I panic. It feels like you’ve disappeared and I can’t reach you.
“You’re a narcissist.”I feel like I don’t exist to you. Like my needs are an inconvenience you’re waiting out.
“You’re toxic.”Being close to you started to hurt, and I don’t know how to be near you and safe at the same time.
“You’re being emotionally unavailable.”I miss you. I’m right here and I still feel completely by myself.
“That’s just your anxious attachment.”Your feelings are too big for me right now and I don’t know how to stay, so I’m labeling them to keep my distance.

Notice that the left column makes your partner the defendant. The right column makes you visible. The left column is armor. The right column is you, without it. Armor keeps you safe and it keeps you alone. That is the trade every label quietly makes for you.

What each of you can actually do

If you are the one reaching for the labels

Your job is not to stop being a person who reads and thinks and understands attachment. That is a gift. Your job is to catch the word before it leaves as a weapon, and ask it a question: what is the raw thing this is protecting? Then risk saying that instead. It will feel more exposed, because it is. The label kept you one safe step above the pain. Stepping down into “I feel like I don’t matter to you” is the vulnerable move, and it is the only one that has ever actually worked. You are trading the illusion of control for the possibility of being reached.

If you are the one being labeled

First, the hurt is real, and the urge to defend yourself is not a character flaw. Being called toxic, or a narcissist, by the person you love lands like a blow, and everything in you wants to fire back or set the record straight. That impulse is protective. It is your softer, more wounded self reaching for a shield, and I want you to know I see that before I ask you to try anything harder.

Here is the trap, though. The moment you argue the category, I am not a narcissist, that is not what gaslighting means, you are on the content battlefield, and the two of you will spend the next hour litigating a definition while the actual bond keeps snapping in the background. You can win the argument about the word and still lose the person.

So try a different move, one that asks you neither to accept the label nor to abandon yourself. Name your own sting and answer the fear underneath the word, in the same breath. Something in your partner is frightened and alone right now, and the clinical term is the clumsy flare they sent up, because they did not know how else to make you turn around. You might say, “It really hurts to be called that. And underneath it, I think I can hear that you feel small and alone right now. Is that what’s happening?” You have not agreed that you are toxic. You have refused to become your partner’s enemy while they are treating you like one.

And if even that feels like too much in the heat of the moment, there is a smaller move: put the word in the room as a third thing, instead of wearing it. “That word just landed between us and pushed us into opposite corners. I can’t reach you from over there. Can we put it down for a second?” You are not arguing about whether the label is true. You are naming what it is doing to the two of you, and opening a door back to each other. That is not surrender. It is the thing that ends the fight.

What the two of you do together

You make the cycle the enemy instead of each other. The labels are moves in a dance, and the dance is the problem, not either dancer. When you can both say “the cycle has us again” instead of “you are toxic,” you have externalized the thing that has been hurting you, and you can finally stand next to each other and look at it, instead of standing across from each other and becoming it.

When the word is actually the right word

I promised I would not pretend the labels are always wrong, so let me be clear. Sometimes “avoidant” is a fair description of a real pattern. Sometimes what someone is doing genuinely does meet the definition of gaslighting. Naming a real pattern accurately, in a calm moment, with a therapist or on your own, can be clarifying and even freeing. There is nothing wrong with the words. Language is not the villain here.

The question is never whether the word is accurate. It is what the word is doing. Is it opening a door or closing one? Is it a piece of insight you are using to understand yourself and reach for your partner, or a piece of armor you are using to win a fight and stay safe from your own tenderness? The same word can be either one. Insight brings you closer to the person. Armor keeps them at exactly the distance that is breaking your heart.

That is the whole thing, really. These words were meant to help us understand each other. Used well, they still do. The work is just to keep noticing whether, in the moment that matters most, you are using them to be understood, or using them to avoid the terrifying, ordinary risk of letting your partner see how much you need them.

Maybe you know all the words by now. Maybe you can name the cycle, trace it, explain it, and you are still stuck inside it. Maybe you are tired of being right about what is wrong and no closer to feeling close.

There is a way back underneath the language. That is the work I do with couples.
