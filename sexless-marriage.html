Sexless Marriage: when the silence says more than the number

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·A sexless marriage is usually defined as sex fewer than about ten times a year, but the number is the least useful part. What matters is the distress, the distance, and what the silence around it has come to mean.
  • ·It is usually not intrinsic low desire. Far more often it is the relationship's negative cycle showing up in the bedroom, and many couples become sexless within the first year or two as that pattern plays out sexually.
  • ·There are almost always two people hurting, not one, and it is rarely as dead as the wanting partner fears. Both usually still long for it and simply cannot talk about wanting in a way that works.
  • ·Many couples do not fight about it. They quietly accept it for years, after one partner stopped reaching because being turned down enough times taught them it was safer not to. Resignation is not the same as peace.
  • ·The way back is not pressure or scheduling. It is making it safe to talk about sex and to want again, working the emotional closeness and the sexual relationship together, not one and then the other.

The loneliest thing in a working relationship

If you are reading this, there is a decent chance you have never said the words "sexless marriage" out loud to anyone. Not to a friend, not to your doctor, maybe not even to your partner. It is one of the loneliest things a person can carry inside an otherwise functioning relationship, and one of the least talked about.

So before anything else: what you are living with is far more common than the silence around it would suggest, and it does not mean your relationship is broken beyond repair or that either of you has failed. And here is the reframe that changes everything, the one I want you to carry through the rest of this piece. A sexless relationship is almost never a story about someone having a low sex drive. It is far more often the relationship's negative cycle, the same disconnection you may feel in other rooms, showing up in the bedroom.

Let me walk through what a sexless marriage actually is, why it happens, why it is rarely as dead as it feels, why the usual advice backfires, and what a different way back looks like.

What actually counts as a "sexless marriage"

A sexless marriage is commonly defined as a relationship with sex fewer than about ten times a year, or less than once a month. The definition comes from research on sexual frequency, and it gives us a rough marker, but I want to be honest with you about how little the number tells us.

Two couples can have sex the same number of times a year and be living in completely different worlds. For one, it is a season, tender and connected, with no ache attached to it. For the other, the number is a wound that gets counted and recounted in the dark. The frequency is not the diagnosis. The distress is. If the distance in your physical relationship is causing one or both of you real pain, loneliness, or shame, then it is worth taking seriously, whatever the tally says. And if it genuinely is not, if you are both at peace, then you do not have a problem just because a statistic says you should.

So the more useful question is not "how often is normal." It is what is this distance costing us, and what is it trying to tell us.

How common this is

I will not throw a wall of statistics at you, partly because some of the most-repeated numbers come from older studies and get quoted with more confidence than they deserve. But the broad picture is well established, and it matters, because shame grows in the belief that you are the only one.

A few things we can actually say with some confidence. Sex has been getting less frequent across the board for a couple of decades, and the decline has been steepest for people in committed relationships, not singles. One large study drawing on decades of national survey data found that American adults had sex about nine fewer times a year in the early 2010s than in the late 1990s, with the drop concentrated among married and partnered people. As for outright sexless relationships, the figure you will see most often is that somewhere around one in six couples goes six months to a year or more without sex. That number is worth holding loosely, since it leans on older survey data, but even held loosely it tells you something important: this is not rare.

More common than the silence around it suggests, and more workable than it feels from inside.

Out of percentages and into people, that last row is millions of them. Some are in the school pickup line next to you. Some look, from the outside, like the couple you envy. So you are not defective, and you are not alone. You are in a situation that a very large number of people quietly know from the inside and almost no one says out loud.

Here is the part that reframes the whole thing. A striking number of couples become sexless early, sometimes within the first year or two, and it is almost never because someone turned out to have a low libido. It is because the relationship's negative cycle started expressing itself sexually. Distance and disconnection make sex feel unsafe, pressured, or hollow, one partner stops reaching for it, and the couple slowly slides into a sexlessness that neither of them chose and both of them ache over.

It is almost never as dead as it feels

If you are the partner who has been reaching, I want to say something you may not believe yet: it is rarely as dead as you think it is.

The partner who has gone quiet on sex is very often still a sexual person. They may still feel desire, still seek release privately, still notice attraction. What they have stopped doing is reaching for it with you, because with you it has become tangled up with pressure, failure, guilt, or the fear of disappointing you again. That is a very different thing from not wanting. And underneath the standoff, both partners usually still long for exactly what they have lost. They have just lost the ability to talk about wanting in a way that lands, so the longing goes underground and looks, from the outside, like indifference.

This matters enormously, because it means what looks like the end is usually a jam, not a death. The wanting is still in the room. It is under a shut-down surface, and part of the work is learning to notice the small flickers of it that are still there, the moment of closeness that softens something, the touch that does not get braced against, and to stop treating them as too small to count.

The two people in a sexless marriage

This is the part most articles get wrong, so I want to slow down here.

It is tempting to frame a sexless relationship as one person who wants sex and one person who withholds it. A wanter and a refuser. That framing feels true when you are the one being turned down, and it is almost never the whole story.

The partner who wants more is usually not just missing the physical act. They are missing evidence that they are still wanted, still chosen, still desired by the one person whose desire they most need. Every unreturned reach lands as a small rejection, and those small rejections stack into a quiet, corrosive question: am I still attractive to you, do you still want me, is there something wrong with me. Over time that person often stops reaching at all, not because they stopped wanting, but because wanting and being turned down began to hurt more than going without.

The partner who wants less is rarely cold or indifferent, though from the outside it can look that way. More often they are carrying their own weight: exhaustion, a body that does not feel like theirs anymore, low desire they do not understand and feel ashamed of, or a slow buildup of hurt and resentment that makes closeness feel unsafe long before it reaches the bedroom. When sex becomes something they sense they are failing at, or something that comes with pressure and disappointment attached, avoidance starts to feel like the only relief. And then they carry guilt for the very distance they are creating.

Two people, both hurting, both often convinced they are the only one in pain. That is the real shape of most sexless relationships. Not a villain and a victim. Two people who love each other and have lost the thread of how to reach each other.

Picture this. One partner reaches over in the dark, and feels the other go still. To the one reaching, that stillness says you are not wanted. To the one who went still, the reach said here is one more thing I am about to fail at. Same ten seconds. Two people quietly breaking, each certain they are the only one in the bed who is hurting. Neither says a word, and in the morning it is as if nothing happened, which is exactly the problem.

And let me name a myth directly, because it keeps people stuck in the wrong story. It is not always the man who wants more. Contrary to the cliche, it is very often the man who quietly shuts sex down, overriding his own body and even his own testosterone to avoid the feelings of failure and rejection that have become wrapped around it. Low desire is not a gender and it is not a character flaw. In either partner, it is usually a sign that sex stopped feeling safe.

Which points at the single most useful shift a couple in this can make: respond to the longing, not the number. Underneath "we never have sex" is almost always something far more tender, I miss you, I feel unwanted, I am afraid I do not do it for you anymore. When partners can hear and answer that, the longing underneath, instead of arguing about frequency, the whole conversation changes temperature.

The couples who have quietly accepted it

There is a version of this I want to name directly, because it is the one I see most and the one that gets written about least.

Some couples are not in crisis about their sexless relationship. They are not fighting about it. They have simply, quietly, made peace with it, sometimes for years. On the surface everything works. They co-parent, they run a household, they are kind to each other. And underneath, one or both of them carries a private grief they have stopped expecting anyone to touch.

Often what happened is this. One partner reached, and reached, and was turned down enough times that reaching started to feel humiliating. So they stopped. Not in anger, but in a kind of resignation. They decided it was easier to accept that this is just how the relationship is than to keep offering something that kept getting declined. They may not feel safe initiating anymore at all, because the memory of being unwanted is right there every time they think about trying. And the other partner, seeing the reaching stop, may quietly feel relieved, or may not notice at all, which becomes its own quiet heartbreak.

If that is you, I want to say clearly: the fact that you have accepted it does not mean it stopped mattering. Resignation is not the same as peace. A lot of people in long-standing sexless relationships are not okay with it. They have just stopped believing it could be different, and stopped letting themselves want it out loud, because wanting it hurt. That grief is real, and it is allowed, even years in. Acceptance that was built out of self-protection can be gently unbuilt.

The couple who looks like they have no problem

There is one version of this that is especially easy to miss, including for the couple themselves. They are not fighting. They would tell you, honestly, that they get along great, that there is no conflict, that they are best friends. And they are quietly sexless.

What is happening here is not a pursuer and a withdrawer. It is two withdrawers. Both partners are so careful not to hurt or inconvenience the other that neither one risks asking for anything. Needs get whispered instead of spoken, or never mentioned at all, and over time the eros drains out of a relationship that looks, from every other angle, remarkably kind. Peace is being kept at the cost of passion, because reaching for each other, really reaching, would mean risking a want that might not be met. If that is you, the good news hidden in it is that there is very little wreckage to clear. What is missing is not repair so much as permission: to want, to ask, to be a little more inconvenient to each other than you have let yourselves be.

Why it happened

When sex fades, the cause is almost never only in the body, and almost never only about sex. And here is a piece that is easy to miss: you actually have two cycles, not one, and they are distinct.

The bleed from one cycle into the other. There is the emotional cycle, the familiar pursue-and-withdraw pattern you may feel in ordinary conflict, and there is a separate sexual cycle with its own moves. They are not the same pattern, but they bleed into each other. When distance builds in the emotional cycle, sex starts to feel unsafe, or like one more obligation, so one partner stops pursuing it and the sexual side stalls on its own. This is exactly why "just feel closer" is not enough by itself, and "just have more sex" is not either. The two are linked but semi-independent, so a sexless relationship usually needs both of them attended to. I have written about the emotional side in the pursuer-withdrawer cycle and the emotional availability gap.

Bodies and health. Chronic illness, chronic pain, hormonal changes, perimenopause and menopause, thyroid issues, low testosterone, and the ordinary depletion of stress and poor sleep all shape desire in ways that have nothing to do with love. When desire drops off sharply, or the body stops responding the way it used to, it is genuinely worth ruling the physical causes in or out with a doctor. Morning erections, hormone levels, pain, and medication side effects are all things a physician can check, and clearing that up early keeps a medical issue from being misread as a lack of love.

Medication and mental health. Antidepressants, especially SSRIs, commonly lower desire. So do depression, anxiety, and unresolved trauma, which pull energy and safety out of the body long before they reach the bedroom.

Where the energy went. Sometimes desire has not vanished so much as quietly rerouted, most often into pornography, which offers a no-pressure sure thing while the harder work of wanting each other keeps getting deferred. I name this without shame, because the point is not that the outlet is evil. It is that it can let the pressure keep leaking out of the tire while the real thing in the bedroom slowly goes flat.

Life transitions. A new baby, grief, a job loss, a big move, caring for aging parents. Desire is often the first thing a nervous system sets down when it is overwhelmed, and the last thing it picks back up.

Unrepaired hurt and resentment. Betrayals large and small, contempt that never got addressed, years of feeling unseen. It is very hard for a body to open to someone it does not feel emotionally safe with. If betrayal is part of your story, I have written separately about emotional and sexual affairs and what real repair asks for.

The physiology underneath all of this, why desire has an accelerator and a set of brakes, and why the brakes matter far more than most of us were ever taught, is worth understanding on its own. I have put the fuller version of that in The Brakes Nobody Told You About, so I will not repeat it here. The short version is that for most people, desire is responsive. It does not show up on its own and then get permission. It shows up in response to safety, warmth, and low enough threat. Which is exactly why the usual fixes tend not to work.

Sex is usually the last thing to go, not the first. By the time the bedroom goes quiet, the reaching had already gone quiet somewhere else.

Why the usual advice backfires

Most advice for a sexless relationship aims straight at the symptom. Schedule sex. Plan a date night. Try harder, spice it up, just do it more.

For some couples in a mild dip, that is enough. But when the distance has real hurt underneath it, aiming at the sex directly usually makes things worse, because it adds pressure to a system that shut down from pressure in the first place. If desire went quiet because reaching stopped feeling safe, then scheduling more reaching, without changing whether it feels safe, just formalizes the thing that hurts. Now there is a calendar reminder for the exact interaction you have been dreading.

You cannot pressure a brake into releasing. The more someone feels they are failing at sex, or owing it, the more their body braces. Desire does not come back because someone tried harder to want. It comes back when the reasons not to want begin to lift. So the useful question is not "how do we do it more." It is "what is this block protecting, and what is the no keeping safe." Curiosity about that opens doors that pressure only ever closes.

The way back

For a long time the standard advice, including a lot of couples therapy, was closeness first: calm the conflict, rebuild the friendship, and trust that sex will follow. That is half right, and the missing half matters. Emotional closeness does not automatically restore sexual closeness. You can feel like good friends again and still find the bedroom stuck, because sex has its own cycle that has to be worked directly. So in the work I do, which is Emotionally Focused Therapy, we tend to two things at once: we soften the emotional cycle that pulled you apart, and we talk about sex openly and early, rather than treating it as the thing we will finally get to once everything else is fixed.

That direct conversation rests on a simple idea with real research behind it. Sex is not only a drive or a performance. In EFT we understand it as an attachment behavior in its own right, one of the main ways partners reach for each other and feel safely bonded (Johnson & Zuccarini, 2010). Once you treat it that way, a few things become possible that pressure never achieves.

The partner who has been aching gets something clarified. You do not have a right to demand sex, but you absolutely have a right to ask that the two of you talk about what is blocking it.

It is okay not to want bad sex. It is not okay to refuse to talk about what is in the way.

The partner who withdrew gets something too. The goal is never to perform sex to keep the peace or to check a box. It is to find your way back to your own desire, on your own terms, so that when you do reach for each other it is real and not dutiful. Alongside that, slow, pressure-free touch with no goal attached, sometimes called sensate focus, can rebuild the body's sense of safety, with expectations kept deliberately low and a plan for the inevitable awkward miss, so that one clumsy evening does not become fresh proof that it will never work. And underneath all of it, we learn to notice the glimmers, the small live sparks of wanting that survive under a shut-down surface, and to treat them as beginnings instead of dismissing them as too small to matter.

I want to be careful not to promise you a clean number here, because real couples are more complicated than any success rate. But it is worth knowing that this work has a genuine track record. The foundational outcome research on Emotionally Focused Therapy found that a large majority of couples, in the range of seven in ten, moved out of relationship distress, with one of the strongest effects of any couples approach studied at the time. That does not mean everyone, and it does not mean quickly. It means the thing you may have quietly given up on is more workable than it feels from inside the silence.

When the absence becomes a wound

One more thing, for the couples who have carried this the longest. We usually think of the wounds in a relationship as things that were done, a betrayal, a cruelty, a broken promise. But an absence can wound just as deeply. Years of not being reached for, not being wanted, not being touched, can land as their own kind of injury, even though no one did anything, precisely because something a bond needs was missing. In EFT we sometimes describe sex, when it is working, as part of the fruit and the fuel of a relationship, so its long absence can leave the whole bond feeling vulnerable and unsure.

The reason it helps to name it as a wound is that wounds can be grieved, and grieved wounds can be repaired. In much the same careful way couples heal from an affair, they can turn toward the grief of a sexless season, let it be spoken and mourned together, and slowly build something new on the other side of it. Naming it is not despair. It is the first honest step toward repair. (If this resonates, my pieces on affairs and what real repair asks for walk through that same kind of healing.)

If the physical part of your relationship has gone quiet, and the silence around it has started to feel permanent, it is not too late and it is not too small to bring up.

If you have been living with this a long time

I want to speak once more to the person who has quietly carried this for years.

The distance in your relationship did not build in a week, and it will not close in a week, but the belief that it can never change is itself part of what keeps it stuck. A lot of couples who thought the physical part of their relationship was simply over have found their way back to each other, not by forcing anything, but by slowly making it safe to want again.

If any of this landed, it may be worth talking to someone who works with exactly this. You can read about how I approach it on my Sex and Intimacy Therapy page, or about couples therapy more broadly. There is no version of this conversation you could bring that I would find shocking, or that would make me think less of either of you. It is some of the most human work there is.

Common questions

What is considered a sexless marriage?

The definition you will see most often is a relationship with sex fewer than about ten times a year, or less than once a month. But the number is the least useful part. Two couples can have sex the same number of times a year and live in completely different worlds, one at peace and one in real pain. What actually matters is the distress and the distance, not the tally.

Is a sexless marriage caused by low sex drive?

Usually not. In most couples I see, the problem is not that someone has an intrinsically low libido. It is that the relationship's negative cycle has moved into the bedroom: distance and disconnection make sex feel unsafe, pressured, or empty, so it fades. Many couples become sexless within the first year or two, not because desire died, but because the emotional pattern started playing out sexually. Both partners often still long for sex and simply cannot talk about wanting in a way that works.

Is it usually the man or the woman who wants less sex?

It varies far more than the cliche suggests, and it is often not who you would expect. Contrary to the stereotype that men always want more, it is frequently the man who quietly shuts sex down, overriding his own body to avoid the feelings of failure or rejection that have become attached to it. Low desire is not a gender, and it is not a character flaw. In either partner it is usually a sign that sex stopped feeling safe.

Can a sexless marriage be fixed?

Often, yes. In Emotionally Focused Therapy we work two things at once: the emotional cycle that pulled you apart, and the sexual relationship directly, because emotional closeness alone does not automatically restore sexual safety. This work has a real track record. The foundational outcome research on EFT found that a large majority of couples, in the range of seven in ten, moved out of relationship distress. It does not mean everyone, and it does not mean quickly.

My partner isn't interested in sex anymore. Does that mean they don't love me?

Usually not, and it is often not as dead as it feels. A partner who has pulled back is rarely cold or indifferent. More often they are carrying exhaustion, a body that does not feel like theirs, shame about their own desire, or a slow buildup of hurt that makes closeness feel unsafe long before it reaches the bedroom. The withdrawn partner very often still wants sex, just not in the pressured, complicated form it has taken. The distance is real, but it is almost never a verdict on how lovable you are.

How do I bring up our sex life without making my partner defensive?

Lead with the longing underneath, not the frequency complaint. "I miss feeling close to you, and I have been afraid to say so" opens a very different conversation than "we never have sex anymore." You do not have a right to demand sex, but you do have a right to ask that the two of you talk about what is blocking it. As I put it with couples, it is okay not to want bad sex; it is not okay to refuse to talk about what is in the way.

We've been in a sexless marriage for years and just accepted it. Is it too late?

It is not too late, and it is not too small to raise. A lot of couples quietly make peace with a sexless relationship, sometimes for years, often because one partner was turned down enough times that reaching started to feel humiliating and they stopped. But resignation is not the same as peace. The absence of sex can become its own quiet grief, and it can be repaired the way other attachment injuries are. Many couples who thought this part of their relationship was over have found their way back.

Does a sexless marriage always lead to an affair or divorce?

No. A sexless period is not a sentence, and the framing that it inevitably ends in an affair or divorce is neither accurate nor helpful. That said, sex is one of the main ways partners reach for and reassure each other, so its long absence can leave the bond more vulnerable to doubt and temptation. That is a reason to gently address it, not to panic. What does the real damage is usually not the frequency itself but the silence and rejection around it, which is exactly the part that can change.
