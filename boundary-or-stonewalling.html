Is It a Boundary, or Are You Stonewalling?

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Boundaries might be the best thing the last decade of therapy culture gave us.

Learning that you are allowed to say “no,” that you do not owe everyone unlimited access to your time and body and attention, that you can protect your own wellbeing without asking permission, this is genuinely good news, and for a lot of people it was hard-won. I want to be clear about that before I say anything else. Boundaries are not the problem. If you are here because you finally learned to set one, I am glad.

But I want to talk about something I see almost every week in my office, because a good idea got twisted into its own opposite. There is a version of “I’m setting a boundary” that is not a boundary at all. It is a wall. And the tell is that it is not protecting the connection, it is ending it.

This is the companion to my article on therapy words becoming weapons. That one was about the sharp end of the language, the accusations, “you’re gaslighting me,” “you’re a narcissist,” the words we reach for when we are moving toward a partner in protest. This one is the mirror image. It is about the words we reach for when we are moving away, when we need to make an exit feel dignified and unassailable. Same trend, opposite direction. Together they are the two halves of the same painful dance.

In brief

  • ·Real boundaries are essential, especially for anyone leaving a controlling relationship. This article defends them, and separates them from a counterfeit.
  • ·“I’m setting a boundary” can quietly become an emotional cut-off, a wall that ends the connection rather than a limit that protects it.
  • ·The nervous system settles by borrowing calm from a safe other (co-regulation). Chronic cut-off starves it of exactly the thing that would soothe it.
  • ·The tell is not the pause. It is whether you leave a door open. A boundary comes back to repair; a wall does not.

First, let me protect the real thing

Before I describe the counterfeit, I need to defend the genuine article, because everything I say next could be misused, and I will not write the version that hands anyone a crowbar.

⚠ Read this before the rest

A real boundary is one of the most important things you can build, and it is especially vital if you are leaving a controlling or abusive relationship. Real boundaries are not up for debate. They do not require the other person’s approval. And here is the line I most want you to hold onto: if someone responds to your boundary by telling you it is not a real boundary, that you are “just avoiding” or “being manipulative,” that pressure is itself a red flag. Nobody gets to overrule your limit by diagnosing it.

So this article is not a tool for deciding whether your partner’s boundary is legitimate. If you are reading it that way, please put it down. It is a mirror, not a courtroom. It is for the honest, private, slightly uncomfortable work of checking your own exits, and asking whether the thing you are calling a boundary is protecting the relationship or quietly starving it. That question is only yours to ask about yourself.

With that held firmly, let me tell you what I actually see.

What a nervous system does with a slammed door

Here is a piece of biology that most of the boundary conversation leaves out. Your nervous system was not built to calm itself down alone. It was built to borrow calm from another nervous system. When you are flooded and someone safe stays near, softens their voice, meets your eyes, your body reads that signal and begins to settle. This is co-regulation, and it is not a weakness or a failure of independence. It is the baseline design. Trying to regulate hard emotion entirely on your own, with no one, is possible, but it is like climbing a steep hill with a heavy pack. You can do it. It just costs far more than it needs to.

Now picture what happens on the other side of a wall. One partner says “I’m setting a boundary, I won’t engage with your toxicity right now,” and walks out. The partner left behind is not simply annoyed. Their attachment system, the oldest, deepest part of the social brain, fires an alarm that translates roughly to I am alone and I do not matter and I cannot reach you. That is not drama. That is primal panic, and the body treats it as a threat to survival, because for most of human history, being cut off from your people was a threat to survival.

So the cut-off does something crueler than just withholding a conversation. It takes the one resource that would actually bring both bodies back down, each other, and removes it at the exact moment it is needed most. Chronic cut-off keeps a relationship in a low, constant state of threat. Nobody gets to settle. The nervous system never gets its co-regulation, and over time it stops even asking.

The part that makes it so hard to name

Here is what makes the weaponized boundary more corrosive than an old-fashioned silent treatment. The word “boundary” makes the exit unchallengeable.

If your partner walks out in cold silence, at least you are allowed to say “that hurt.” But if they walk out announcing a boundary, and you protest, now you are the one “violating a boundary.” You have been cast as the aggressor for the crime of wanting connection. So the partner left in panic is not only abandoned, they are also forbidden from saying so.

That double-bind is the real damage. It is not the pause. It is the pause that has been armored against protest.

So let me be fair to the person leaving

Very often, the person setting the “boundary” is genuinely overwhelmed. Not manipulative. Flooded. There is a real physiological state, heart pounding, thoughts scattering, the sense that if this conversation continues for one more minute something will break, where the thinking brain has genuinely gone offline. In that state, stepping away is not only understandable, it can be the wisest thing in the room.

So the need to pause is real, and I would never tell you to override it. The problem was never the pause. The problem is the pause with no way back. The problem is the door that closes and does not say when it will open. What turns a healthy step-away into a wall is not the leaving. It is the leaving without a return.

The tell: a boundary keeps a door open

This is the whole thing, so let me make it as clean as I can.

A real boundary is about what you will do, and it protects your capacity to stay connected. “I get flooded when we raise our voices, so I’m going to take twenty minutes to calm down, and then I want to come back and finish this with you.” That is a boundary. It names your limit, it governs your own behavior, and crucially, it carries a promise to return. It is a speed bump. It slows the car so you do not crash. It keeps you both on the road.

A wall is about getting away from you, and it protects the person by disconnecting. “I’m not engaging with your toxicity” names no limit of your own, makes no promise, and offers no way back. It is a dead end. And your body can feel the difference between the two long before your mind can explain it.

Boundary vs. Barrier

I built this for the couples I work with, and I will say again what it is and is not. This is a mirror for your own exits, not a checklist for prosecuting your partner’s. Read it with yourself in mind.

Functional boundary
(the bridge)		 Protective barrier
(the wall)
What it’s really for To protect your ability to stay connected To protect you by getting away
What it focuses on Your own behavior (“I’ll step out if I start yelling”) The other person (“you can’t talk to me like that”)
How it sounds Clear and warm: “I’m flooded, I need 20 minutes, and I’m coming back” Vague or clinical: cold silence, or a therapy-speak label
Timeframe Temporary, with a plan to return and repair Open-ended; feels like a permanent exit
The inner story “I value us enough to pause before we do damage” “I have to shut you out or I’ll be destroyed”
What it does to your partner Lets both nervous systems settle Triggers panic and leaves them alone in it

The short version: a boundary is a speed bump that protects the connection. A barrier is a dead end that starves it of the very thing it needs to survive.

How it usually plays out

A conversation about something ordinary starts to heat up. One partner feels the criticism landing, and inside, an old alarm goes off, some version of I’m failing them again, I can’t get this right, I can’t do this. But none of that gets said. Instead, they reach for the clean, unanswerable line: “I’m setting a boundary for my mental health. I’m not going to engage with this toxicity.” And they walk out.

On the surface it looks like self-care. Underneath, what actually happened is that the more composed-sounding partner left the field and left the other one in free fall, heart pounding, staring at a closed door, and now unable to even say it hurts, because saying so would mean “not respecting a boundary.” The exit took the moral high ground. The panic got no witness. And here is the quiet tragedy: the person who left is not calm on the other side of that door. They are often flooded and ashamed and telling themselves they did the healthy thing, while the distance confirms the very fear that started it, that they are failing, that they cannot do this. Both people end up alone, each certain the other one wanted it that way.

How it gets corrected

The pause was never the problem, so we do not take the pause away. We put the door back.

The shift is from a cut-off to a reach, and it is a smaller change than it sounds. The same need for space, spoken from the fear underneath instead of from behind the wall, becomes something a partner can actually receive:

The reach, instead of the wall “I’m starting to freeze up, because I’m scared I’m letting you down. I do need a few minutes. But I’m not leaving this, and I’m not leaving you. Can we slow down so I don’t lose you? I’ll come back.”

Read those two versions side by side. “I won’t engage with your toxicity” and “I’m scared I’m letting you down, and I’m coming back.” Same request for twenty minutes. Completely different message to the other person’s nervous system. One says you are the threat and I am gone. The other says I am overwhelmed and I am still yours. The first starves. The second co-regulates, even in the act of stepping away, because it carries the promise of return.

If you tend to reach for the boundary-as-exit

The work is to catch the flooded feeling and name it before you name a boundary. Say the scared thing first. It will feel more exposed, because it is, and it is the only version that does not leave your partner alone.

If you are the one on the other side of the wall

The work is harder to hear but just as real. Your protest, loud as it is, is a reach, and to the person flooding it can land as more of the flood. When you can say “I’m not trying to trap you, I just panic when you disappear, and I need to know you’re coming back,” you make it safer for the door to open. You are not responsible for the wall. You are, together, responsible for the dance.

When the pause really is a boundary

Let me end where I started, because I do not want a single reader to walk away afraid to protect themselves. Sometimes the step-away is exactly right. Sometimes you are genuinely flooded and the kindest, wisest thing you can do is stop before you say something you cannot take back. That is not a wall. That is wisdom, as long as the door stays open and you come back.

And sometimes what you are dealing with is not a couple’s cycle at all, but a partner who uses distance to control you, who never returns, who punishes you for needing anything. That is not the subject of this article, and no checklist here should talk you out of trusting what you see. Real boundaries exist precisely to protect you from that.

The test, in the end, is not the leaving. It is the returning. A boundary says I need a moment, and I am coming back to you. A wall says I am gone. Your nervous system already knows the difference. This is just a way of teaching your words to catch up.

Maybe you keep ending up on opposite sides of a closing door. Maybe you are the one who needs to leave the room, and you do not know how to do it without disappearing. Maybe you are the one left on the other side, panicking, and told you are the problem for saying so.

There is a way to step back without cutting off. That is the work I do with couples.
