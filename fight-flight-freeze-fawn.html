Fight, Flight, Freeze, Fawn: What Survival Mode Looks Like in a Relationship

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Four living rooms, four fights, the same alarm.

In the first, a partner is standing, voice raised, listing every grievance of the last five years. In the second, someone has just walked out mid-sentence, car keys in hand. In the third, a partner sits on the couch with a blank face, saying “I don’t know” for the fourth time. And in the fourth living room there is no fight at all, because one partner agreed to everything before the disagreement could even form.

From the outside, these look like four different relationships with four different problems: an anger problem, an avoidance problem, a communication problem, a doormat problem. They are not. They are four nervous systems doing exactly the same thing: surviving a moment that feels dangerous.

I wrote elsewhere about why the body of the person you love can trip your threat alarm faster than conscious thought. This article is about what happens next, the four responses your body can reach for once the alarm has tripped, and the two directions a nervous system can move underneath all of them. Because until you can recognize these responses, in yourself and in your partner, you will keep reading survival as character. You will call care indifference, overload stubbornness, and fear agreeableness. And you will keep fighting the person instead of the pattern.

In brief

  • ·Fight, flight, freeze, and fawn are automatic survival responses, not personality flaws and not choices. They are more ancient than you.
  • ·Fight is usually desperation dressed as aggression. Flight is care that cannot stay in the room. Freeze is an overloaded system shutting down to protect itself. Fawn is safety bought by disappearing.
  • ·Underneath all four, the nervous system moves in one of two directions: hyperactivation, which looks like anger and protest, or hypoactivation, which looks like freezing up and going numb.
  • ·Couples commonly polarize, one partner hyperactivating while the other hypoactivates, and each partner’s survival response becomes the other’s trigger.
  • ·You cannot choose your response mid-flood. You can learn to recognize it earlier, help your partner read it correctly, and repair after.

The Menu Your Body Chooses From

When the alarm trips, your body has a small menu of options, and none of them are on it because you chose them. They are more ancient than you. They kept your ancestors alive.

One correction to a common assumption before we walk through them: these responses are not only installed in childhood. Sue Johnson’s work with veterans shows how adult survival training does it too. The compartmentalizing, the numbing, the handle-the-emergency-first-and-talk-later sequence that keeps a soldier, a medic, or a police officer alive at work becomes, at home, the exact wall their partner crashes into. As she told one couple: what he had to do to survive in war is now getting in the way of being open and accessible to you. If you are military, medical, or first-response, or partnered with someone who is, read what follows with that in mind. The wall was built on purpose, for good reasons, somewhere else.

Fight looks like escalation. In an argument, the defenses go up first. Then the anger and frustration surge, the voice rises, the words turn harsh, old grievances come off the shelf. For some, the body believes that being loud enough is the only way to finally be heard. Others learned something earlier and darker: that attacking first means not getting attacked. If you strike before the blow you are bracing for, at least you control when the pain arrives. From the outside, fight looks like aggression. From the inside it usually feels like desperation: I have to get through to you. I have to make you see this matters.

There is one more engine under fight worth naming, because it looks like pure aggression and is anything but. Shame immobilizes. It makes the body feel small, pinned, unable to move. And clinicians see the same pattern over and over: rage is one of the few forces strong enough to break a body out of that immobility. So the partner who explodes precisely when they feel most exposed, most caught, most in the wrong, is often not attacking from power. They are escaping shame by the only exit their body has found. This never excuses aggression. But if you have watched your partner detonate at the exact moment you expected an apology, this is usually what you were watching.

Flight looks like leaving. Walking out of the room, suddenly needing to check email, changing the subject, going to the gym, staying late at work. The body is trying to protect something, the peace, their own sanity, sometimes the relationship itself, by getting out before more damage is done. And here is what I most want the fighters reading this to hear: to you, a partner in flight looks like they do not care. In almost every couple I have sat with, the opposite is true. The partner who leaves often cares so much that staying in the fight feels unbearable, and their care shows up in different ways, in the quiet things they keep doing, in the fact that they come back. Leaving is not indifference. It is a different survival math.

Freeze looks like shutdown. The blank face, the one-word answers, the “I don’t know” that infuriates the other partner because it seems like withholding. It is not withholding. Freeze happens when the mind and body are completely overwhelmed: the brain is so overly activated that it does the opposite of what you would expect and shuts down, and the mind automatically dissociates from what is happening in the room. Think of a laptop that has been pushed too hard and suddenly powers itself off. It did not quit on you. It shut down to protect its circuits from permanent damage. That is a freeze: automatic, protective, and completely involuntary. I have sat with many partners, often but not only men, who wanted desperately to answer the question “what are you feeling?” and could not reach anything to report. The system was not refusing. It was offline.

Fawn is the response that gets missed most often, because it looks like being easy to be with. Fawning is appeasement: agreeing when you do not agree, apologizing first whether or not the fault was yours, scanning your partner’s mood and rearranging yourself around it, keeping the peace at any price. For many people this was learned early and thoroughly, in homes where soothing an unpredictable or frightening parent was the safest available move. In a long-term relationship it can pass for harmony for years. But it is not peace, it is compliance driven by fear, and the partner who fawns slowly disappears from the relationship. I have sat with couples where one partner says “we never fight,” and the other says, quietly, “I don’t know who you really are anymore.” Fawning avoids ruptures by making sure there is no full self present to rupture with. And here is something worth knowing: couples therapists are specifically trained not to mistake this presentation for health. A couple where both partners have learned to keep things smooth can look calm, polite, even enviable, and be further from real safety than the couple next door who still fights.

Civility is not de-escalation. Quiet is not the same as safe.

Two Directions Under the Four Responses

Underneath these four responses, the nervous system really only has two directions it can go under threat. Hyperactivation is the alarm turned up: energy surges, the heart races, anger flares, words come faster and sharper. From the outside it looks like rage or criticism. From the inside it is protest, a system fighting to close the distance and get a response. Hypoactivation is the alarm turned down, the system going offline to survive: numbness, blankness, freezing up, the flat “I’m fine” that is anything but fine. Fight lives in hyperactivation. Freeze and shutdown live in hypoactivation. Flight and fawn can be powered by either, which is part of why they are so often misread.

A simpler way to hold all of this: think of your brain in an argument like a traffic light. Green means you feel safe and connected. You are curious, playful, able to give a strange comment the benefit of the doubt. Yellow means the alarm has tripped: tunnel vision, ears mostly shut, not your best self no matter how much you love your partner. Red means shutdown: numb, blank, hopeless. Most bad fights are simply two yellow brains talking to each other. And here is the part worth remembering: the light you are on, not the words your partner says, decides how those words land. On green, a sigh is just a sigh. On yellow, the same sigh sounds like an accusation.

This distinction matters because it tells you what each partner actually needs in the moment. A hyperactivated partner does not need to be told to calm down; they need to know they have been heard, that the connection is not about to be lost. A hypoactivated partner does not need more intensity to “wake them up”; they need the pressure to drop low enough for the system to come back online. The most common mistake I see couples make is giving their partner what their own nervous system would need, at the exact moment their partner needs the opposite.

When Your Survival Response Meets Mine

Most couples contain some combination, and very commonly the two partners polarize: one hyperactivates while the other hypoactivates. Her volume rises because he is going blank; he goes blank because her volume is rising. This is the engine of the pursue-withdraw pattern I have written about elsewhere. Each partner’s survival response is the other partner’s trigger. That is how ninety seconds is enough. The trigger map is simple enough to say in one sentence: pursuers are most reliably triggered by their partner’s withdrawal, and withdrawers by their partner’s critical push. If you know your position in the dance, you can predict your next fight before it happens.

One more pattern deserves naming here, because couples routinely misread it as improvement. Pursuers burn out. After enough rejected reaches, the partner who used to protest goes quiet: numb, highly protected, not yet gone but grieving toward the door. Most couples who tell me “we’re both withdrawn now” turn out to be one true withdrawer and one burned-out pursuer. And counterintuitively, that quiet is the worse sign, not the better one. There is still power in a fight; silence has none. It is also why, when a burned-out partner starts feeling anger again in therapy, I am often quietly encouraged. As one EFT Trainer puts it, it hurts the way a frostbitten finger hurts as it thaws. The feeling coming back is the healing, even though it comes back as pain first.

And this is why I keep telling couples that the enemy is not either of you. Nobody chose their response. Your body picked it, long ago, from what was available in the home you grew up in, and it picked well: it got you here. The problem is that the strategy that kept you safe then is now colliding, nightly, with the strategy that kept your partner safe. That collision, not either partner’s character, is what has been running your worst fights.

What to Do With This

I will not pretend recognition alone dissolves the pattern. But it changes more than you might expect.

Name your own response, without shame. Not “I’m a stonewaller” but “when I get flooded, my system goes offline.” The first framing is a character verdict. The second is information your partner can actually work with.

Teach each other your tells. “When I go quiet, I am not ignoring you. I am overloaded, and I need a few minutes before I can find words.” “When my voice rises, I am not trying to hurt you. I am panicking that I’ve lost you.” Couples who can exchange these translations stop misreading survival as character.

Respect the involuntary part. You cannot demand that a frozen partner speak, any more than they can demand you stop feeling your heart race. What you can build together is a way to pause that includes a promise to return, and a habit of repairing after every rupture. I have written more about de-escalation and repair in the main article of this series.

Check the repair in your body, not your words. EFT Trainers Ryan Rana and George Faller teach a move called body in, body out. When something hits you, locate its physical marker first, the tight chest, the dropped stomach, rather than debating the words. Then, after your partner responds or comforts you, check the marker again. If the body loosened, the repair landed. If it did not, something got missed, whatever the words said. “Are we good?” “We’re fine” has fooled far more couples than this ever will.

Get curious about where the response was installed. If your fight response learned to strike first, or your fawn learned that your needs were the price of safety, that learning has a history, and it can be updated. That is the subject of the third article in this series, and it is some of the deepest work I do with couples and individuals.

Questions Couples Ask Me About Survival Responses

Is my partner’s silence stonewalling or freezing?

On the surface they look identical. The difference is what is happening inside and what happens after. A freeze is involuntary overload; the person often cannot speak even when they want to. What matters most is the pattern around it: a partner who comes back when their system settles, and repairs, is not the same as a partner who uses silence as leverage. In my office, the “stonewaller” usually turns out to be flooded, not indifferent.

Can one person have more than one response?

Yes, and most people do. You might fight with your partner, fawn with your boss, and freeze with your father. The menu depends on the relationship, the stakes, and how far past threshold you are. Many people also stack responses: fight until it fails, then collapse into shutdown.

Which response is the worst one?

None of them. Each has costs and each once made perfect sense. What damages relationships is not any single response but the loop two responses build together, and the absence of repair afterward. If anger is your response, though, John Bowlby’s old distinction is worth knowing. The anger of hope is a reproach aimed at restoring a bond that still feels repairable; underneath, it says this hurts because you matter. The anger of despair fires when the bond feels already lost, and it turns purposeless and destructive. Most people can tell which one is living in their house. Neither excuses aggression or intimidation, but they point in opposite directions, and only one of them is running out of time.

Why does my partner pull away when things are going well?

Because for some nervous systems, intensity itself is the danger signal, including positive intensity. A gushing compliment, a big joyful reaction, an enthusiastic reach can trip the same wary shutdown as criticism. One client’s phrase for it has stayed with me: “I just want a ripple, not a tsunami.” If your partner retreats at your happiest moments, it may not be rejection or self-sabotage. It may be a body that learned to distrust intensity in every flavor, asking for joy in doses it can survive.

Can I change my survival response?

Not by willpower in the middle of a flood, no. But responses soften as the nervous system accumulates new experiences of conflict that does not end in catastrophe. That is slow, real change, and it is precisely what good couples therapy is built to create.

If You Recognized Yourself

If you found yourself in one of those four living rooms, or if you finally recognized your partner in one of them, that recognition is worth something. It is the difference between “my partner doesn’t care” and “my partner’s body is surviving me right now,” and I have watched that single reframe soften years of resentment in a single session.
