Is It Love Bombing, or Are You Just ‘Too Much’?

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

There is a strange new fear going around. People are becoming afraid of their own enthusiasm.

Somewhere in the last few years, “love bombing” jumped from clinical writing about abuse into everyday dating talk, and the meaning stretched on the way. Now, if a new partner texts a lot, plans something thoughtful, or says they are excited about you, a chorus online is ready to call it a red flag. So people have started to flinch at warmth. They suppress the urge to reach. They watch a partner’s affection through narrowed eyes, and they perform a kind of studied coolness because someone told them detachment is what health looks like.

I want to untangle this, because two true things are getting collapsed into one. Love bombing is real, and it is serious. And eager, anxious, wholehearted reaching is also real, and it is not a disorder. Telling them apart matters, and the good news is that the way to tell them apart is not what the internet is selling you.

Here is the reassurance the panic leaves out: the large majority of intense early connections are not love bombing. They are just connection, two nervous systems lighting up at once. The serious exception is real, and I will name it clearly first, but it is the exception, not the rule.

In brief

  • ·True love bombing is not just intense affection. It is the idealization phase of coercive control, a setup that is built to turn into devaluation.
  • ·Eager, anxious reaching looks similar from the outside but comes from the opposite place: a bid for a safe connection, not a bid for control.
  • ·The tell is not the intensity. It is what a partner does when you ask to slow down, and whether they pull you away from your people.
  • ·You are not too much. Eagerness is not a red flag. If you see control or fear, though, trust it and use the resources below.

First, the part that is genuinely serious

Let me be clear about the real thing before I defend the ordinary thing, because I do not want to wave anyone off a danger they are actually sensing.

When love bombing is real, it is not just intense affection. It is the opening move of coercive control. In the clinical picture it is the idealization phase, the flood of adoration and grand gestures and “I have never felt this way” that comes first, precisely so the later devaluation, the criticism, the isolation, the control, lands with maximum disorientation. That is what makes it frightening. The warmth was not the relationship. It was the bait. Real love bombing is often the first visible chapter of intimate partner terrorism, and by the time the second chapter arrives, the person is already isolated and doubting themselves.

⚠ If your instinct is picking up more than intensity

Please trust it. If the affection comes with a slow pull away from your friends and family. If your hesitation gets punished. If you feel watched, managed, or afraid. That is not a pace problem, and it is not something to talk yourself out of.

Noticing “this is too much, too fast” can be wisdom, and I will never tell you to override it. What I want to add is that intensity by itself is not the evidence, and this is where the culture has it backwards.

What eager attachment actually is

Here is what gets swept up and mislabeled far more often than anyone admits: a nervous system reaching hard for connection.

When someone comes on strong out of eagerness rather than strategy, the behavior, wanting to be together, texting often, seeking reassurance, is not a hook. It is a bid. Underneath it is a question the body is asking on a loop: am I safe with you, do I matter, are you going to stay. An anxiously attached person feels that question loudly, and when they sense any distance, they turn the volume up, reaching harder to close a gap that scares them. It can look like a lot. It is not manipulation. It is a person ringing the bell to find out if anyone is home.

This deserves saying loudly, because nearly every article on this topic is written to the suspicious party, and almost none of it is written to you, the one doing the reaching. So let me say it straight. Your warmth is not a symptom. Your wanting is not a warning sign. Texting first, planning something thoughtful, being visibly glad about a person: none of that is pathology, and none of it makes you dangerous to be near.

You are not too much. You were told you were, by a culture that mistook coldness for maturity and started calling a flinch a boundary. Eagerness is not a red flag. It is a heart that still works. The goal was never to shrink your enthusiasm down to something the anxious internet finds palatable. It is to let it breathe while you keep your feet under you and watch, over time, whether it is landing somewhere safe.

The tell is not the intensity. It is the response to a pause.

You cannot see a partner’s intent. Neither can I. So any test built on “figure out whether they mean to control you” is unusable, and it is the kind of guess that gets made wrong in the dangerous direction. Move the whole question onto something you can actually observe: what happens when you slow it down.

Intensity is neutral. What tells you the truth is what a partner does when you say “this is moving a little fast for me.”

Say some version of that, gently, and watch what comes back. Someone eager but safe will feel the sting, maybe over-apologize, maybe text too much about it, but they will respect the pace and come back to you. Someone love bombing will bulldoze the boundary, sulk, turn it around so you are the cold and ungrateful one, or go quiet to punish you. The reach can survive a “slow down.” The setup cannot tolerate it, because the whole point of the setup was that you not have your own pace.

There is a second signal, and it is time. Eager attachment has no second act built into it. It either settles as safety grows, or it just stays warm. Love bombing has a devaluation phase waiting inside it, so it turns. You do not have to diagnose someone on date two. Let the intensity be intensity, hold your own pace, and watch how it responds and how it ages.

Words to try

To ask for a slower pace“I really like where this is going, and I want to slow down a little so I can actually enjoy it. Can we do that?”

If you are the one being called ‘too much’“I hear that my pace feels like a lot. I’m not trying to trap you. I get scared when I feel far from you. Can we find a speed that works for both of us?”

What that looks like in real life

The same moment plays out in two completely different ways. The intensity can look identical. The response is where the truth is.

The moment An eager reach Love bombing
You’re busy this weekend Disappointed, maybe texts too much about missing you, but says “have fun.” “I cancelled everything for you, I guess I care more.” Shows up anyway, or goes cold.
A gift Something you would love. It is about you. Extravagant things that leave you feeling indebted, with a story attached about how generous they are. It is about them.
Your friends and family Wants into your world and likes your people. Softly frames your people as those who “don’t really get us.” The isolation beginning.
The pace Feels a lot, but can let you set the speed. Future-fakes, moving in, marriage, “soulmate” in week two, and treats your hesitation as betrayal.

Of these, the third row matters most. A reach wants to join your life. A setup wants to become your life, and quietly clear the room of anyone who might notice.

When the label becomes a weapon inside a relationship

There is a version of this that shows up in couples I already work with, long past the dating phase. One partner reaches, and the other, the one who tends to pull away, has learned to say “you’re love bombing me” or “you’re too much.” The clinical word does something convenient. It reframes a bid for closeness as a pathology, which justifies the distance.

But labeling a reach as manipulation does not calm the reacher. It panics them. Now they are not only unmet, they are accused, so they ring the bell louder to prove they are sincere, which the withdrawing partner reads as more proof of “bombing,” and the two of you accelerate the exact cycle that is hurting you. This is the pursuer-withdrawer cycle with a clinical term poured on it, and it is a close cousin of the way therapy words become weapons. The word feels like insight. It functions like a wall.

Why the wrong read costs you either way

Call an eager reach love bombing, and you teach a good-hearted person that their warmth is dangerous. You push people into performing aloofness, suppressing genuine longing, and treating detachment as maturity. Inside a couple, you hand a withdrawer a reason to stay behind the wall.

Call real love bombing “just their anxious attachment,” and you can talk yourself past the first clear sign of someone who is going to try to control you.

Both are costly. The cultural error right now runs heavily toward the first, so that is where most of this article sits. But the second is the one with teeth, which is why, when it is genuinely unclear, the move is not to decide alone from a checklist. It is to slow down, keep your people close, and let time and a good boundary show you what you are dealing with.

My honest answer

If you are the eager one, afraid you are “too much,” hear this. Your enthusiasm is not the problem, and you do not owe anyone a smaller, cooler version of yourself to prove you are healthy. Reach. Then keep your feet under you: hold your own pace, keep your friendships alive, and notice not how strongly you feel, but how safe it feels to set a limit. A love worth having can hold your whole heart. It will never ask you to bring less of it.

If you are the wary one, afraid the warmth is a trap, hear this. Your radar is not paranoia. You do not have to explain away what you see. Test it kindly, ask for a slower pace, and watch what happens. A safe person can bend. Watch, too, whether you are being drawn away from the people who love you, because that is the tell that matters most.

And if what you are seeing is control, fear, or a quiet campaign to cut you off from your world, that is not a pace question and it is not this article’s to solve. Please reach for the support at the top of this page.

Where to start, depending on where you are

There is no single right next step here, because the people reading this are in very different places. Here is how I would point you.

Doing this work on your own? If you are dating, single, or your partner is not ready, individual therapy is a good place to understand your own attachment and steady your reach.

A newer couple, serious about each other? The best time to learn your cycle is before the wedding, not in year ten. That is exactly what premarital counseling is for.

An established couple stuck in “too much” and “not enough”? That loop is the heart of EFT couples therapy. If you want to move faster, an intensive covers months of work in a few focused days.

Not sure which one is you? That is completely normal. Sorting this out on your own is hard to do and easy to get wrong, which is exactly what a first conversation is for. Book a free consultation and we will figure it out together.

The rush of an early connection is one of the more human things there is. It is not, by itself, evidence of anything except that you are alive to another person.

The work is not to kill the feeling. It is to stay standing inside it, keep your own pace, keep your people, and let a boundary and a little time tell you the truth.
