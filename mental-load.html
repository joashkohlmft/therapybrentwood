The Mental Load August 2026⏱ 12 min read

The Chore Fight That Was Never About Chores

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·The mental load is real, and in most homes it is not evenly shared. Naming that is not nagging, it is telling the truth about an imbalance.
  • ·But the fight it causes, the one about the dishes and the calendar and who booked the dentist, is rarely only about tasks. Underneath the logistics is an attachment alarm: am I carrying this whole life alone, and do I even matter to you?
  • ·“Nagging” is usually a reach in disguise. Blame is the safer, more armored way of asking a tender question the person is too worn down to ask straight.
  • ·“Weaponized incompetence” is real in its effects, but it is rarely laziness. More often it is a partner frozen by shame, sure they can never get it right, so they stop trying.
  • ·The pursue-and-withdraw loop, not either person, is the actual problem. And once the bond feels safe again, dividing the labor gets dramatically easier. Safety first, logistics second.

The fight that really is about the dishes (and also is not)

There is a particular fight that a lot of couples could perform in their sleep. One partner is standing in a kitchen that they cleaned four hours ago and is somehow dirty again, holding a mental list of the fourteen things only they seem to track, and something finally gives. “I have to ask for everything.” And the other partner, stung, reaches for the line that ends the conversation and the closeness at the same time: “I’m not a mind reader,” or the one that really lands, “I’m not your child.”

Let me say the honest thing first, because it usually gets skipped. On the surface, that fight really is about the dishes. And the calendar. And the fact that one of you is running a background program at all times, the invisible spreadsheet of who needs what and when, while the other genuinely does not seem to be. That imbalance is not imaginary, and it is not a personality quirk to be lovingly accepted. It is a real and often lopsided distribution of labor, and the person carrying more has every right to name it.

So this is not an article that dissolves a legitimate grievance into “it’s just miscommunication.” The load is real. What I want to show you is that the fight about the load is carrying something even heavier than the load, and that until you can hear that heavier thing, no chore chart on the fridge is going to fix it.

Why one of you carries more

Before we go underneath it, let us stay on the surface a moment longer, because it deserves it.

In most different-sex couples, and it does tend to run this direction even though it does not always, one partner becomes the household’s project manager. Not because they are naturally more organized, but because someone has to hold the whole picture, and the culture quietly assigned that someone a long time ago. They are the one who notices the shoes are getting too small, that the permission slip is due, that grandma’s birthday is Thursday. This is the invisible part of invisible labor: the noticing and the worrying and the remembering, all of which happen before a single visible task gets done.

Running that program has a cost, and it is not only fatigue. It breeds a low, constant resentment, which is not a character flaw. It is a completely normal response to being unhelped. And it comes with a particular kind of loneliness, because the person carrying it often feels a little crazy for how much it bothers them. They swallow nine small frustrations for every one they voice, and then when they finally voice the one, they get told they are overreacting to a cup left by the sink. The gap between how much they are holding and how little it seems to register is its own quiet injury.

If that is you, I want to be clear: you are not too sensitive, and you are not asking for too much. You are describing something true.

“Nagging” is a reach, not an attack

Here is the first turn, and it is the one that changes everything.

When that partner finally snaps, “why do I have to ask you to do anything,” it sounds like an attack, and it often gets received as one. But listen to what is actually underneath it. It is a question, and a frightened one. Do you see what I am carrying? Am I alone in this? If I stopped holding all of it, would anything be caught, or would it just fall?

That is not a criticism. That is a bid for connection wearing the armor of a complaint. And people reach for the armor for a reason. It feels far too exposed to walk up to your partner and say, “I feel invisible in my own home, and I am scared I don’t matter to you the way I need to.” So instead the nervous system offers a safer, sturdier line: “You didn’t take out the trash again.” The trash is real. But the trash is also standing in for the sentence the person cannot yet bear to say out loud.

What gets called nagging is usually a bid for connection wearing the armor of a complaint.

Which means there is a trap built into this fight. If the other partner hears only the words, they might actually go take out the trash, then return to their phone, satisfied that the problem is solved. And the partner who reached is left more alone than before, because the task got handled and the person did not. The bin was never the point. Being met was the point.

I will say one more thing here, because it matters. The partner who is still fighting, still nagging, still bringing it up, is not the one I worry about most. There is still hope in a protest. The person who has gone quiet, who has stopped asking because asking never worked, who now just does it all themselves with a closed face, that is the harder place. Silence is not peace. It is often the sound of someone who has given up on being reached.

The other side, without letting anyone off the hook

Now the part that keeps this honest, because a good version of this cannot only be an indictment of one partner.

Picture the other person in the fight. From the outside they look checked out, defensive, maybe willfully useless. But step inside for a second. Very often this is someone who has learned that participating in the home is a losing game. They load the dishwasher and it gets re-loaded in front of them. They handle bedtime and hear everything they did wrong. Their nervous system has quietly filed domestic life under “a test I always fail.” So they start telling themselves the thing that makes it bearable: no matter what I do, it is never enough, so why walk into that.

This is where the phrase everyone is using, weaponized incompetence, needs a more honest look. Sometimes, yes, there is a genuine free ride happening, and I am not going to pretend that never occurs. But far more often, what looks like manufactured helplessness is a self-protective move. It is easier to be accused of doing nothing than to be accused, again, of doing it wrong. The “I’m just bad at this” is not always a dodge. Sometimes it is a person frozen by the fear of disappointing someone they love, choosing the failure that hurts less.

You can hold both of these at once. The imbalance is unfair and the under-doing partner is usually not a villain. In fact you have to hold both, because the moment this becomes “you are lazy and I am the martyr,” the cycle locks tighter. Contempt has never once motivated a withdrawn partner to lean in. It only teaches them the stove is hot.

The real problem is the loop, not either of you

Diagram: The Household Protest Loop

Once you see the two inner experiences side by side, the machine becomes obvious. The load piles up, the carrier sounds the alarm the only way that feels safe, which is sharply. The withdrawer hears not “I need you” but “you have failed me yet again,” and does the thing that has always kept them safest, which is to get small and disappear. That disappearance is, to the carrier, the final proof: I really am on my own here. So the volume goes up, purely out of panic, and the louder it gets the more the withdrawer hears a verdict rather than a plea, and the further they go.

Neither of them is doing this on purpose. Neither of them is the problem. The loop is the problem. And you cannot out-argue a loop with a better-designed chore spreadsheet, because the spreadsheet is aimed at the surface and the engine is running underneath.

What the dishes are actually asking

Strip the household cycle all the way down and you find the same three questions that live under every distressed bond. Are you there for me. Do I matter to you. Will you come when I need you. The mental load fight is those questions in an apron.

This is why the small asks carry so much more weight than they should. When someone asks their partner to unload the dishwasher, and it is the fourth time, and it comes out sharp, the sharpness is not really about the dishwasher. The task has become symbolic. “Will you do this without me having to manage you” has quietly become “will you show up for me without me having to beg.” Caregiving in a couple was never meant to be transactional, a ledger of tasks split down the middle. It is relational. It is one of the main ways partners actually say I have got you to each other. When the caregiving goes lopsided, what the carrier feels is not just overworked. It is unheld.

How a home actually re-balances (it is not a chore chart)

Here is the part that surprises people, and it is genuinely hopeful.

The goal is not to sit the two of you down and negotiate a perfectly equal division of labor while the resentment is still hot. That negotiation fails, every time, because you are trying to solve a safety problem with a logistics tool. The order runs the other way. First the bond has to feel safe again, and then, almost on its own, the labor gets easier to share. Secure partners are flexible and generous with each other by default. Once a request stops feeling like a referendum on your worth, a request to unload the dishwasher is just a request to unload the dishwasher.

So the move is not a spreadsheet. It is a different kind of conversation, one that most couples have never actually had. It has a shape.

It starts by naming the loop out loud together, as the shared enemy. Not “you never help,” but “when the house gets chaotic, an alarm goes off in me that says I’m all alone, and I come at you hard, and you hear that you’ve failed me, so you go quiet and disappear, and your disappearing is the thing that scares me most.” When a couple can say that together, both people stop being the villain at the same time.

Then it asks each partner to reach for the softer thing under their usual move. For the carrier, that means letting the anger step aside so the loneliness and the fear underneath can be heard: “I don’t want to be your nag and I don’t want to be your mother. But when I’m holding all of this by myself, I get so scared that I don’t matter to you. I need us to be a team.” For the withdrawer, it means letting the defense drop so the shame can be spoken instead of acted out: “It’s so hard to hear that you feel alone, because I always feel like I’m failing you, and that’s why I leave. But I don’t want to run to the garage anymore. I want to help carry this.”

And it asks the responding partner to get good at four small things, which are really the whole of good caregiving: notice the landscape before you are asked, so your partner does not have to manage you into seeing it. Treat what they feel as real even when you would not feel it yourself. Resist making their overwhelm about you and your guilt. And invite the feedback instead of bracing against it. None of that is about doing more chores. It is about becoming safe to reach for. The chores follow.

An honest, hopeful close

I want to leave you with the thing I most believe about this, having watched it in the room many times.

The couples who solve the mental load do not solve it by becoming better logistics managers. They solve it by getting the threat out of the middle of the room, so that the two of them are standing on the same side of it. When the dishes stop being a test of whether you are loved, they go back to being dishes, and two people who trust each other sort dishes out with almost no drama at all. The exhaustion in this fight is real, and so is the unfairness. But the loneliness underneath it, the part that actually breaks people, that one has a way through, and it is not on the fridge. It is in learning to say the scared, true thing, and to be met when you do.

This is the heart of the work I do in couples therapy, built on Emotionally Focused Therapy, and it is some of the most relieving work there is, because the care almost never turned out to be gone. It was just buried under a very long, very tired, very unshared list.

When the dishes stop being a test of whether you are loved, they go back to being dishes. Two people who feel like a team again sort them out with almost no drama at all.

Common questions

Is the mental load a real thing or just an excuse?

It is real and it is measurable. In most homes the invisible work of noticing, planning, and remembering falls unevenly on one partner, and naming that is not an excuse, it is an accurate description of an imbalance worth taking seriously.

Why do I have to ask my partner to help with everything?

Because the “asking” is usually the visible tip of a much larger invisible job: you are the one holding the whole mental picture. The exhaustion is not from the tasks alone, it is from being the only one running the background program, and often from feeling unseen in it.

Is weaponized incompetence real, or is it just an insult?

The pattern is real, one partner ends up doing less by doing things poorly or claiming they can’t. But it is usually not laziness or a deliberate scheme. More often it is self-protective: a partner who feels they can never get it right stops trying, because being accused of doing nothing hurts less than being accused of doing it wrong.

We fight about chores constantly. What are we actually fighting about?

Almost always, something underneath the chores. The heat is out of proportion to the dishwasher because the dishwasher has become symbolic. Underneath “why do I have to ask” is usually “do you see me, am I carrying this alone, do I matter to you.”

Isn't nagging the problem?

What gets labeled nagging is usually a bid for connection in armor. It is a person asking a tender, frightened question the only way that feels safe, which is sharply. The fix is not to stop asking, it is to help the softer question underneath get heard and answered.

My partner went quiet and stopped fighting about it. Isn't that better?

Usually not. A partner who has gone silent has often not made peace, they have given up on being reached. Protest still contains hope. Quiet resignation is frequently the more worrying sign, not the calmer one.

Will a chore chart or a fair-division app fix this?

Rarely on its own, because those tools aim at the surface while the engine runs underneath. If the labor still feels like a test of whether you are loved, the chart becomes one more thing to fight about. Restore emotional safety first, and the practical division gets dramatically easier to negotiate.

Does this mean the imbalance is my fault for how I'm asking?

No. The imbalance can be genuinely unfair and the way it gets fought about can still be keeping you both stuck. Two things are true at once: you have a right to a real partner in the load, and the pursue-and-withdraw cycle around it is nobody’s solo fault. The way out is changing the cycle together, not deciding who is the villain.
