Can Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome Be Healed? What Becomes Possible When Both Partners Do the Work

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome, 4-part series

Part 1: When One Partner Carries the Relationship Alone Part 2: What ARS Looks Like in the Room Part 3: You're Not Crazy Part 4: Can ARS Be Healed?

This is Part 4 of a four-part series on Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome. If you are arriving here first, start with Part 1, which names and defines ARS, or Part 3, written directly for the person living inside this dynamic. This piece looks at what healing actually makes possible, and what the path forward looks like, whether your partner is willing to do the work or not.

In brief

  • ·Whether ARS can heal depends almost entirely on whether both partners are willing to engage, the prognosis with mutual engagement is genuinely good.
  • ·With EFT, the carrying partner’s grief can be witnessed and the withdrawing partner can learn to show up differently.
  • ·When only one partner is willing, healing looks different, it may mean individual work, discernment counseling, or grief about what the relationship has not been.
  • ·Healing is possible either way, but it requires honesty about what is actually present.

The Question Underneath the Question

When someone asks me whether Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome can be healed, what they are usually really asking is something more specific and more frightening.

Can my relationship be saved?

And underneath that: Is it worth saving?

And underneath that, the question they are often most afraid to ask out loud: If my partner never changes, does that mean I have to choose between staying and disappearing, or leaving and starting over?

These are not small questions. They deserve honest answers. Not reassuring ones, not careful ones, honest ones.

So let me start there.

The Honest Answer About Prognosis

Yes. ARS can be healed.

Not managed. Not endured with greater equanimity. Not survived by becoming someone who needs less. Healed. In the sense that the structural asymmetry that created the injury can be restructured, the nervous system that has been running on alarm can find its way back to something resembling rest, and the relationship, if both partners are willing, can become something genuinely different from what it has been.

But the honest answer also includes this: the prognosis is significantly different depending on whether one partner is willing to do the work, or both.

This is not a comfortable thing to say. But it is a true one, and people living inside ARS deserve the truth more than they deserve comfort right now.

When Both Partners Are Willing

When both partners come to therapy with genuine willingness, not the willingness of compliance, not the willingness of someone who has agreed to come so their partner will stop asking, but real willingness, a readiness to look at the dance they have been in and acknowledge their part in it, something remarkable becomes possible.

The research on couples therapy using an attachment-informed approach is genuinely encouraging. Studies consistently show recovery rates from relationship distress in the range of seventy percent or higher. Couples who reach the deeper change events in therapy, the moments where both partners can finally speak their vulnerability to each other directly, show sustained improvement not just at the end of treatment but years afterward. The change, when it happens, tends to hold.

This is not because therapy teaches couples new communication scripts or better fighting techniques. It is because something more fundamental shifts.

The cycle changes.

In ARS, the cycle has been running in one direction for so long that both partners have forgotten there was ever another way to be together. The pursuing partner has forgotten what it feels like to reach from a place of calm rather than alarm. The withdrawing partner has forgotten, or perhaps never fully knew, what it feels like to turn toward rather than away when the relationship feels unsafe.

Therapy creates the conditions for both partners to discover something new about themselves and each other. Slowly, carefully, with a great deal of support.

What the Change Actually Looks Like

For the pursuing partner, healing is not about learning to need less. It is about learning to reach differently.

There is a particular moment in this kind of therapy that I find myself thinking about often. It is the moment when a pursuing partner, who has spent months or years reaching with anger, with urgency, with the desperate energy of someone who has been screaming into a void, finally finds the quieter thing underneath.

The longing beneath the alarm.

And from that place, instead of "Why won't you ever talk to me?!" something softer comes out. Something true. "I miss you. I am scared when you go quiet. I need to know I matter to you."

That shift, from protest to vulnerability, from demand to genuine reaching, is one of the most powerful moments in couples therapy. Because it gives the withdrawing partner something they can actually respond to. The anger, however understandable, tended to confirm their fear that they were always failing. The vulnerability gives them something different. Something that invites rather than accuses.

For the withdrawing partner, healing is not about learning to tolerate more. It is about learning that turning toward is safer than they believe it to be. That their presence, genuinely offered, does not have to be perfect to be enough. That "I hear you, and I am here" spoken simply and sincerely can do more for their partner than any amount of perfectly executed problem-solving. It is the moment they discover that their presence, offered imperfectly and honestly, is actually enough, and that the person they love has never needed them to be perfect. Only there.

When these two shifts meet each other in the same moment, something happens between two people that is genuinely transformative. It is not dramatic, usually. It often looks quiet from the outside. But inside it, both people feel something shift. A door that has been closed for a very long time opens, just a little, and the light that comes through is different from anything the relationship has felt in a while.

This is what the research calls a bonding event. And it is what changes a cycle.

Key term: Bonding event. A term from EFT research for the moment when one partner reaches with genuine vulnerability and the other responds with presence and care, creating a new emotional experience that updates the attachment pattern between them and predicts lasting recovery.

What Therapy Is Actually Doing

People sometimes come to therapy expecting to be taught something. A new way to communicate. A framework for conflict. A set of tools they can take home and practice.

What tends to happen instead is something more unexpected. And more profound.

The first thing most people notice is that the room feels different from any conversation they have had about their relationship before. There is no agenda to protect anyone. No need to be fair or balanced or careful about how things land. There is just space. And in that space, for perhaps the first time, the real thing can be said.

Not the managed version. Not the version edited for a partner who might shut down, or a previous therapist who kept redirecting toward communication skills. The actual, unedited, this-is-what-it-has-been-like thing.

And when that gets said, and met, something shifts.

Not immediately. Not all at once. But slowly, over sessions, a different relationship to the pain begins to emerge. The alarm that has been running at full volume begins, gradually, to quiet. Not because the situation has changed yet, but because the nervous system is finally getting the one thing it most needed: to be fully known by someone who did not flinch.

From that place, something else becomes accessible. The feeling beneath the anger. The grief beneath the urgency. The quieter, more vulnerable truth that has been driving all of it from the beginning.

And it is from that place, that softer and more honest place, that real change becomes possible. In the therapy room first. And then, if both partners are willing, between them.

A Moment Worth Describing

I want to tell you about a particular kind of moment that happens in this work. Not a dramatic one. Not the kind of breakthrough that looks like a breakthrough from the outside.

It usually happens quietly, mid-session, in a moment that almost passes without notice.

The pursuing partner has been talking. She has been articulate and precise about her pain, the way people are when they have been rehearsing something for a long time. She knows the incidents. She knows the dates. She can describe the exact quality of his silence on a Tuesday evening three weeks ago in a way that makes the room feel it too.

And then something shifts. I am not always sure what causes it. Sometimes it is a question. Sometimes it is just the quality of the silence in the room, the way slowing down creates enough space for something else to surface.

Her voice changes. It gets quieter. The precision falls away, and what is left is something much less composed and much more true.

"I just... I don't know where he goes. When he gets quiet like that. I don't know if he's still in this with me. And I need to know. I really need to know."

Not an accusation. Not a demand. A reaching. A genuine, undefended reaching, from the longing that the cycle has been covering all along.

And across the room, something happens in him that I have learned to watch for. A slight softening around the jaw. A shift in how he is sitting. He has heard something in that sentence that he could not hear when it came dressed in anger and urgency. He has heard what is actually being asked of him. And it turns out, it is something he can give.

He turns toward her. Not perfectly. Not with a speech. With something simpler and more than enough.

"I'm still here. I'm not going anywhere."

That moment does not fix everything. The work continues. But something fundamental has changed in the room, and both people feel it. The cycle has been interrupted not by a rule or a technique but by two people finally hearing each other's actual language.

That moment is available to couples who are willing to do this work. I have sat with it more times than I can count. And it never stops being the thing that reminds me why this work matters.

When Only One Partner Is Willing

This is the harder conversation. And it is one that too many pieces about relationship healing skip entirely, because it is uncomfortable and because it does not end with an easy resolution.

So let me be honest.

If your partner is unwilling to examine their role in the dynamic, unwilling to consider that their withdrawal has consequences, unwilling to engage in any process of reflection or growth, then couples therapy cannot restructure the relationship on its own. A therapist cannot do for a relationship what one of the partners is unwilling to do.

That is a painful truth. And if it applies to your situation, I want to hold it with you rather than hand it to you and walk away.

What remains possible when only one partner is willing is still significant. It is not the full healing of the relationship as it currently exists. But it is real, and it matters.

One path is individual therapy, focused specifically on the attachment wound that ARS has created. This work can help you find the feeling beneath the alarm, understand the childhood blueprint that made you vulnerable to this dynamic, and begin to loosen the grip of the internal shame cycle. It can help you get clear about what you need, what you can and cannot continue to carry, and what a life of genuine connection might actually look like.

It is worth naming something honestly here, though. If you begin individual therapy with me first, I would not be able to subsequently work with you and your partner together in couples therapy. This is an ethical boundary I hold carefully, and it matters because it protects the integrity of both processes. Individual therapy asks you to develop a particular kind of trust and safety with your therapist. Couples therapy asks something different of that same relationship. Mixing the two, even with the best intentions, creates a conflict that ultimately serves neither you nor your partner well.

So the path matters. And it is worth thinking through before we begin.

This is not a limitation I name to complicate things. It is one I name because getting the path right from the beginning is one of the most important things I can do for you.

A good starting point is a consultation, where we can look together at where you are, what you are hoping for, and which form of support makes the most sense given your specific situation. Some people need individual work first. Some are ready to bring their partner in from the beginning. Some are not yet sure whether the relationship has a future and need a different kind of conversation altogether, which is where discernment counseling comes in. All of these are real options. None of them is the wrong one if it is the right fit for where you actually are.

The Question of Whether to Stay

I am not going to tell you whether to stay in your relationship.

That is not my decision. It should not be anyone's decision but yours, made with full information, full self-knowledge, and whatever support you need to make it clearly and without pressure.

But I want to honor the weight of the question. Because I think it often gets treated as a binary, stay or go, fight or surrender, love them or leave them, and the reality of living inside ARS is so much more complicated than that framing allows.

There are people reading this who know, somewhere underneath the exhaustion and the hope and the shame, that the relationship has already ended in all but name. That what they are really doing is gathering the courage to acknowledge something they have already been living for a long time. If that is you, I want you to know that clarity, however painful, is not failure. It is the beginning of something. And you deserve support in finding your way through it, not judgment.

There are people reading this who are genuinely uncertain. Who love their partner. Who can see, beneath the cycle, something real that was once there and might, with the right help, be there again. Who are not ready to give up but are not sure they can keep going the way things have been. If that is you, I want you to know that uncertainty is not weakness. It is an honest response to a genuinely difficult situation. And it deserves a different kind of conversation than either couples therapy or a decision to leave. Discernment counseling, which I describe in the next section, was designed for exactly this place.

And there are people reading this who are ready. Who want to bring their partner in, do the work, and find out what this relationship is capable of becoming when both people are genuinely trying. If that is you, I want you to know that readiness is not naive. It is brave. And the research on what is possible when both partners engage fully is genuinely encouraging.

All three of these people are real. All three deserve to be met exactly where they are.

The question of whether to stay is not one I can answer for you. But I can sit with you while you find your way to your own answer. And I can make sure that whatever you decide, you make that decision from a place of clarity and support rather than exhaustion and isolation.

That is what the consultation is for.

What Discernment Counseling Offers

There is a particular form of couples work that I want to name here, because it is specifically designed for the situation where one partner is more ambivalent than the other about whether the relationship has a future.

It is called discernment counseling.

Unlike standard couples therapy, which assumes both partners are committed to working on the relationship, discernment counseling is designed for couples who are uncertain. Where one partner may be leaning toward leaving and the other is leaning toward staying. Where the question is not yet "how do we fix this?" but "should we even try?"

Discernment counseling is not about making the decision for you. It is about slowing down enough to make the decision well. To understand what has actually happened in the relationship, what each partner's role in it has been, and what would genuinely need to change for the relationship to become something different.

For couples navigating ARS, where one partner has often been carrying so much for so long that they are no longer sure what they want, discernment counseling can be a profoundly clarifying experience.

It is worth knowing it exists.

What Healing Asks of You

Whether you pursue couples therapy, individual therapy, discernment counseling, or some combination of all three, there is something that healing from ARS asks of you regardless of path.

It asks you to stop working so hard for love and start letting yourself be met by it.

That sounds simple. It is not simple. For someone who has spent months or years in the Asymmetrical Repair dynamic, the act of not pursuing, of not managing, of not fixing, can feel like free-falling. The alarm system that has kept you vigilant and reaching does not simply switch off because you have decided to try something different.

But it can be recalibrated. Slowly, through real experience of being met. Through a therapeutic relationship where your pain is not minimized or managed but genuinely held. Through the gradual accumulation of evidence that it is possible to be fully known and not abandoned.

That recalibration is what healing actually is. Not the absence of fear. Not the elimination of the wound. But a nervous system that has learned, through experience, that safety is possible. That reaching does not always have to be desperate. That someone can turn toward you.

And that you, simply as you are, are worth turning toward.

The Beginning of a Different Story

Daisy still comes home from work on Tuesdays.

But in the version of the story where healing has happened, something is different.

He looks up from his phone.

Not because she asked him to. Not because she found the right script or became easier or stopped needing things. But because somewhere in the work they did together, he finally understood what his silence had been costing her. And she finally found the words for what she needed that did not come out as accusations, but as genuine reaching.

"I just need to know we're okay."

And he says: "We're okay. Come here."

That is not a perfect relationship. It is a real one. One where both people are present, both people are reaching, and neither person is carrying the whole weight of the bond alone.

That is not every story. Some people do this work and find that what emerges is not a repaired relationship but a clearer sense of themselves and what they need next. That too is healing. That too is a form of the story getting better.

But for those whose partner is willing, whose relationship has something real still breathing underneath the cycle, the version of the story where he looks up, where she does not have to reach desperately just to be seen, where Tuesday evenings are simply Tuesday evenings, that version is possible.

Not without work. Not without sitting through the discomfort of being seen more clearly than you have been, and seeing your partner more clearly too. But possible.

And if something in this four-part series has named something real for you, then you already know that the story you are living right now is not the only one available to you.

There is a different one.

You do not have to find your way to it alone.
