Repair & Forgiveness December 2025 ⏱ 13 min read

What true forgiveness is, and what it is not

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·Forgiveness is one of the most misunderstood words in relationship repair. It is not forgetting, not excusing, not something you owe anyone on a schedule.
  • ·The wound you are trying to forgive is usually not “you did a bad thing.” Underneath, it is “you were not there when I was most helpless.” Most betrayals are felt as abandonment.
  • ·Forgiveness and reconciliation are two different things. Forgiveness does not require the other person’s cooperation, though in a spiritual framework it is rarely purely solo. Reconciliation is a joint project that requires the other person to earn back trust.
  • ·Forgiveness by itself does not rebuild trust. Letting go of resentment is not the same as being able to depend on someone again, and renewed trust is the real goal.
  • ·A real apology is not the words “I’m sorry.” It is a specific set of moves that most quick apologies skip, which is why quick apologies so often make things worse.
  • ·Genuine forgiveness is a process, not a moment, and rarely a straight line. It goes through the wound, never around it.

Before we define it: forgiveness is not owed

If someone has hurt you deeply, you have probably already been handed a lot of advice about forgiveness. Forgive and forget. Let it go. Holding a grudge only hurts you. Some of it comes from people who love you and cannot bear to watch you in pain. Some of it comes from a culture that treats forgiveness as a moral obligation and your reluctance as a character flaw.

I want to start by taking that weight off. You are not a worse person for finding this hard. Forgiveness that is demanded, rushed, or performed to make everyone else comfortable is not the real thing, and it does not heal you. What follows is not a lecture about why you should forgive. It is an honest look at what forgiveness actually is, so that if and when you move toward it, you are moving toward something real.

The wound underneath: this is usually about abandonment

Before we can talk about forgiving an injury, it helps to understand what the injury actually was. And here the attachment researcher Sue Johnson, who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy, changed how I see this work.

When she and her colleagues first studied the hurts that bleed the life out of a relationship, they assumed they would all be betrayals. What they found was subtler. Underneath the betrayal, what the hurt partner keeps returning to is almost always a moment of abandonment: “How could you leave me when I was that vulnerable?” These injuries tend to happen at the times we need each other most: a frightening diagnosis, a miscarriage, the death of a parent, a job lost overnight. And they land as a searing answer to the oldest attachment question: are you there for me when I am most in need? That question is pass or fail. There is very little room for “sort of.”

This is why a seemingly small event can carry such enormous weight, and why “you’re overreacting, it was years ago” is exactly the wrong response. It is not the size of the event that matters. It is the vulnerability it touched. One unanswered moment at the wrong time can eclipse hundreds of good ones.

Johnson gives us a simple way to find the real wound. Under the anger, the hurt partner is usually asking whether, in that moment, they felt one of four things. I think of them as the four D’s:

  • ·Deprived of comfort when they most needed it.
  • ·Deserted, left alone with something too big to carry.
  • ·Devalued, as if they and their feelings did not matter.
  • ·In Danger, suddenly seeing the person who was supposed to be their safe place as a source of harm.

And here is the part that keeps this compassionate: the person who caused the injury is usually not a villain. Most of the time they had good intentions and simply did not know how to tune in, or they were so flooded with their own fear that they went into problem-solving mode and left their partner alone in the feeling. Non-malicious, and still deeply wounding. Both are true. (This is the same fight-or-flight survival wiring I describe in the piece on fight, flight, freeze, and fawn.)

Most betrayals are felt as abandonment, and the weight of a wound comes from the vulnerability it touched, not the size of the event.

What forgiveness is not

Most of the pain around this word comes from things people believe forgiveness requires, that it does not.

  • ·Forgiveness is not forgetting. You do not have to erase the memory or pretend the injury never happened. A healed wound still leaves a scar; the scar is not a failure to forgive. In fact, in the best outcomes, the injury does not disappear at all. It gets woven into the couple’s story as proof that they can be hurt and still find their way back.
  • ·Forgiveness is not excusing. To forgive is not to decide that what happened was acceptable or not that bad. You can hold something as genuinely wrong and still, over time, release its grip on you.
  • ·Forgiveness is not reconciliation. You can forgive someone and never let them back into your life. This one gets its own section.
  • ·Forgiveness is not a feeling you wait to arrive. It is closer to a decision and a direction than a mood, and the feelings usually follow it slowly, not the other way around.
  • ·Forgiveness is not a one-time event. It comes in layers, and it can un-forgive itself on a hard day. That is not backsliding. That is how it works.
  • ·Forgiveness is not owed on a timeline. No one gets to hand you a deadline.

Forgiveness and reconciliation are not the same thing

Here is the distinction that changes everything. Forgiveness and reconciliation are two separate processes that can happen independently.

Forgiveness does not depend on the other person. Much of it is inner work: releasing resentment and putting down the demand for revenge, which you can begin whether or not the other person apologizes, changes, or even knows. But I would not call it purely internal or solo. For many people, and certainly within the spiritual tradition I come from, forgiveness is relational at its heart. It can be worked out between a person and God, and it is often extended toward the person who hurt you, even when reconciliation is off the table. So the honest version is this: forgiveness does not require the other person’s cooperation, but that does not make it something you do entirely alone.

Reconciliation is relational and joint. It is the rebuilding of an actual relationship, and it cannot be done alone. It requires the person who caused the harm to own it, change, and earn back trust over time. Where forgiveness needs no apology, reconciliation absolutely does.

There is a further trap hidden inside reconciliation, and Sue Johnson names it directly: forgiveness by itself does not restore trust. Deciding to let go of your resentment is a real and good thing, but it does not, on its own, make the other person safe to depend on again. That takes a different and harder process.

So the true goal of repair is not simply forgiveness. It is renewed trust, the ability to lean on this person again without bracing for the next injury. Forgiveness opens the door. Rebuilt trust is what lets you walk back through it.

Once you separate these, the trapped feeling often eases. You can forgive a parent and keep them at a distance. You can forgive an ex and never speak to them again. And you can decide to reconcile a marriage after an affair while forgiveness is still very much in process, because reconciliation is built on changed behavior over time, not on you having finished grieving.

The four things people do with an injury

The therapist Janis Abrahms Spring, in her work on forgiveness after betrayal, describes four different responses to being deeply hurt, and I find them clarifying.

The first is cheap forgiveness: forgiving too quickly and too easily, to keep the peace, before the wound has been dealt with at all. It looks generous. It is actually self-abandonment, and the hurt just goes underground.

The second is refusing to forgive: holding onto the injury for the protection or power it seems to offer. It can feel righteous, and it slowly walls you off inside your own bitterness.

The third is acceptance, her quietly radical contribution. When genuine forgiveness is impossible, because the offender will not own what they did or is gone, acceptance is a way to heal anyway, on your own, without needing them to participate.

The fourth is genuine forgiveness, the hardest and most healing. It is not a free gift. It is something the two of you work through together, in which the hurt person gets to express the full weight of their pain and the offender genuinely apologizes, takes responsibility, and earns back trust.

Most of what our culture calls forgiveness is actually cheap forgiveness. Naming which one you are really doing is the first honest step.

Why “just forgive” can be so damaging

When someone forgives to end the discomfort or stop their partner’s guilt, they are usually doing one of two things: cheap forgiveness, or a fawn response.

I want to name that second one plainly. For many people, especially those who learned early that keeping others happy was the price of safety, “I forgive you” comes out almost reflexively in the face of conflict. It is not an act of grace. It is the nervous system reaching for the fastest way to make the danger stop, the same survival instinct as fight or flight, pointed at appeasement.

Sue Johnson describes what happens to injuries that get buried this way, and it is worth hearing. The hurt partner makes a silent vow she calls the “Never Again” moment: never again will I be that open with you. From there they live in a low-grade version of traumatic stress, hypervigilant, avoidant, flooded with the memory one minute and numb the next. The injury does not fade. It festers. Couples can compartmentalize it for a while and go cool and distant, but it breaks back out the moment real closeness is asked for. This is the clinical reason “time heals” is a myth: an unspoken wound is not resting, it is calcifying.

Real forgiveness cannot skip the wound. It goes through it.

What a real apology actually is

If there is one place couples go wrong, it is here. Most people think an apology is the words “I’m sorry.” It is not. Sue Johnson, watching hundreds of these conversations, mapped what separates an apology that heals from one that makes things worse, and the difference is stark.

A genuine apology does five things. The person who caused the hurt lets it land, so you can see the pain actually reaches them. They tell you plainly that your hurt and even your anger are legitimate. They own exactly what they did, not a vague “if I hurt you.” They let you see their shame, that they too are disappointed in how they behaved. And they reassure you that they will help you heal from here.

That last move is what turns an apology from a statement of guilt into an invitation to reconnect. These five moves are the heart of a single step, step four, in the six-step repair arc below.

Against that, look at the apologies that fail, the ones Johnson calls token apologies. There is the four-second exit apology: “Yeah, sorry about that, what’s for dinner?” The minimizing apology: “Well, maybe I did, but you also…” The forced apology: “I guess I’m supposed to say sorry.” And the instrumental apology: “Nothing’s going to work until I say this, so, sorry.” Each one is really about ending the discomfort of the apologizer, not tending the wound of the injured. And each one, in a serious injury, adds insult to it. A hurt partner can always feel the difference between an apology aimed at their pain and one aimed at the exit.

If you are the one apologizing and this feels impossibly hard, there is a reason. A real apology asks you to sit in shame without fleeing it, and to let your partner’s pain change you. That is genuinely difficult. But it is the difference between words and repair.

How healing actually happens: the repair arc

Forgiveness in a couple is not a single conversation, but it does have a shape. Sue Johnson lays out the steps of what she calls the dance of forgiveness and reconciliation, and I use a version of it in the room. Here it is in plain terms.

  1. 1Name the wound. The hurt partner names the pain simply, as a description rather than a case for the prosecution: here is what happened, and here is what it did to my sense of safety with you.
  2. 2Stay present and take it in. The injuring partner stays emotionally present and acknowledges both the pain and their part in causing it. Until the hurt is truly seen, it cannot be put down.
  3. 3Lower the guard. The hurt partner begins to lower the guard they had put up, the silent “Never Again” vow to never be that exposed again, and risks showing the depth of the grief underneath the anger.
  4. 4Offer a real apology. The injuring partner takes real ownership and offers the genuine apology described above.
  5. 5Ask for what you need now. The hurt partner names what they need now to feel safe and asks for it directly, and the partner responds in exactly the way they could not the first time.
  6. 6Build a new story. Together they build a new story out of it, sometimes with concrete rituals of reassurance, so the injury becomes evidence that they can rupture and repair rather than a landmine they tiptoe around.

Notice that safety comes first and forgiveness comes late. You cannot run this while the relationship still feels dangerous, which is why so many couples need help holding it. But when it works, the injury does not just get forgiven. It gets metabolized into the bond.

A few myths worth dropping

Almost every couple carries at least one of these. The most common is that saying sorry settles it: “I apologized, so why are you still upset?” But an apology is an opening, not a payment that buys forgiveness. A quick sorry aimed at ending the discomfort usually deepens the hurt rather than closing it. What actually rebuilds trust is not the words, it is changed behavior repeated over time.

A close second is the belief that forgiving means forgetting, or quietly agreeing it was not that bad. It does not. You can remember an injury clearly, still hold it as genuinely wrong, and gradually release its grip on you. And no one is owed your forgiveness on a timeline, least of all someone who is not sorry.

Then there is the assumption that forgiving and reconciling are the same move, or that being back under one roof means you are healed. They are separate. You can forgive and keep your distance, and you can be fully reunited while still living on top of a buried wound, which is the most fragile place of all.

And the one that keeps couples most stuck: “it was small, so you are overreacting.” The size of the pain is set by the vulnerability the moment touched, not by how the event looks from the outside. Waving it off as trivial is usually the very thing that turns a passing hurt into a lasting one.

Being back under one roof is not the same as being healed. Reunion on a buried wound is the most fragile place of all.

When the other person will not participate

Not every story includes a remorseful partner. Sometimes the person who hurt you will never own it, or is gone, or unsafe. Genuine, mutual forgiveness is off the table. This is exactly where acceptance matters. You are not stuck choosing between forced forgiveness and permanent bitterness. You can work through the injury on your own, grieve what happened and what you will not get, make meaning of it, and gradually loosen its hold on your daily life, without ever needing them to earn it. That is real, hard-won healing too.

A word for the one who caused the harm

If you are the person who did the wounding, there is a forgiveness question for you too, and it is easy to get wrong. Self-forgiveness is not rushing to feel better so the guilt will stop, and it is not letting yourself off the hook. It comes last, not first, and it is earned the same way your partner’s forgiveness is: by facing what you did honestly, staying present for its impact, and changing. The urge to forgive yourself quickly, so the shame will lift, is its own cheap forgiveness. Your steadiness in that discomfort is part of the repair.

The hopeful part

None of this is meant to make forgiveness sound impossible. Sue Johnson’s point, and mine, is the opposite: there is no injury-proof relationship, and the couples who learn they can rupture and genuinely repair end up more securely bonded than they were before the injury, because they now have proof they can survive a rupture. The goal was never a relationship where no one ever steps on anyone’s toes. It is a relationship where, when you do, you know the way back.

Further reading: Sue Johnson, Hold Me Tight; and Janis Abrahms Spring, How Can I Forgive You?

If there is an injury the two of you keep circling and never quite heal, you do not have to keep having the same conversation alone.

Common questions

What is the difference between forgiveness and reconciliation?

Forgiveness does not require the other person to apologize, change, or even know: much of it is inner work, releasing resentment and the demand for revenge, and it can begin one-sidedly. It is not purely solo, though; in many traditions, including a spiritual understanding, forgiveness is also relational, extended toward God and toward the person who hurt you. Reconciliation is different: it is relational and joint, the rebuilding of an actual relationship, and it requires the person who caused the harm to own it, change, and earn back trust. You can forgive without reconciling, and you should not reconcile without genuine repair.

Does forgiveness rebuild trust?

Not by itself. Deciding to let go of resentment is real and good, but it does not automatically make someone safe to depend on again. Renewed trust is a separate process, built through changed behavior and consistency over time. This is why the goal of repair is not just forgiveness but rebuilt trust.

What makes an apology real?

A genuine apology does five things: the pain visibly lands on the person who caused it; they affirm that your hurt and anger are legitimate; they own exactly what they did; they let their own shame show; and they commit to helping you heal. Quick 'token' apologies, the ones aimed at ending discomfort, usually skip these and end up deepening the wound.

Do I have to forgive someone who is not sorry?

No. You are not morally obligated to forgive an unrepentant person. Genuine forgiveness is a mutual process the offender takes part in. When they will not, a healthier path than forced forgiveness or permanent bitterness is acceptance: working through the injury and releasing its grip on your own.

Does forgiveness mean forgetting or excusing what happened?

No. You do not have to forget the injury or decide it was acceptable. You can remember it clearly, hold it as wrong, and still release its hold on your inner life. In healthy repair the injury is not erased; it becomes part of the couple's story as proof they can be hurt and recover.

Is it bad to forgive quickly?

Often, yes. Forgiving too fast, to keep the peace, is 'cheap forgiveness.' The wound goes underground and hardens into resentment. For people who learned to appease to stay safe, fast forgiveness can also be a fawn response rather than a free choice. Real forgiveness goes through the wound, not around it.

Why does a small incident hurt so much?

Because the pain is set by the vulnerability the moment touched, not by the size of the event. Attachment injuries usually happen when we most need our partner, and they answer the question 'are you there for me?' with a searing no. That is why 'it was years ago' or 'it was nothing' misses the point and tends to make the hurt worse.

Can you forgive a partner after an affair and still stay together?

Yes, but the two are separate tracks. You can commit to rebuilding the marriage while forgiveness is still in process, because reconciliation is built on the involved partner's changed behavior over time, not on the hurt partner having finished grieving. What makes it work is genuine repair: the wound fully voiced, a real apology, and trust rebuilt.
