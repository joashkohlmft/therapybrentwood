How long does couples therapy take?

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Triple-Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

The honest answer

  • ·It depends — and that is not a cop-out. Real couples are complicated. They arrive burned out, exhausted, sometimes already half-decided. The research gives a number; lived experience gives a range.
  • ·Early relief — most couples feel a meaningful shift in how conflict feels within the first 4–8 sessions of couples work.
  • ·Full process — typically 3 to 6 months for couples who engage genuinely. More for those carrying significant trauma, betrayal, or years of entrenched patterns.
  • ·EFT outcomes hold — research shows gains continue to deepen at two-year follow-up. You do not revert when therapy ends.

But the question almost never means what it literally says. Before anything else, it is worth sitting with what is actually being asked — because that is often the more important conversation.

What you’re really asking

"Can we afford this?"

Before we talk about sessions, let’s look at the other side of the ledger. An uncontested divorce in Nashville runs around $8,000. A contested divorce — the kind that happens when communication has broken down so completely that a judge has to make the decisions — can reach $40,000 or more. That’s before accounting for the cost to children, to both careers, and to the years that follow.

A full course of EFT couples therapy typically costs $2,700 to $3,600 — less than half the cost of an uncontested divorce, and a fraction of a contested one. The difference is not just what you spend. It is what you have when it is over.

"Is our relationship actually fixable?"

This is the harder question, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a cheerful one.

Several things shape whether couples therapy works. One of the most important is the couple’s own belief that it can. John Gottman’s research on Positive and Negative Sentiment Override is striking: couples who still hold fundamental goodwill toward each other — who interpret their partner’s ambiguous behavior charitably rather than suspiciously — have substantially better outcomes. When hurt has accumulated long enough, you start reading your partner’s neutral expression as contempt. That lens is hard to work through. Not impossible, but it requires more.

Motivation matters. So does the presence of children — their ages, their needs, and what a separation would mean for the parenting relationship. Whether either partner has ever witnessed a healthy relationship modeled matters too; couples who are learning a new relational language from scratch face a different kind of work than those repairing what was once good.

I am among the most highly trained couples therapists in this area, with extensive experience working with complex situations — high-conflict couples, couples navigating betrayal, couples where one partner has nearly given up. I say this not to make a sale, but because you deserve to know what you are bringing your relationship into. I have sat with couples who arrived completely hopeless, and I have watched something real change between them. The model works, and I know how to use it.

Shame about needing help at all.

“Are we really so broken that we need a professional to tell us how to talk to each other?”

I understand this feeling more than you might expect. Shame is something I have lived with — growing up in Malaysia, navigating the gap between what you show the world and what you carry privately. In many Asian cultures, the expectation is that family matters stay inside the family, that struggling means failing, that asking for help is a sign of weakness rather than wisdom. That is not a distant cultural observation for me. It is something I continue to work through in my own ongoing growth.

So I want to say this without hedging: needing help is not evidence of failure. The couples who come to me are not people who didn’t care enough. They are people who cared so much that the pain of the distance became unbearable. Showing up and saying “this matters to me” is not weakness. It is the most courageous thing I see people do.

What I can tell you is this: there is nothing you have done, nothing that has happened between you, that will make me think less of you for being here. The courage it takes to show up is something I will never take for granted.

"When will the pain stop?"

This question lives underneath almost every other one. And I want to answer it directly: soon, and sooner than most couples expect.

The pain, when you look at it closely, is also telling you something important. It is proportional to how much you care. People do not grieve relationships that never mattered. The depth of the hurt is a signal, not a sentence.

What I know from working deeply with couples in real crisis is that healing begins early — often within the first few sessions of actual couples work. Not complete healing. Not the end of difficulty. But something real shifts: a conversation that goes differently than it would have before, a moment where one partner is genuinely received instead of deflected, a de-escalation that used to feel impossible. When both partners bring their authentic emotions, their genuine commitment, and a willingness to be truly seen — the process moves. I will always be looking to relieve your pain as quickly as the relationship safely allows. I will guide you through this.

The depth of the hurt is a signal, not a sentence. People do not grieve relationships that never mattered.

Hope-seeking.

Some couples ask “how long” because they need the answer to be short enough to justify saying yes. They are not really asking about logistics. They are looking for permission to begin.

I want to give it directly: there is hope. I am genuinely excited to work with the couples who come to me — not as a performance of optimism, but because I have seen what this process is capable of across a wide range of situations, including the ones that looked least promising on paper. I move at whatever pace you need. My goal is to focus on your relationship and the pace it needs to heal, not a generic protocol.

What the timeline actually looks like

EFT research typically shows 8 to 20 sessions for couples in clinical trials. But those studies use carefully screened samples — couples without major trauma, without affairs in progress, without one partner who has already emotionally left. Real couples are different. They come in exhausted from years of trying. Some arrive burned out, skeptical that anything will change. Some are here because their partner asked them to come before filing for divorce — checking a box rather than genuinely seeking repair. That context matters enormously for how the work unfolds and how long it takes.

What I can say honestly is this:

4–8

sessions of couples work before most people feel something real beginning to shift

3–6

months for couples who engage genuinely — longer for complex situations

2 yrs

follow-up at which EFT gains continue to hold and deepen (ICEEFT Research Summary)

What shortens the process: both partners genuinely want to be there, patterns have not been entrenched for decades, there is no active betrayal requiring its own processing, and sessions are held consistently.

What extends it: years of accumulated distance, significant individual trauma, a recent affair or major breach of trust, one partner who is present in body but not in commitment. These are not disqualifying — they are honest variables. And for couples who need to move faster than weekly sessions allow, the intensive format compresses months of work into days.

A note on waiting: Research suggests couples wait an average of six years from when significant problems emerge before seeking help. By that point, patterns are more entrenched, trust has eroded further, and emotional distance has become habitual. Waiting longer does not make the work impossible. It does make it longer. If you are reading this and something in your relationship is already not right, earlier is almost always better.

The intensive option

For couples who cannot wait months for weekly sessions to accumulate, or who are in acute crisis, or who simply want to accelerate the early phases of work, the therapy intensive changes the calculation entirely.

A two-day intensive achieves in concentrated hours what weekly therapy might take several months to reach. It creates a different kind of container — deeper, more focused, less fragmented by the week-to-week interruptions of ordinary life. For couples navigating a recent betrayal, or for whom the distance has become so great that weekly sessions feel too slow, it is often the most efficient path.

The intensive is not right for every couple. But for the right couple at the right moment, it is transformative.

What “done” actually means

Therapy ends not when you stop having conflict — but when you have enough of a secure base to navigate conflict on your own. When you can reach for each other and be received. When the cycle is no longer running your relationship; you are.

This is worth saying explicitly: EFT outcomes hold. Research consistently shows they are sustained and continue to improve at two-year follow-up. You do not revert when therapy ends. Because what changed is not your behavior — it is the emotional architecture underneath it. That is what makes EFT genuinely different from approaches that teach skills or give homework. The change is structural. It stays.

The question is not really how long therapy takes.

The question is whether the relationship is worth investing in. And the fact that you are here, reading this, suggests you already know the answer.

Common questions

How soon will we feel a real difference?

Earlier than most couples expect. The de-escalation phase — when conflict begins to feel less intense and the cycle starts to slow — typically begins within sessions 4 to 8. This is not the end of the work, but it is real. Most couples describe a noticeable change in how certain conversations feel, even before the deeper attachment restructuring has begun.

What if one of us is more committed than the other?

Asymmetry in motivation is common and does not disqualify a couple from meaningful work. Some of my most significant cases have involved couples where one partner arrived very reluctant. Motivation often shifts once each person begins to feel genuinely understood in the room — which tends to happen early in EFT. The free consultation is a good place to voice doubts before committing.

We are already thinking about separation. Is it too late?

Not necessarily. Couples who are uncertain about the future — where one or both partners is leaning out — often benefit most from discernment counseling rather than couples therapy. Discernment counseling is not about fixing the relationship; it is about gaining clarity on whether to try. It exists specifically for couples in that painful place of uncertainty.

Can we take a break and come back?

Yes, though breaks during the de-escalation phase tend to slow momentum. Once the couple has completed that stage and reached a more stable relational baseline, pausing becomes easier without losing ground. I will let you know where we are and what a break would mean at any given point in the work.

How does an intensive compare to weekly sessions on time?

A two-day intensive can compress what might take several months of weekly sessions to achieve. It is particularly useful for couples in acute crisis, those with demanding schedules, or those who want to move through the early phases quickly before returning to a weekly cadence. It is not right for every situation, but for the right couple it dramatically shortens the overall timeline.
