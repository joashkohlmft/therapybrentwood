The Brakes Nobody Told You About: What’s Actually Happening When Sex Disappears from a Relationship

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

People do not walk into a therapist’s office and say: I have an attachment wound that is showing up in my bedroom.

They say things like this.

“We haven’t had sex in eight months and I don’t know how to bring it up.”

“I want it, she doesn’t. Or maybe I want it too much. I can’t even tell anymore.”

“He says he’s not broken, so what does that make me?”

“Every time we do have sex it feels like I’m the only one who actually showed up.”

These are the real questions. And most of the answers people find, online, in books, even in therapy, treat the bedroom as if the problem lives there. It almost never does.

Here are six misreadings that consistently send couples in the wrong direction, and what attachment science, sex research, and EFT say about what is actually happening.

The Six Misreadings

Six ways conventional wisdom misreads the problem, and keeps couples looking for the answer in the wrong room.

  1. 1. “We have a desire problem.”
  2. 2. “We need better communication about sex.”
  3. 3. “One of us is the sexual pursuer and it’s becoming a problem.”
  4. 4. “My partner says they love me but doesn’t want to be physical.”
  5. 5. “We’ve tried everything.”
  6. 6. “Is it normal that we barely have sex anymore?”
1

In brief

  • ·Low desire is almost never a physical problem or a sign that love has faded, it is almost always a safety signal from the nervous system.
  • ·Emily Nagoski’s research identifies two systems governing desire: accelerators and brakes. Most desire problems are brake problems, not accelerator problems.
  • ·The primary brake is emotional safety in the relationship, conflict, disconnection, and unresolved attachment injuries suppress desire even when both partners want to reconnect.
  • ·EFT addresses the root cause, the emotional safety of the bond, rather than treating desire in isolation from the relationship.

“We have a desire problem.” No. You have a safety problem.

What most people are told “Schedule sex. Explore new techniques. Check your hormone levels. Read Mating in Captivity.”

Desire discrepancy is one of the most commonly searched relationship topics online, and the most reliably misdiagnosed problem in therapy. None of the standard advice is useless. But here is what it misses entirely.

Research on attachment and sexuality consistently shows that desire does not operate in a vacuum. It operates inside a relational climate. When two people feel genuinely safe with each other, seen, valued, emotionally accessible, desire tends to emerge naturally. When they do not, the body quietly closes the door, often before the person even consciously registers why.

Emily Nagoski, sex researcher and author of Come as You Are, gives us the most useful model for understanding this: the dual control model of sexual response. Every person has a sexual excitation system, the accelerator, that responds to sexually relevant stimuli. And a sexual inhibition system, the brakes, that responds to perceived threats and reasons not to be aroused.

Key term: Dual control model. A model of sexual response developed by John Bancroft and Erick Janssen, popularized by Emily Nagoski. Two systems operate simultaneously: an excitation system (accelerator) that responds to sexually relevant cues, and an inhibition system (brakes) that responds to threats or reasons not to be aroused. Most sexual difficulties are brake problems, not accelerator problems.

Both systems are always active. The balance between them shapes everything.

Diagram: Emily Nagoski’s Dual Control Model of Sexual Response

Most sexual difficulties are not a broken accelerator. They are brakes that are too sensitive, pressed by relational fear, unresolved conflict, or the quiet erosion of emotional safety. And you cannot step on the gas hard enough to override brakes that are responding to a genuine threat signal in the relationship.

The partner who “doesn’t want sex” is not broken. Their nervous system has simply stopped trusting the encounter enough to fully arrive in it.

Most sexual difficulties are not about a broken accelerator. They are about brakes that are too sensitive, and the brakes are almost always pressed by something relational.
Research: Emily Nagoski, PhD

Spontaneous vs. Responsive Desire

Many people assume that healthy sexual desire appears spontaneously, the way hunger does. In reality, a significant proportion of people, particularly those in long-term relationships, primarily experience responsive desire: desire that emerges in response to erotic context rather than before it. Responsive desire is not low desire. It is not a problem. It is simply a different, equally healthy way desire works. Misunderstanding this difference causes enormous suffering in couples, and significant relief when it is finally named. Source: Nagoski, E. (2015). Come As You Are. Simon & Schuster.

2

“We need better communication about sex.” Actually, you need a safer relationship first.

What most people are told “Talk about your preferences. Fill out desire inventories. Schedule structured touch exercises. Use sensate focus.”

Sex therapists have known for decades that couples who can talk openly about sex tend to have better sex. This is true. But here is where the standard approach goes sideways: the homework it assigns assumes that communication is a skill problem. For many couples, it is not.

Sensate focus, the gold standard of behavioral sex therapy since Masters and Johnson in the 1970s, has a meaningful limitation that researchers have quietly been noting for years. When couples are caught in emotional disconnection, behavioral techniques alone produce substantially poorer outcomes. Clinical research suggests success rates can drop below fifty percent when the underlying attachment bond has not been addressed. The body will not receive an instruction that the nervous system is reading as unsafe. You cannot technique your way into vulnerability.

What the attachment lens offers is a completely different starting question, not what do you want in bed, but do you feel safe enough to want anything at all?

Diagram: How Emotional Safety Shapes Sexual Desire
3

“One of us is the sexual pursuer and it’s becoming a problem.” Yes. And this is not just about sex.

What most people are told “One partner has higher desire. This is a mismatch you need to manage and negotiate.”

One of the most important concepts to enter clinical work on sexuality in recent years is the idea of parallel cycles, the emotional pursue/withdraw dance and the sexual pursue/withdraw dance running simultaneously and reinforcing each other.

When the emotional pursuer is also the sexual pursuer, and the emotional withdrawer is also the sexual withdrawer, the dynamic doubles. The pursuer absorbs a double rejection. The withdrawer feels double the pressure. And what looks on the surface like mismatched libidos is actually two people caught in the same negative cycle, playing it out in the bedroom as well as the living room.

Diagram: The Parallel Cycles: Emotional & Sexual Pursue-Withdraw

The sexual pursuer is not obsessed with sex. Underneath the urgency, they are asking the most human of questions: Do you still want me? Am I enough? Am I safe here with you? Sex, for them, is not just pleasure, it is attachment made tangible. And the withdrawer is not withholding. They are often someone for whom emotional vulnerability feels so risky that the body simply cannot open without significant safety first.

Until the cycle is named and interrupted, no amount of new moves in the bedroom will reach what this is actually about.

Research: George Faller, LMFT & Dr. Laurie Watson, LMFT, LPC

Brave Love, Great Sex: The Parallel Cycles Framework

George Faller, one of EFT’s leading trainers, and certified sex therapist Dr. Laurie Watson have developed the concept of parallel emotional and sexual pursue-withdraw cycles in their clinical work together, explored through their podcast Foreplay Radio: Couples & Sex Therapy. Their framework names what most couples cannot see on their own: that the emotional and sexual cycles are not separate problems but the same relational dynamic expressed in two arenas simultaneously. Addressing one without the other leaves the work incomplete.

4

“My partner says they love me but doesn’t want to be physical.” This is not a contradiction.

What most people are told “If they love you, they should want to be intimate with you. Something is wrong, with them, with you, or with the relationship.”

This is one of the most confusing and quietly devastating experiences in a long-term relationship. Your partner says the right things. Nothing catastrophic has happened. And yet physical intimacy has slowly drained away, and you are left wondering what that means about you, about them, about the future.

Attachment research offers a distinction that most people have never been given: the difference between solace sex, sealed-off sex, and synchrony sex.

Clinical Framework: Attachment & Sexual Response

Three Types of Sex in Relationships

Solace sex, one partner reaches for physical contact to soothe anxiety or prove the relationship is still intact. It can feel reassuring but rarely feels fully alive. Sealed-off sex, the avoidant partner is technically present but emotionally elsewhere; the encounter feels hollow for both. Synchrony sex, two people are genuinely present, connected, and safe. This is the only kind associated with mutual satisfaction and erotic aliveness. Synchrony sex does not require novelty or technique. It requires the emotional ground to be solid enough that both people can fully arrive. This framework draws on the research of Sue Johnson and the sexual wellbeing work of Emily Nagoski.

When someone says “I love you but I’m just not in the mood,” what is often actually happening is that the emotional bond has become thin enough that the body is no longer free to fully arrive. That is not a statement about attraction. It is a statement about the state of the connection. And connection can be rebuilt.

When the emotional ground beneath two people erodes, the body reads the intimacy as unsafe, long before the mind catches up. What looks like a desire problem is almost always a bond problem.
5

“We’ve tried everything.” Have you tried the one thing behavioral approaches almost never touch?

What most people are told “Here is more homework. A new framework. Another book. A structured exercise for Tuesday night.”

Structured homework. Intimacy schedules. Books promising the secrets to reigniting passion. These are the tools most couples cycle through before they arrive in a therapist’s office feeling genuinely hopeless. And to be clear: many have real value for couples whose emotional bond is essentially secure. For those couples, behavioral support can be enough.

But for couples where the emotional ground has become unsafe, all the behavioral scaffolding in the world cannot do the work that only the bond itself can do.

The best four-letter word for intimacy is not the one you might expect. It is talk, not performance talk, not technique talk, but the kind where two people actually tell the truth about what it feels like to be them, in this relationship, in this moment. That kind of vulnerability is where erotic aliveness actually lives. Secure attachment does not kill eroticism, as the popular myth insists. The research shows it is the primary condition under which eroticism can actually thrive.

This is also where EFT-informed sex and intimacy therapy differs from traditional approaches. Rather than asking what you want to do differently in the bedroom, it asks what is happening between you, at the level of safety, vulnerability, and emotional risk, that makes the bedroom feel like a place where something is at stake rather than a place where you can simply arrive.

6

“Is it normal that we barely have sex anymore?” Yes. And it is also worth understanding why.

What most people are told “This is very common in long-term relationships. Here are some tips to get back on track.”

This is the most frequently asked, most quietly desperate question typed into search bars late at night. And the answer most often returned is some variation of: this is normal, here are some tips.

What is almost never said is this: the frequency of sex in a relationship is often a fairly accurate barometer of the emotional safety in that relationship. Not a perfect one, hormones, health, stress, parenting, grief, medication all play their part. But when the emotional bond is genuinely secure, most couples find a way back to physical intimacy through the natural pull of that security. When the bond has eroded, sex tends to erode with it, often quietly, without either partner fully understanding what is happening or why.

This is what an attachment-focused approach to sexual intimacy addresses that traditional sex therapy often does not: not the frequency or the technique, but the felt sense of being truly wanted, truly seen, and truly safe with the person on the other side of the bed.

That felt sense is not a bonus feature of a good relationship. It is the foundation everything else is built on. And it is something that can be worked toward, even when the distance has been there for a long time.

Where This Work Actually Happens

Sexual intimacy and emotional intimacy are not two separate conversations. They are the same conversation, spoken in different registers. When couples come to sex and intimacy therapy at this practice, the work begins not in the bedroom but in the space between two people, in the patterns of reaching and pulling back, in the places where one person stopped risking and the other stopped noticing.

EFT-informed therapy works at the level of the emotional bond: rebuilding safety, naming the cycle, creating the kind of corrective emotional experience that allows the body to finally feel safe enough to arrive. It draws on the science of attachment, the dual control model, and the lived reality of what it costs to be vulnerable with someone when you are not sure whether reaching will be worth it.

If you have read this far, something in here probably resonates. You are not broken. And you are not past the point where things can change.

The door does not have to stay closed. Whatever has happened in the space between you and your partner, the emotional ground can be rebuilt, and desire tends to follow it.
