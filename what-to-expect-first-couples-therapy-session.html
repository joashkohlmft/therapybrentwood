What to expect in your first couples therapy session

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Triple-Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·Session 1 is a conjoint intake, both partners together. The therapist listens, maps the negative cycle, and offers the first reframe: it’s not you vs. your partner.
  • ·Sessions 2 & 3 are individual, one with each partner. A thorough attachment history and space for what didn’t surface in the first session.
  • ·Session 4 onwards is where couples therapy proper begins, the EFT de-escalation phase.
  • ·Nobody will be blamed. EFT is structurally designed to prevent it.
  • ·Situations involving trauma, a recent affair, or significant complexity may require additional intake sessions before couples work begins.

The fear most couples carry in

Most couples walk into the first session braced for something that never happens. They expect to be asked to recreate their worst argument. They expect the therapist to decide who is the problem. They expect to feel judged, exposed, or worse off than when they arrived.

None of that is what happens here.

My approach to therapy, and to people, is grounded in a belief I hold without qualification: everyone who walks through the door genuinely wants to change. Not perform change. Not manage their partner better. Actually change. The defenses, the reactivity, the shutdowns and the criticisms, these are not character flaws. They are protective strategies that made sense at some point and are now getting in the way of the connection both partners want. My job is to be curious about that, not judgmental.

EFT as a model is built the same way. It does not look for the villain. It does not adjudicate arguments or assign fault. What it looks for, from the very first session, is the pattern both partners are caught in together. That shift in framing changes everything about how therapy feels.

Before the first session: the free consultation

The process starts before any session, with a complimentary 20-minute consultation. This is a genuine conversation, not a formality. I want to understand what’s happening. You need to decide whether I’m the right fit. You can ask me anything: how I work, what EFT actually involves, what the early sessions look like, whether I have experience with your specific situation.

Many couples arrive with one partner more reluctant than the other. The consultation is especially useful for that partner, a chance to hear directly how the process works, ask whatever they need to ask, and decide whether to trust it before committing.

There is no pressure and no obligation. Just an honest conversation.

The session-by-session structure

One of the things I want to be transparent about is how the early phase of EFT actually unfolds. Most couples expect to start “doing therapy” in the first session together. What actually happens is more deliberate than that, and more useful.

Diagram: Your First Few Sessions, Step by Step
1

Session 1: Conjoint intake (both partners together)

The first session is an assessment. I am listening, watching, and beginning to understand three things: what brought you here, how you interact with each other, and what is happening underneath the surface. Before the session I’ll send intake paperwork, informed consent and a questionnaire asking about your history, what’s bringing you in, and what each of you hopes to change. Fill it out honestly. There are no wrong answers.

In the session itself, I’ll ask each of you to share what brings you in, in your own words, at your own pace. What I’m listening for is less the specific content and more the emotional texture: what matters to each of you, how you describe each other, what you reach for when things get hard. By the middle of the session I’m already forming a picture of your negative cycle, the recurring pattern of moves and countermoves that leaves both of you feeling worse. I will usually begin to name this out loud before the session ends.

The most important moment in many first sessions is this reframe: the cycle is the problem, not each other. It is not you vs. your partner. It is the two of you against a pattern you have both been caught in. Many couples describe this as the first time in a long time they felt like they were on the same side.

2

Session 2: Individual intake with Partner A

After the first conjoint session, I meet with each partner individually. Session 2 is with one partner; session 3 is with the other. These individual sessions exist for a specific purpose: to take a thorough individual attachment history with each of you, tracing the experiences and patterns that shaped how you show up in close relationships. This context, your early attachment experiences, your family of origin, the relational templates you carry, is essential for understanding what is happening between you now.

The individual session is also where we cover what didn’t surface in the first conjoint meeting. There is always more. In the room together, both partners are often managing how they come across, protecting each other in ways they may not even notice, or simply running out of time. The individual session gives you space to be fully honest about your own experience.

3

Session 3: Individual intake with Partner B

The same as session 2, now with the other partner. By the end of these two individual sessions, I have a full picture of each person’s history, fears, and the vulnerabilities beneath their reactive behaviors. Sometimes one individual session per partner is enough. In more complex situations, significant trauma history, a great deal of relational history, or things that simply need more time, I may schedule an additional individual session with one or both partners. I will tell you what I think is needed as we go.

4

Session 4 onwards: Couples therapy begins

This is where couples therapy proper starts, the de-escalation phase of EFT. By now I have a clear picture of your negative cycle, each partner’s attachment history, and the vulnerabilities underneath the reactive behaviors. The foundation is laid. The work that begins here is about gaining emotional insight and a corrective emotional experience, so both of you can begin to see the cycle rather than simply be inside it, to name it, understand it together, and stop letting it run the relationship.

This is not dramatic work, but it is foundational. Many couples notice meaningful shifts during this phase even before the deeper relational restructuring begins, a conversation that goes differently, a moment where someone reaches and is actually received, a de-escalation that used to feel impossible.

A note on complications: Some situations require more intake sessions before moving into the couples work. A recently disclosed affair, a major breach of trust, a history of complex trauma in one or both partners, these require a more careful, extended groundwork phase. This is not a delay. Rushing into couples work before the therapeutic foundation is stable enough to hold it does more harm than good. I will always be honest with you about where we are and why.

The no-secrets policy, and why it protects you

Before the individual sessions begin, there is something I want to name clearly: I hold a no-secrets policy in couples therapy.

This means that significant information shared in an individual session cannot be held privately within the therapy process. I want to explain why before you reach the individual sessions, not as a warning but as a matter of transparency: in couples therapy, the relationship is the client, not either individual. If I were to carry a significant secret from one partner while sitting in the room with both of you, I would no longer be working for the relationship. I would be working around it.

The no-secrets policy is not about pressure to disclose everything immediately, or surveillance of private thoughts. It is about making sure that the therapeutic work we do together is built on ground that can actually hold it. If something comes up in an individual session that needs to find its way into the couples work, we will talk about how and when to do that together, gently, at the right pace, with both of us thinking about what the relationship needs.

What the first session is not

It is not a debate. I am not deciding who was right about a specific argument. It is not a communication skills workshop. I will not give you homework on “I feel” statements or active listening exercises. It is not a verdict on whether your relationship is worth saving. That is your decision, not mine. And it is not a test of whether you are doing therapy correctly. There is no correct way to show up. Wherever you are, that is the right place to start.

What you should not feel, at any point, is blamed, ganged up on, or worse than when you walked in. If you do, say so. To me, directly. Good therapy requires honesty in both directions, and I can receive it.

How you’ll probably feel afterward

Most couples leave the first session feeling one of two things. The first is relief, a genuine sense that someone finally understands what has been happening between you, and that it has a name, a shape, and a way through. The second is stirred up, raw, more emotional than expected, unsettled in a way that is hard to name. Both are good signs. The relief means the work has already begun. The stirred-up feeling means something real was touched, which is exactly where healing starts.

Most couples describe the first session not as confrontational, but as the first time in a long time they felt like someone understood both of them at once.

Common questions

Do we both have to want therapy equally for it to work?

No. One partner being more reluctant than the other is extremely common. Some of the most meaningful therapeutic work I have done has been with couples where one person started out very uncertain. The free consultation is partly for that partner, to hear directly how the process works and to ask whatever they need to ask.

What if one of us doesn’t feel ready to come back after the first session?

Tell me. Directly, and honestly. It is far more useful to hear that than to have the reluctant partner quietly disengage from the process. If the first session raised concerns or felt off in some way, I want to understand what happened and whether there is a way through it.

Why do you see each of us individually, is that standard in EFT?

Not all EFT therapists do individual intake sessions. I do them because I have found that the quality of the couples work is substantially better when I have a full picture of each person’s attachment history before we begin. What each partner has lived through, what they carry, and what they fear is not always visible in the room with their partner present.

What if we have a crisis before we even begin, like a discovered affair?

The intake process will take longer. A recently disclosed affair is a significant relational trauma, and the groundwork phase needs to be more careful and extended before couples work can begin safely. Couples in acute crisis often benefit most from a therapy intensive, a concentrated format that creates a stable container for the most difficult work.

How is an EFT first session different from Gottman or other approaches?

A Gottman therapist might use the first session to administer formal assessments, discuss specific communication behaviors, or identify the “Four Horsemen” in your interactions. An EFT therapist is doing something different: tracking the emotional undercurrent, mapping the attachment cycle, and beginning to understand the fears beneath the reactive behaviors. The difference is less about what you talk about and more about where the therapist’s attention is directed, and what they are listening for.

You don’t have to have everything figured out before you come in. You don’t need to have a prepared case to make, or know exactly what is wrong, or be ready to be vulnerable in the first hour.

You just have to show up. That is enough to start.
