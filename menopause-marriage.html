Menodivorce: when menopause gets the blame

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

In brief

  • ·The rise in midlife separation is real, and menopause often gets named as the thing that broke the marriage. Biology is usually the trigger, not the cause.
  • ·Perimenopause and menopause are physically real: lower estrogen, sleep loss, hot flashes, brain fog, and a genuine drop in spontaneous desire. This is treatable, and a menopause-informed doctor matters. But medicine treats the body, not the bond.
  • ·What menopause usually does is act as a floodlight. It exposes a slow erosion in a bond the couple managed to ignore while careers and kids filled the room. The change reveals the distance, it rarely creates it.
  • ·The most common wound is a misread: one partner pulls back because of pain, fatigue, or low desire, and the other reads it as rejection. Both then protest, and the cycle does the damage.
  • ·Desire in midlife is mostly responsive, it follows safety and closeness rather than arriving first. You rebuild it by easing the brakes, not by pushing harder. Safety first, then sex.
  • ·You do not need to replace your partner. You need to replace the dance.

The story couples tell

There is a version of this that I hear often, and it usually starts the same way. “We were fine, and then the hormones changed, and everything fell apart.” One partner going through the change feels like a stranger in her own body, exhausted, irritable, not remotely interested in sex, wondering where she went. The other feels shut out, confused, quietly certain he did something wrong or is no longer wanted. And somewhere in there the word divorce gets said out loud for the first time, and it lands like the logical conclusion to a biological event nobody chose.

Let me say the honest thing first, because this deserves it before anything else. What you are feeling in this season is real, and it is disorienting, and you are not imagining how much harder everything got. The physical changes are not small, and the loneliness that can open up in a couple during this stretch is one of the most painful kinds there is. I am not here to correct your experience. I am here to offer a different reading of what is actually happening, because the usual reading, that the hormones ended the marriage, is not quite true, and the truer version has a way through.

The change is real, and it is the trigger, not the cause

Here is the reframe, and I want to hold both halves of it at once.

The biology is real. Perimenopause and menopause are not a mood. They are a genuine physiological shift, and the stress they put on a person and a partnership is legitimate. What they are not, usually, is the actual cause of the divorce. They are the trigger that exposes something that was already there.

Think of it this way. For years, most couples run their relationship on autopilot while careers and children and logistics fill every waking hour. The bond between the two adults quietly goes untended, not out of malice, just out of being busy and tired and outnumbered. It erodes slowly, invisibly, a little each year. And then midlife arrives and pulls the distractions away at the exact moment the body demands attention, and suddenly there is nowhere to look but at each other. Menopause does not manufacture the distance. It switches on a floodlight in a room the couple had been keeping dark.

That is actually good news, even though it does not feel like it. Because a bond that eroded can be rebuilt. A marriage that was quietly starving is a very different problem from a marriage that a hormone destroyed, and it is a far more hopeful one.

A straight note on the biology, because it is real and treatable

The physical side of this deserves real care, not a reframe that waves it away. Perimenopause and menopause bring a drop in estrogen that can mean vaginal dryness and thinning tissue, which is to say sex can genuinely start to hurt, along with disrupted sleep, night sweats, hot flashes, brain fog, mood shifts, and a real decline in the kind of desire that used to arrive on its own. None of that is in your head, and none of it is a character flaw or a sign you have stopped loving your partner.

It is also, in large part, treatable. A menopause-informed clinician (in the US, a provider certified through the Menopause Society, formerly NAMS) can talk with you about options: systemic hormone therapy for symptoms like hot flashes and sleep, low-dose vaginal estrogen for dryness and pain, which acts locally and is low-risk for many women, good lubricants and moisturizers, and sleep and mood support. Please get the physical side evaluated. Just know what the medicine can and cannot do. It can give the body back a great deal. It cannot, by itself, repair the loneliness that has built up between two people. For that you usually need both: the doctor for the body, and the work of turning back toward each other for the bond.

Why now: a bonding crisis at a life transition

There is a reason this so often hits all at once, and it is not bad luck.

Long-term love is not static. It moves through stages, and every major transition, even a welcome one, is a kind of bonding crisis, a moment where the relationship has to be reshaped to fit a new reality or it starts to buckle under it. Menopause rarely arrives alone. It tends to show up in the same few years as the empty nest, as career shifts or retirement, as aging parents who suddenly need care. Any one of these would ask a lot of a couple. Together they are a perfect storm.

The empty nest deserves its own line, because it is often the quiet co-conspirator here. If the children were the main bridge holding two people in daily contact, their leaving does not just make the house quiet. It exposes the gap where the couple’s own connection was supposed to be. Many partners discover, the year the last kid goes, that they have been co-parents and logistics partners for a long time and are not quite sure how to be a couple anymore.

Underneath all of it is a single attachment question, the one our nervous system asks whenever the ground shifts. Are you still there for me? Do I still matter to you, in this new body, this new stage, this new life? That question is not weakness. It is the most human thing there is. And whether a couple can hear it and answer it, underneath all the noise about hormones and sex, is very often what decides which marriages make it through this passage and which ones do not.

The misread that starts the war

Here is where so much of the real damage happens, and it is almost always a misreading.

When the partner going through menopause pulls back, because sex has become uncomfortable, because she is exhausted, because desire simply is not showing up the way it used to, the other partner very often reads that withdrawal as being about them. “She does not want me anymore. She is not attracted to me. She is pulling away because she is done.” That appraisal feels like fact, and it is almost always wrong.

Because from the inside, the withdrawing partner is usually not rejecting anyone. She is protecting. She pulls back to manage her own discomfort, her own fear that she is failing to be what her partner needs, her own grief about a body that is not behaving like it used to. The retreat is a shield, not a verdict. And what it is shielding is often a tender fear: I do not want to disappoint you, and I do not know how to be sexual in this body yet, so I will just avoid the whole thing.

Then the cycle takes over. The one who feels shut out lands in a kind of solitary confinement, and their own attachment alarm starts screaming. So they protest the distance, and protest rarely comes out soft. It comes out as criticism, as sulking, as pressure, sometimes as a running commentary on how long it has been. Which is, of course, the precise opposite of what would help. The pursued partner, already feeling like a disappointment, now feels like a disappointment under audit, and pulls back further. The more one pushes, the more the other retreats, and each retreat convinces the pursuer that the rejection was real all along.

The single most useful thing a couple in this spot can learn is to read the other’s sexual pulling-away not as coldness, but as a protective move with a fear underneath it. There is a reason for the retreat, and the reason deserves to be heard rather than prosecuted.

Keep reaching through the disconnection

The way out does not start in the bedroom. It starts with getting the fear out from behind the armor.

Underneath the criticism and the pressure, the pursuing partner is usually asking one thing: am I still wanted, are we still us? And underneath the withdrawal, the other is usually saying: I am scared I cannot be what you need right now, and I cannot bear to fail you. When those two truths can finally be said plainly, instead of acted out through pressure and avoidance, the whole temperature changes. The couple stops being two people on opposite sides of a rejection and becomes two frightened people who both, it turns out, are still reaching.

That is why the order matters so much. You cannot negotiate your way back to a good sex life while one partner feels like a failure and the other feels like a reject. Safety has to come first. De-escalate the cycle, get each person hearing the fear under the other’s behavior, rebuild the sense that we are on the same side, and only then move toward the physical. Trying to fix the sex before the safety is like trying to plant in frozen ground.

Rebuilding desire on a secure base

Now the practical part, because once safety is coming back, there is real and specific work that helps. And some of it runs against everything the culture taught us about how desire is supposed to work.

Diagram: Two Engines of Desire

Start with how desire actually works in midlife. In early romance, desire tends to be spontaneous. It shows up on its own, like weather, and then leads to closeness. For many people, especially women, and especially in midlife, that flips. Desire becomes responsive. It does not arrive first and drive you toward your partner. It follows closeness, warmth, and a decision to be open, and then arousal catches up. This is not a malfunction. It is the normal, mature shape of desire. But if you are both sitting around waiting to spontaneously feel like it, the way you did at twenty-five, you may wait forever, and read the waiting as proof that it is gone. It is not gone. It just runs on a different engine now, one you start by choosing to step toward each other rather than by waiting to be struck.

Take intercourse off the table, on purpose, for a while. This sounds backward and it is one of the most powerful things a couple can do. Agree, together, that sex is not the destination for now, and rebuild a whole vocabulary of touch that is not a runway to intercourse. Long hugs. Hands on skin. Holding each other in bed with no expectation that it goes anywhere. Prolonged eye contact. Cuddling, stroking, warmth for its own sake. This is sometimes called nondemand touch, and it does two crucial things at once: it soothes two nervous systems that have been braced against each other, and it removes the performance pressure that is one of the single biggest brakes on desire. When touch stops being a test or a demand, the body slowly becomes willing to want again.

Work the brakes, not the gas. Desire has an accelerator and it has brakes, and in midlife the brakes matter far more than the gas. Pain, exhaustion, self-consciousness, the fear of disappointing, the memory of the last time it went badly, these are the brakes, and no amount of pushing the accelerator overcomes a foot jammed on the brake. You rebuild desire by easing the brakes: treating the pain medically, taking the pressure off, making it emotionally safe, going slow. Pleasuring, not pressuring.

Let sex become a shared adventure again, not a pulse-check. When a couple is anxious, sex quietly turns into a test. “If we do it, we are okay. If we do not, we are in trouble.” That turns every encounter into a referendum, which is the least sexy frame imaginable. As safety returns, sex can go back to being what it is meant to be between two people who trust each other: a low-pressure, curious, tender exploration, one that can look different than it did at thirty and still be deeply good. Bodies change. The sexual self does not end. It transitions into something you get to discover together.

Changing the music of your midlife dance

I want to end where the hope actually lives.

The couples who come through this passage, and many do, are not the ones who found a magic hormone or a younger partner. They are the ones who realized the enemy was never the estrogen, and never each other. It was the dance, the old cycle of retreat and pursuit that the change happened to expose. And a dance can be relearned. When emotional safety comes back into the room, the hard practical negotiations of midlife, the sex, the bodies, the new stage of life, stop being a battlefield and become something you figure out on the same side of the table.

You do not need to replace your partner. You need to replace your dance. That is the heart of the work I do in couples therapy, built on Emotionally Focused Therapy, and this passage, as painful as it is, turns out to be one of the more hopeful places to do it, because the two people almost always still want the same thing. They want to matter to each other again. The change did not take that away. It just turned on the lights.

You do not need to replace your partner. You need to replace the dance. When the change stops being a verdict and becomes a passage you cross together, midlife can be where a marriage deepens rather than where it ends.

Common questions

Is menodivorce a real thing, or does menopause actually cause divorce?

Midlife and later-life (“gray”) divorces really are rising, and they often cluster around the menopausal years, so the pattern is real. But menopause is usually the trigger, not the root cause. The physical changes put a marriage under a spotlight, and what breaks is typically a bond that had been quietly eroding for years, not one the hormones destroyed on their own.

Why do so many divorces happen around menopause, and who tends to initiate them?

Later-life divorces are most often initiated by women, frequently ones who are no longer willing to stay in a lonely, “empty-shell” marriage once the distractions of career and child-rearing fall away. Menopause tends to coincide with the empty nest and other midlife transitions, and that pile-up exposes a disconnection that was survivable while everyone was busy and becomes unbearable once the house goes quiet.

My wife lost interest in sex during menopause. Does she still love me?

Almost certainly, and her pulling away is usually not about you. Lower desire in menopause is driven by real physical changes and often by a protective fear of not being able to be what you need right now. Many partners read that withdrawal as rejection when it is actually a shield over vulnerability. The repair is to get curious about the fear underneath, not to take the distance as a verdict.

Is low libido after menopause permanent? Can desire come back?

It very often comes back, though it may work differently than before. Part of the answer is medical (treating pain, dryness, and sleep with a menopause-informed doctor), and part is relational. Desire in midlife tends to be responsive, it follows emotional safety and closeness rather than arriving spontaneously, so couples who rebuild safety and take the pressure off frequently find that desire returns, in a new and often deeper form.

Should we see a doctor or a couples therapist?

Ideally both, in parallel, because they repair different halves of the problem, and treating only one half is a big reason couples in this season stay stuck. A menopause-informed physician gives you back the body: hormone therapy, vaginal estrogen for pain, sleep and mood support. But no medication repairs the loneliness, the rejection misreads, or the pursue-and-retreat cycle that has hardened between you, and that is the half that actually decides whether the marriage makes it. It matters who you see for that part. Generic communication tips and “just date more” advice rarely move a bond this stuck. My work is built on Emotionally Focused Therapy, the most research-supported approach for couples, with roughly three in four distressed couples moving to recovery in the studies, and it is designed for exactly this: getting underneath the surface fights to the attachment fears driving them, calming the cycle so you stop wounding each other, and rebuilding safety and desire on purpose, which general talk therapy usually does not target. As an ICEEFT-certified EFT therapist, helping couples through this specific midlife passage is a large part of what I do. The doctor gives you back your body; this work gives you back each other, and most couples here need both at once.

What is responsive desire?

It is desire that follows closeness rather than preceding it. Instead of a spontaneous urge that strikes and then leads to intimacy, responsive desire is cultivated: you choose to step toward warmth and safe touch, and arousal follows. It is the normal, mature shape of desire for many people, especially in midlife, and understanding it is often the difference between “the spark is dead” and “the spark works differently now.”

We've felt like roommates since the kids left and menopause hit. Is it too late?

It is very rarely too late. What you are describing, drifting into co-managers and near-strangers, is common and treatable, not a death sentence. The bond is usually not gone, it is starved, exposed all at once by the empty nest and the physical change landing together. Couples routinely rebuild real closeness in this stage, and later-life relationships can grow, not just survive.

Can sex actually be good again after menopause?

Yes, though it may look different than it did at thirty. Menopause changes the body; it does not end the sexual self. With the physical side treated, the performance pressure eased, and emotional safety restored, many couples find their way to a sex life that is slower, tenderer, and in some ways more connected than before. It is a transition to work with, not a verdict to accept.
