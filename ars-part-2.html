What Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome Looks Like in the Room: An EFT Perspective

Written by Joash Koh, LMFT · ICEEFT Certified EFT Therapist · Brentwood, Tennessee

Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome — 4-part series

Part 1: When One Partner Carries the Relationship Alone Part 2: What ARS Looks Like in the Room Part 3: You're Not Crazy Part 4: Can ARS Be Healed?

What Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome Looks Like in the Room: An EFT Perspective

This is Part 2 of a four-part series on Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome. If you haven't read Part 1 yet, start there. This piece goes deeper into the clinical picture and is written for therapists and for clients who want to understand what is actually happening beneath the surface of their relationship dynamic.

What the Room Looks Like

They arrive together, which already means something.

She sits forward in her chair. He leans back. She has a list, not literally, but you can feel it, the careful accumulation of incidents, timestamps, the exact words he used on a Thursday three weeks ago. He has his arms loosely crossed, not hostile exactly, more like someone bracing themselves for something they've been through before.

She starts talking. Her voice is tight at first, then faster, then the tears come, and with the tears, an edge. Something that sounds like anger but lands, if you are tracking it carefully, more like grief. More like someone who has been holding a door open for a very long time and is starting to lose feeling in their arm.

He listens. Or he appears to listen. His face does what faces do when they have learned that any visible reaction will be used as evidence. He says: "I don't know what you want from me." He says: "Nothing I do is ever enough." He says: "I'm here, aren't I?"

She says: "Being here isn't the same as being present."

And the cycle, right there, has just completed another rotation.

This is the room. And if you have sat in this room as a therapist for any length of time, you know exactly what I mean when I say it has a particular quality to it. A particular weight. Something that is not quite conflict and not quite silence, but something in between, a space where two people are speaking entirely different emotional languages and neither one knows it yet.

What you are looking at is not simply a pursuer-withdrawer pattern.

You may be looking at Asymmetrical Repair Syndrome.

Whether you are a therapist reading this because a couple in your caseload has been nagging at you, something about them feels different from your other pursue-withdraw couples, or you are a client who just forwarded Part 1 to your therapist and are now trying to find the words for what you cannot seem to make anyone understand, this piece is for both of you. The clinical and the personal are not separate here. They never are. What happens in the therapy room is a mirror of what happens in the bedroom, at the kitchen table, in the car on the way home from another dinner where no one said the thing that needed saying. Understanding the clinical picture does not make the pain more abstract. If anything, it makes it more legible. And legibility is where healing begins.

ARS and the Standard Pursuer-Withdrawer Pattern: What's Different

The pursuer-withdrawer dynamic is one of the most well-researched patterns in couples therapy. It is the most common cycle seen in distressed couples, and it is characterized by one partner moving toward, reaching, demanding, or criticizing, and the other pulling back, shutting down, or going quiet.

In the standard cycle, both partners are doing something adaptive. Both are responding to the same relational threat, the terrifying possibility that the bond between them is not safe, from opposite directions. The pursuer says, with their whole body: come closer, I am frightened. The withdrawer says, with their whole body: I cannot do this without making it worse. Neither partner is the villain. The cycle is the villain.

ARS exists within this framework, but it describes something that has gone further. Something that has calcified.

In a standard pursuer-withdrawer cycle, there is movement on both sides, however distorted and painful. The withdrawer may still initiate repair in other ways, through acts of service, or through physical closeness, or through a quiet "are we okay?" the morning after a fight. The pursuer, while desperate, can sometimes also rest. There are moments of genuine connection that both partners can access, even if those moments are increasingly rare.

In ARS, the asymmetry has become structural. The pursuing partner has become the sole keeper of the relationship's emotional life. They track the health of the bond the way you track a vital sign. They initiate every repair. They carry every rupture. They hold the relationship's history, its wounds, its possibilities, and its grief, essentially alone.

And the withdrawing partner has, over time, learned something dangerous: that the pursuer will always come to them. That waiting is safe. That silence has no consequences, because eventually the other person will break it.

This is the structural difference. And it changes everything about how the cycle functions, and how the injury deepens.

What EFT Sees Beneath the Surface

In Emotionally Focused Therapy, we understand that what couples fight about is almost never what they are actually fighting about. The argument about the dishes, the unanswered text, the tone of voice on a tired Tuesday evening, these are the surface. Beneath them is something much more primal and much more human.

Every negative cycle is, at its core, an attachment drama. A story about two people who desperately need to know they are safe with each other, and who have found themselves in a dance that makes that safety impossible to feel.

To understand ARS through an EFT lens, we need to look at two levels of experience: what is visible in the room, and what is happening beneath it.

What is visible is the secondary, reactive layer. The pursuer's anger. The criticism. The relentless returning to the same wound. The withdrawer's silence. Their flat affect. Their "I don't know what you want from me." These are the behaviors that distressed couples, and sometimes their therapists, fixate on. These are what get labeled toxic, or anxious, or avoidant.

But secondary emotions are, by definition, reactions. They are the emotional equivalent of scar tissue. They form to protect something more vulnerable underneath.

What is beneath the surface is where the real story lives.

Beneath the pursuer's anger is almost always a terror. A bone-deep fear that they are not worth staying for. That they are fundamentally too much, or not enough, or both simultaneously. That the silence means what the worst part of them has always suspected: I don't matter to you.

Beneath the withdrawer's silence is almost always something equally painful. A sense of inadequacy so acute that engagement feels impossible. A fear that anything they say will be wrong, will make it worse, will confirm what their partner already seems to believe about them. An internal voice that says: you can never get it right with her. You are never enough.

In EFT, we call these primary emotions. They are the vulnerable, attachment-related feelings that sit below the reactive surface, and they are the feelings that, when finally accessed and expressed, have the power to transform a cycle rather than simply interrupt it.

In ARS, the clinical challenge is this: the pursuing partner's primary emotions have been buried so deeply beneath years of reactive pursuit, beneath the anger and the urgency and the exhaustion, that even they may have lost access to them. They have been in fight-or-flight for so long that the softer, more vulnerable feeling beneath the alarm, the longing, the grief, the quiet "I just miss you", has become almost unreachable.

And the withdrawing partner, for their part, may not even realize they are in a cycle. From their perspective, they are simply surviving a partner who is always upset, always wanting something, always making them feel like a failure. Their withdrawal is not cruelty. It is a self-protective strategy that made sense once, in some earlier relational context, and has never been updated.

If you are reading this as the person who pursues, I want to pause here and speak directly to you for a moment.

What I just described, the terror beneath the anger, the longing beneath the demand, you may not have words for it yet. You may not even be fully aware it is there. When you have been in alarm for long enough, the softer feeling underneath gets harder and harder to reach. All you know is the urgency. All you feel is the fire.

But the fire is not the whole story. Somewhere beneath it, there is probably something much quieter. Something that sounds less like "why won't you just talk to me" and more like "I miss you. I am scared. I need to know I matter to you."

That quieter thing is what therapy is trying to find. Not to suppress the fire, but to find what the fire has been protecting all along.

The Specific Shape of ARS in the EFT Room

When ARS is present, certain clinical markers tend to appear consistently. These are worth naming, because they shift how you work.

The pursuer carries the relationship's entire emotional memory. In session, they remember every rupture, every failed repair, every moment they reached and found nothing. The withdrawer often genuinely cannot recall specific incidents with the same specificity. This is not always gaslighting, though it can feel that way to the pursuer. It is a reflection of how differently the two partners have been inhabiting the relationship. One has been maintaining meticulous records of a bond in danger. The other has been surviving day to day, not tracking.

The pursuing partner has often lost contact with their own attachment needs. What comes out in session, and in arguments, is protest. Criticism. Frustration. But underneath the protest is a person who is longing, desperately, to simply be held, reassured, known. The therapeutic task is to help them move from the protest into the longing. To find the softer reach beneath the hard demand.

The withdrawing partner is often genuinely bewildered. Not always. But frequently. They experience their partner's intensity as confirmation of their own inadequacy, and their withdrawal as an attempt to de-escalate rather than abandon. They are doing what every overwhelmed nervous system does: shutting down to prevent further damage.

The standard de-escalation tools work slowly, if at all, at first. Because the pursuing partner has been carrying the relationship alone for so long, even small moments of withdrawal in session, the withdrawer looking away, giving a short answer, going quiet, can re-activate the full ARS alarm system. The body does not know it is in a therapy room. It knows it is in the presence of the person who keeps going quiet, and it responds accordingly.

The pursuer often presents as the "difficult" one. They are the one crying, the one with the list, the one whose voice has an edge. In a therapy model that prioritizes de-escalation and behavioral control, they can end up inadvertently identified as the primary clinical problem. This is one of the most important clinical risks in working with ARS couples. The person who is most visibly distressed is not the person who is most responsible for the dynamic.

The Cycle Beneath the Cycle

In ARS, there is a cycle operating at the interpersonal level, between the partners. And there is a second cycle operating inside the pursuing partner, a private internal loop that compounds the injury every time the external cycle completes.

It works like this.

The external alarm fires: partner withdraws. The pursuer reaches. The withdrawer retreats further. The pursuer pushes harder. Something gets said that cannot be unsaid. The withdrawer goes completely silent. The pursuer is left alone with the aftermath.

If you are the one who pursues, you may recognize this moment with painful clarity. Not the argument itself, but what comes after. The strange, hollow quiet when it is finally over. The way you are left sitting with everything that just happened while the other person seems to simply... move on. Sleep. Make coffee. Check their phone.

And then the internal cycle begins.

The pursuer reviews everything they said and did. They find the moments they were too loud, too intense, too much. The Internal Judge arrives and delivers its verdict: this is why they leave. This is why you can't keep anyone close. You are the problem.

And the next time the partner withdraws, the pursuer is not just fighting the current withdrawal. They are fighting the accumulated weight of every previous withdrawal, every failed repair, and the growing internal conviction that their intensity is to blame for all of it.

This is the cycle beneath the cycle. And it is what makes ARS a dual injury: interpersonal and intrapsychic simultaneously.

It is also, it is worth saying plainly, one of the loneliest experiences a person can have inside a relationship. To be fighting a battle on two fronts simultaneously, the one in the room and the one inside your own head, with no one who fully understands what either of them costs you.

What the Therapeutic Work Requires

Working with ARS from an attachment-informed perspective means holding a very specific clinical posture.

It means refusing to pathologize the protest. The pursuer's anger, their criticism, their desperate returning to the same wound, these are attachment protests. They are not anger management deficits. They are a person screaming into the void: I am still here. Does anyone hear me? The therapist's job is to hear what is underneath the scream.

It means moving beneath the reactive surface on both sides. With the pursuer, this means gently, persistently asking: what is the fear beneath the anger? What is the longing beneath the demand? With the withdrawer, it means doing the equally important work of helping them understand that their silence, however unintentional, is landing as abandonment. Not because they are abandoning. But because the pursuing partner's nervous system cannot tell the difference.

It means tracking the internal experience of the pursuing partner with extraordinary care. When the withdrawer goes quiet in session, even briefly, the therapist needs to stay close to the pursuer. Not to manage the pursuer's reaction, but to help them notice what just happened inside their body, and to begin building the capacity to tolerate that moment without the alarm consuming them entirely.

In practice, this might sound like pausing mid-session and turning toward the pursuing partner after a moment of withdrawal in the room. Something like:

"I noticed he went quiet just then, and something shifted in you. Can we slow that down together? What happened in your body in that moment?"

Or, when the pursuer's anger has been running the session and you sense the vulnerability beneath it:

"There's so much fire here, and I don't want to rush past it. But I'm wondering... underneath all of this, underneath the frustration, is there something that feels more like... fear? Or maybe grief? Something quieter that doesn't get as much airtime?"

And when naming the asymmetry directly to both partners, without allocating blame:

"What I'm seeing is that you," turning to the pursuer, "have been the keeper of this relationship's emotional life. The one who notices when something is wrong, who brings it up, who tries to repair it. And that is an enormous amount to carry alone. It makes complete sense that your system is exhausted and on high alert. And you," turning to the withdrawer, "have been doing something that probably made sense at some point, going quiet to avoid making things worse. Neither of you chose this dance. But I want us to start looking at what it has cost both of you."

That last move is crucial in ARS work. Naming the structural imbalance clearly, without making the withdrawer the identified problem, while also not asking the pursuer to minimize what they have been carrying. It is a both/and frame, held with care.

It means being transparent with both partners about the cycle itself. In EFT, we name the dance. We say: this is what happens between you. Neither of you chose it. Both of you are caught in it. And the cycle is the enemy, not each other.

In ARS, naming the structural asymmetry is also part of the clinical work. Helping the pursuing partner understand that their hypervigilance is not a character flaw but a logical response to an environment that has repeatedly confirmed the bond is unsafe. And helping the withdrawing partner understand that their absence, however quietly offered, has real consequences for the person left in the silence.

Why This Matters for the Therapist in the Room

If you are a clinician reading this, I want to name something directly.

ARS couples are some of the most challenging to sit with, not because they are difficult clients, but because the dynamic pulls for a particular kind of therapist response. The pursuer's intensity can feel like pressure. The withdrawer's quiet can feel like relief. And without careful self-awareness, a therapist can find themselves subtly aligning with the withdrawer's stillness, gently asking the pursuer to "tone it down," to "try a softer start-up," to "give their partner some space."

In a standard pursuer-withdrawer dynamic, this is often appropriate clinical work.

In ARS, it can re-enact the very injury that brought the couple to the room.

The therapist's job is to be a secure base for both people. To hold the pursuer's pain without being destabilized by it. To draw out the withdrawer's experience without allowing their silence to dominate the room. And to name, clearly and without blame, what the asymmetry has cost.

That is the work. And I want to say to any clinician reading this who has sat with a couple like this and felt the pull of the room, who has caught themselves gently redirecting the pursuer's intensity one too many times, who has left a session wondering if they got it quite right: you are not alone in that. These couples are hard precisely because the dynamic is so well-designed to pull for a particular response. Noticing the pull is already the work. And if something in this piece is making you want to slow down and look again at a case that has been sitting quietly in the back of your mind, trust that instinct. That instinct is good clinical radar.

Part 3 is written directly for the person living inside this dynamic, the one who is exhausted, who has stopped believing things will change, who needs someone to simply say: I see you, and this makes sense. Part 4 looks at what healing makes possible, what changes when both partners are willing, and what remains available even when only one is ready. Both pieces are here when you are.

And if something in this piece has made you want to slow down and talk to someone, whether you arrived here as a clinician or as someone who recognized their own relationship in every paragraph, I am here. That is not a footnote. It is an open door.
